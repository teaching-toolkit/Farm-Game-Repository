# The Farm That Burned Down

## Before the first page

Evening. The room smells of tea.

Minena has brought it in on a tray, in the small tin pot with the bent handle, and spilled about half of it on the way. Nobody minds. The tea that is left is very hot and very strong, which is how the old woman likes it.

The others are everywhere. On the floor. On the end of the bed. One of them is lying across the old woman's feet like a draught-stopper. Her name is Fenna. She is very old now, and these are her grandchildren, all of them, and there are a lot of them.

On the blanket lies a thin brown book with a cracked spine. By the bed stands a dented bucket. It is full of Minena's things: stones, feathers, a snail shell, a button nobody has claimed.

"Tell us about the farm that burned down," says Minena.

"Again?"

"YES."

"Moonedge," says the old woman. "That one."

"And don't forget the bells," whispers someone very small, from under the bed.

"Shh! The bells come *later*!"

"In order!"

"I wasn't going to *do* the bells. I was only *reminding*."

The old woman closes her eyes. For a moment she looks as if she has fallen asleep, and three children lean in to check.

"I'm not sure I remember it right," she says. "It was a long time ago."

"You always remember it right."

"Then I always remember it the same. That's not the same thing." She opens one eye. "I never start with the fire. I start with the ash. And a tent that smelled of horse."

Then she starts to hum. A few slow notes, going up and turning round, as if they are looking for somewhere to sit.

"You're humming," says Minena.

"Am I?" says the old woman, and goes on doing it.

---

## Chapter One: Ashes

The tent smells of horse.

That is the first thing. It is her father's tent, borrowed, and it has spent years folded up in a stable, and it has opinions about that. Fenna lies inside it on the first morning and breathes through her mouth, and then she gets up, because lying down has never been one of her talents.

Outside, everything is grey.

Grey ash. Grey mud. Black posts sticking up where a house once stood, like the last teeth in a very old mouth. A chimney with no house round it. A gate with no fence on either side, which is still, somehow, shut.

This is Moonedge. This was her family's farm. She was eight when she left it. She is fifteen now, and she has walked for four days to stand in the middle of it, and it is exactly as burnt as everybody said.

She walks the whole of it before breakfast. She does not walk through the black rectangle where the barn was. She walks round that.

And then, at the far edge of the field, she stops.

There is a patch of green.

It is about the size of a tablecloth. Everything around it is ash, and it is green. Not a tired green, either. A proper green, with clover in it, as if somebody has been keeping it.

In the middle of the green patch stands one stalk of wheat. It is ripe and gold and leaning a little, the way wheat leans when it is heavy. But it is not heavy. Every grain has been nibbled off it, neatly, one by one.

All but one.

Fenna kneels and picks the last grain off the stalk. It sits in the middle of her palm, small and hard and golden.

Somebody ate all the others.

And somebody left this one.

She looks round. Ash. Wind. A crow, being unhelpful. Nobody.

She closes her hand.

---

In the tent there is a box.

Her grandmother left it to her. Grandmother Berit died this winter, in the little cottage on the hill where she had raised Fenna for seven years, and the box was waiting on the kitchen table afterwards with Fenna's name on the lid in thin brown ink, as if Berit had known exactly how things would go. Which she usually had.

In the box there are four things.

A book, thin and brown, with a cracked spine.

A small tin pot with a bent handle.

Berit's old hat, which is shapeless and green and smells of her.

And a bucket. A dented old bucket that Fenna knows at once, the way you know your own shoe. It is smaller than she remembers. That is the only thing about it that has changed.

On top of the book lies a note.

*You are fifteen. That is old enough to go. If you are reading this, you went. Good.*

*Do not apologise to the bucket.*

*—B.*

Fenna reads it three times. Then she puts on the hat. It comes down over her eyebrows. She leaves it there.

The book is a farm book. Every page is about something on a farm: fire, water, weeds, wheat, bread, hens. Some of it is printed. Most of it is in Berit's handwriting, crammed into the margins, going round corners, climbing up the sides. On the very first page, all by itself, it says:

*Start small. Small is plenty.*

Fenna looks at the grain in her hand. It is very small.

And as she looks at it, a song comes back to her. Berit's kitchen song, the one she sang while she stirred things. There were words to it once, a rhyme about the animals on the farm. Fenna can only get the first line.

"Long ears sow the clover green," she says, out loud, to nobody.

Nobody says the next line.

She plants the grain anyway. At the edge of the green patch, where the ground is softest. She pats the soil down over it with two fingers, and then, because nobody is watching, she pats it once more.

---

The last of her bread is wrapped in a corner of cloth cut from a sack. Somebody has burnt a wheel onto the cloth and the letters WEIR. Weir is the river town where her family lives now. Her mother, her father, her little sister, her little brother. Her cousin.

Folded inside the bread is a note in her mother's quick, round writing.

*The house is small. The table is long. Come when you're ready. Everyone sends love.*

*(Everyone means everyone. —M.)*

Under that there is a drawing by her little sister. It is a picture of the farm as her sister imagines it. There is a house, a tree, a sun with a face, and a chicken. The chicken is bigger than the house.

Fenna eats the bread standing up, in four bites. She pins the drawing inside the tent flap, where she will see it every morning, and does not look at it again for the rest of the day.

She told them she would stay a while. She did not say how long a while was. That was on purpose.

---

That night she sees the stars.

She had forgotten. In Weir there are lamps, and in Berit's valley there were hills in the way, and at Moonedge there is nothing between her and the sky at all. The sky starts at her boots and goes up and up and over and down the other side. There are so many stars that the dark between them looks crowded. There are stars behind the stars. There is a pale smear across the whole middle of it, like somebody has spilled flour on a black table and not wiped it up, and the smear is made of stars too, more than anyone could count in a life, and every one of them has been burning there the whole time, all seven years, every night, whether anybody looked up or not.

Nobody told her there would be so many.

Nobody was here to tell her.

Then she notices the stones.

At the edge of the forest, where the trees begin, there is a line of white stones. By day she had not looked at them. Now they are glowing. Not much. The way your hand glows when you hold it over a candle.

And there is a tune.

It is very faint. It is coming from the stones, or from the trees behind them, or from somewhere further in than that. A few slow notes, going up, and turning round. It has been following her all week, this tune. She heard it on the road. She heard it in her sleep. She thought she was making it up.

She finds that she is humming it.

The stones get a little brighter.

She stops humming, to see. They stay bright, exactly as long as she stands still. When she shifts her feet, they dim.

Fenna stands at the edge of her burnt farm, under all those stars, and does not move for quite a long time.

She does not go into the forest.

Not tonight.

---

In the morning she makes a fire.

There is a page for that in the book. In the margin, in thin brown ink:

*A fire is a guest. Never leave it alone in the house, and never blame the house. —B.*

Fenna does not like fire very much. She would not say so. She just makes this one very carefully, with the stones close round it, and she fills the bucket from the stream and sets it right beside the fire before she even strikes the flint.

The first spark sulks.

The second spark dies of embarrassment.

The third spark catches, and Fenna leans over it and blows, very gently, until her eyebrows complain.

Then there is a fire. A small one. In this place. The ash around it glows a little at the edges, as if it remembers something.

Fenna sits as far back from it as the warmth allows, and makes porridge in the tin pot.

Somewhere off in the dark of the field, something goes:

THUMP.

THUMP.

THUMP.

Then a little roll of thumps, very fast, like a drum being told off.

Fenna banks the fire down. The thumping stops.

She looks into the dark for a while. Then she eats her porridge. Standing up.

---

The weeds came back first.

Of course they did. They came back to a burnt field before anything else would, which tells you something about weeds. There are thistles, and docks, and a sort of long hairy thing that does not have a name and does not seem to want one.

Fenna pulls one. Its root is longer than her arm, and very proud of it.

She pulls another. She pulls forty more. She pulls until her hands are green and her back has an opinion and the pile beside her is taller than she is.

On the weed page, in the margin, small and slanted:

*Weeds are only plants nobody invited. Some of my best friends were weeds. —B.*

"Thank you, Berit," says Fenna, to the book. "Very helpful."

She pulls the last weed. And in the quiet after it comes out, she hears something.

A bell.

A tiny bell, high in the air. Not a cow bell or a door bell or any bell she knows. More like a bell that a beetle would ring, if beetles went to church.

*Ting.*

Fenna does not look up. She is not sure why. She just keeps her eyes on the weeds, very firmly.

Then nothing.

When she turns round, there are three sticks lying behind her in a pile. A very silly pile. One is standing on its end. One is balanced across the top of it. One is leaning on the other two as if it is tired of the whole business.

Nothing is missing. Nothing is broken. It is just three sticks, being silly.

Fenna looks at them for a long moment.

Then she uses them as kindling.

---

On the water page:

*An empty bucket is a hat. A full one is a promise. Fill it at dusk, not at dawn. —B.*

Fenna looks at the bucket. It is empty. She fills it at once, though it is the middle of the morning, and sets it down by the tent where she can see it.

The old bucket clanks against the stones as she puts it down. It sounds almost pleased with itself.

---

The first visitor is a goat.

The goat comes up the track at a trot, sees Fenna's weed pile, and makes for it with the face of somebody who has been told about a party.

Behind the goat comes an old woman with a stick, a red face and a lot to say about goats.

"You come BACK here, you bearded—" The old woman stops. She has seen the hat.

She looks at the hat for a long time before she looks at the face under it.

"I'd know that hat on a scarecrow," she says.

"It was my grandmother's," says Fenna.

"I know whose it was." The old woman leans on her stick. "Maud. Granny Maud, they call me down there, as if I'd be anybody's granny." She jerks her head at the goat, which is now eating the weed pile from the bottom, so that the top sinks. "That's not mine either. Well. It is. It doesn't think so."

Fenna has a great many questions. What she says is, "Would you like some porridge?"

"I would not," says Maud. "I have a goat to be cross with." But she does not move for a while. She just stands there, looking at the hat, and the tent, and the bucket.

"She said you'd come," says Maud at last. Then she takes the goat by the beard, which it does not like, and goes.

---

The second visitor is a seller.

She comes along the track with a cart full of sacks and a coat with more pockets than any coat should have. She stops at the gate. She sniffs the air.

"Is that porridge?"

Fenna hands her a bowl over the gate. The seller eats it standing up, very fast, in about four spoonfuls, the way Fenna does. Fenna watches her do it and feels a little strange, as if somebody is doing an impression of her, badly.

"Good porridge," says the seller. "You're from up here, aren't you? The Moonedge lot." She hands the bowl back and looks Fenna up and down. "You're hardly more than a child."

"I'm fifteen."

"That's what I said." The seller shoulders her cart. "I'll tell all of Hazelwood somebody's cooking up here. They'll want to know. They'll pretend they don't." She is already walking. "I'll come by again when the road's dry," she calls. "If it's ever dry."

Hazelwood is the village below the hill. Fenna can see its roofs from the gate, and the smoke from its chimneys, and the square tower of its Hall. She has not been down there yet. She has a great deal to do first.

She has a great deal to do for quite a while.

---

The third visitor is a boy with sheep.

He stands at the fence with nine sheep crowded round his legs and shivers. It is a cold day. Fenna's fire is going. He looks at it the way a dog looks at a sausage.

"You can come in," says Fenna. "It's warm."

"No, thank you," says the boy.

"You're shivering."

"I know." He does not move. "The sheep don't like it in there," he says. "And neither do I."

He does not say why. He does not have to. It is a burnt farm, and he is a boy, and there are stories.

So Fenna takes a burning log out of the fire with the tongs and hands it over the fence. The boy holds it out in front of him and warms his hands at it. The sheep crowd round. The log smokes. Everybody coughs.

"Tom," says the boy, after a while.

"Fenna."

"I know," says Tom. Then he goes red and walks off with his sheep and the log, and Fenna does not get the log back.

---

What is left of the barn is a black rectangle in the ash, a little sunk, like a bed that nobody has made. At one end stands a doorframe, all by itself, with no door and no wall. On its post hangs an iron hook.

The hook is empty.

Fenna walks round the rectangle. She does not walk across it. She does not look at the hook for longer than it takes to see it.

Then she finds she has a great deal to do about the woodpile. The woodpile is a mess. She re-stacks it. Then she looks at the stack and decides it could be better, and re-stacks it again, faster.

When she has finished, the woodpile is the tidiest woodpile in the whole valley. It is so tidy it looks nervous.

---

By the burnt post of the old gate stands a row of buckets.

Fenna had thought they were rubbish. But each one has a small tin leaf nailed under its rim, very neatly, like a name tag. The oldest buckets are rusted brown. The newest are still bright.

She needs buckets. She has a whole field to water and one bucket to do it with. So she takes them, and uses them, and puts them back by the post every evening, because that is where they live.

On the flat top of the gatepost there is something else. A short row of stones. Small, flat, grey river stones, smooth as soap. Not the white stones from the forest edge. Not from up here at all. The oldest ones have a fur of moss. The newest is clean.

Fenna picks one up. It is cool and fits her palm exactly.

She puts it back exactly where it was.

---

Under the black boards of the old workshop she finds a page.

It is burnt at the edges and brown in the middle, and at the top, in big careful letters, it says MOONEDGE. Down one side runs a row of little drawings of sacks. At the bottom, written in red:

*The Lord's share.*

Fenna knows what that is, the way you know words you heard grown-ups say in the kitchen. Moonedge was never theirs, not really. It was the Lord's land. The family farmed it, and every harvest, part of what they grew went up the hill to the Lord. That was his share.

On the page, the share goes like this.

One sack in ten.

Then one in five.

Then one in three.

Then one in two. That last one has a circle round it. Two circles, in fact, pressed hard, so the pen went through.

One edge of the page is burnt away. There was another column there once. She cannot read it.

Every time, the Lord's share is bigger.

Fenna folds the page small. Then smaller. She puts it in the pocket of her coat, and she does not think about it, very hard, for the rest of the day.

---

The workbench is three logs, one plank and a great deal of confidence. Fenna sets a stone on it to test it. The stone stays.

This, she decides, is a very good bench.

---

In the end she has to go down to Hazelwood. She needs nails and salt and string, and nails and salt and string do not grow, however nicely you ask.

The road down is mostly mud, with a few rumours of gravel. In the first rut lies one boot. Not hers. It looks as if it gave up halfway and sat down.

The village is smaller than she remembers. Or she is bigger. A dog barks at her from the first house. A little girl stares at her until her mother pulls her away by the sleeve.

The market is small and loud and smells of onions. Fenna buys her nails and her salt and her string and says nothing else at all. The woman at the next stall looks at Fenna's hat, and then, carefully, at the ground.

Nobody says the word *Moonedge*.

Everybody is thinking it. You can tell. It is like walking through a room where everyone has just stopped talking.

The woman who sells the salt gives her change. One coin is a small dented one, with a face on it that looks as if it has had a very long day. Round the edge it says WEXLEY.

"That's the Lord," says the woman. "He likes to be on things."

Fenna puts it in her pocket. She takes it out to check it. She puts it back.

In the middle of the square there is a notice board. It is covered in small papers. Somebody needs a basket mending. Somebody has a pig for sale, "a good pig, a talker". Somebody has lost a glove, a green one, left hand. Fenna reads every single notice twice, with her hat pulled low, because reading is a thing you can do without anybody talking to you.

There is a girl at the mill gate, with flour on her nose, who watches Fenna read. When Fenna turns, the girl comes over very fast, looking back over her shoulder at the mill the whole way.

"Can you make baskets?" she whispers. "Ella. From the mill. I need one. My father mustn't know it's from—" She stops. "From up there."

"Why not?"

Ella just looks at the mill. It is not turning. The water under it is low and brown and sulky.

Fenna makes the basket that evening, out of willow from the stream. She brings it down the next market day. Ella takes it, and gives her a brass bell in return, bright as a button, and whispers, "For luck," with one eye on the mill.

Fenna shakes the bell. It goes *BONG*.

She listens, for a moment, for the other bell. The tiny one, high up.

This is not that bell.

---

On the way home she meets Granny Maud again, still chasing the goat.

Maud catches Fenna's face in both her hands. Her hands are rough and smell of goat.

"Oh, Fenna," she says. "Berit all over. Berit's stubborn self." Her eyes have gone wet. "That goat," she says. "Kicks up dust."

There is no dust. It has been raining all week.

"She raised you on that hill," says Maud. "And she wrote and told me she was sending you back. I wrote and said, Berit, you cannot *send* a person. And she wrote back and said: *Watch me.*"

Maud lets go of Fenna's face. She wipes her eyes with the heel of her hand and glares at the goat, which is eating a hedge.

"Don't tell her I said," says Maud. Then she remembers, and her mouth goes funny. "Well. You can't now, can you."

---

Summer comes.

The ash goes green at the edges, a thin soft green, like the fur on something asleep. Fenna walks over it slowly, in case it is a mistake and the ground notices.

The air over the field wobbles in the heat like jelly. Somewhere a cricket is making a complaint.

She makes herself an axe. She chips a flint into shape against another flint, *chip, chip, chip*, until the edge is sharp enough to split a hair. It fits her hand as if it had been waiting for one.

And the wheat comes up. The one grain she planted made a stalk, and the stalk made an ear, and when she cuts it and rubs it between her palms and blows the chaff away, there in her hand is a little heap of gold.

She counts them.

One grain went into the ground.

Look at them now.

She closes her fingers round them, carefully, the way you would hold a baby bird.

At the bottom of the ash page, in the same brown ink:

*Ash feeds the soil. The soil does not hold a grudge. Take notes. —B.*

---

Then there are the rocks.

Fenna lifts one out of the field. Under it is a smaller rock, looking surprised. Under that is another. Whatever lives under Moonedge has been collecting rocks for a very long time, and it has a lot of them.

She digs them up all day. She carries them to the edge of the field and drops them in a heap. A big, messy, ordinary heap.

In the morning the heap is a tower.

Every rock is balanced on the rock under it. The biggest is at the bottom, and they get smaller and smaller going up, and the very smallest is right at the top, sitting there like a hat.

Nothing is missing. Nothing has been taken. Somebody has just built a tower out of her rocks in the night, very carefully, and gone away again.

Fenna looks round. Nobody. Only a very small sound, high and bright, like a bell, as if somebody is waiting to see what she will think.

What she thinks is that she needs rocks for a hearth.

So she takes the tower down. Rock by rock. Starting at the top, with the little one like a hat. She carries them over to the fire and builds them into a hearth, a proper one, flat and solid, a good hearth for a guest.

High up, somewhere, the small bright sound goes out. Like a candle.

The quiet after it is a different quiet from before.

It stays.

---

Something rustles in the long grass at the edge of the field. It stops when she looks.

She does not look again. She has noticed that looking is not working.

---

The quern grinds with a noise like a cart on a bad road. What comes out is flour.

Fenna tastes a pinch off her thumb. It tastes of work and, a little, of somewhere she has been before.

The seller comes back with her cart, when the road is dry, and tells Fenna her name this time. Rue. Rue wants a sack of flour for her stall, and pays in coins and in news.

"The miller tasted a pinch," says Rue. "He said *adequate*. Like a man swallowing a bee." She grins. "From him that's a love letter." She checks three of her pockets for something, finds it in the fourth, and hands Fenna a twist of salt. "Oh. And a carter in Weir asked if I'd seen you. Big man. Sad eyes. Didn't give a name. *Look for the bucket,* he said." Rue shrugs. "I said you were busy. Was that right?"

Fenna has gone very still.

"Yes," she says. "That was right."

Her father drives a carter's wagon in Weir. Her father has sad eyes and is a big man and does not give his name if he can help it.

She makes three more sacks of flour that week, very fast, and does not think about it.

---

At the end of summer, Granny Maud comes up the hill with a goat on a rope and a bucket in her other hand. The bucket has a small tin leaf nailed under its rim.

She fills it at the stream. She carries it to the burnt gatepost and sets it on top, full, beside the row of grey stones.

"It stays there till morning," says Maud. "Every year, end of summer. Don't touch it."

Fenna wants that water. The field is dry. The new wheat is thirsty. A whole bucket, sitting there doing nothing, is the hardest thing she has seen all year.

She leaves it alone. It takes every bit of her.

In the morning the bucket is empty.

Nobody says who drank it. Nobody seems to think anybody should say. Maud takes the bucket down and turns it over and taps the bottom, and it rings.

"We came with buckets, that night," says Maud. She is not looking at Fenna. She is looking at the black rectangle where the barn was. "The whole village. All the way up the hill, hand to hand." She puts the bucket back with the others, under the post. "Too late."

That is all she says about it. Fenna does not ask any more. She is not ready to hear the answer, and Maud seems to know it.

---

Autumn.

Smoke from the village chimneys drifts up over the hill, and for a moment the wind smells of other people's suppers. Fenna stops what she is doing. Then she starts again, a little faster.

She makes a scarecrow out of two sticks and a sack. By noon the crows are holding a meeting on its shoulder. Minutes are taken. Nothing is decided.

One crow comes and sits on her fence and looks at her work for a long time. It makes a small dry sound. Fenna decides it was a review.

---

Digging a drain at the top of the field, her spade rings on stone.

Not one stone. A curve of them, fitted together. A ring.

It is the old well. It has been dry since before the fire. Fenna clears the earth away from the rim with her hands, and her fingers find a notch in one of the stones, worn smooth. Her hand goes to it before she knows why. It is just the right height for a small girl standing on a crate, reaching in.

She was that small girl. She stood on that crate.

She digs. The hole goes down and down, and then the bottom of it goes dark, and then it goes wet.

Water.

She lowers the bucket on a rope, and pulls it up, and drinks out of her two hands. It tastes of stone, and cold, and very faintly of morning.

---

Two children come up the track. They are very hungry and very brave, in that order. They dare each other to touch the burnt gatepost, and run away screaming when they do it, and come back.

"Lotte," says the girl.

"Linus," says the boy. "We're twins."

"I'm older."

"By *eleven minutes*."

"Eleven minutes is a lot of minutes."

"There's a ghost up here," says Linus to Fenna, seriously. "Everyone says."

"It's a cat," says Lotte.

"It's a GHOST."

"Everyone knows," says Lotte, "that the farm used to ring by itself. When there was nobody in it. *Ring ring ring.* At night." She looks round. "That's a cat."

"Cats don't *ring*."

"*Some* cats ring."

Fenna gives them both a bowl of porridge. They eat it sitting on the ground with their backs against the gatepost, arguing about whether cats ring, and then they go home with porridge on their chins and their dare done.

---

One day there are no thistles left. No rocks. No stumps.

Where the weeds stood there is only soil, brown and soft and a great deal bigger than she expected. It goes all the way to the forest. It goes all the way to the gate.

It is a field.

"Field," says Fenna, out loud, to see how it fits.

It fits.

---

That autumn the village wants soup.

There is a notice about it on the board. Market day, soup for anyone. Bring a pot if you have one.

Fenna has a pot. Fenna has vegetables, now, real ones, from her real field. She will not borrow a cart, because borrowing is asking, so she carries two full pots of soup all the way down the hill, one in each hand.

The hill wins. But only just.

On the square stands a big black iron pot on three legs over a fire. It is the village Pot, with a capital P, and it is kept by a round woman with a red face called Maribel Kettle, who has been told every joke there is about her name and laughed at none of them.

Fenna tries to hand over her soup and go.

"Sit," says Maribel.

"I've got to get back—"

"Sit. Eat. Taste before you talk. That's the rule at this pot." Maribel looks at her harder. "Whose are you, then?"

Fenna tells her.

At her family's name the whole Pot goes quiet. Just for one breath. The spoons stop. Somebody's baby stops.

Then Maribel passes her the salt.

Fenna sits on an upturned bucket for one whole minute. It is the longest she has sat down since she came.

A tall woman on the far side of the Pot leans across and drops something into Fenna's lap. It is a smooth grey river stone.

"The Soup Stone," she says. "Goes round. Whoever's got it brings the next pot." She sniffs. "So nobody's pot is the only pot."

Fenna holds the stone. It is warm from the woman's pocket.

The soup goes round in bowls. People take it without a word, and come back for more without a word. An old woman tastes it and says, "Needs salt." Then she takes a second bowl.

Behind her a small child whispers, loud enough for half the square: "Is it the burnt farm soup?"

The old woman does not turn round.

"It's soup," she says.

The child has a second bowl too.

---

Winter.

The snow comes in the night, without a sound. In the morning the green patch is the last green thing for miles.

Round it, in the snow, there are tracks. Long prints and short prints, side by side, going round and round the patch. Three times round. Then they go away, across the white field, and stop at the edge of the forest.

It is cold enough now for the water bucket to grow a lid. Fenna chips the ice off and drinks the chips. Breakfast is mostly an excuse to stand near the fire. It is a very good excuse.

The tent flaps all night in the wind. She pulls the blanket up over her nose and lies there, wide awake, listening to her own tent breathe in and out.

In the morning there is a hole in the tent, low down, where the wind has worried at a seam. Fenna patches it. She uses the only cloth she has: the corner of sack her bread came in, with the burnt wheel on it and the letters WEIR. She sews it on with very small stitches, letters facing in, so that every night she goes to sleep looking at the name of the town she is not in.

---

On Longnight, the longest night of the year, something happens in the village.

From the hill Fenna can see it. One by one, all the lights of Hazelwood go out. Window by window. Street by street. Until the only lights left are the windows of the Hall, in the middle, glowing yellow. And from the Hall a sound comes up the valley. People. Lots of them. A hum, like bees in a box. Like a whole village in one room, talking.

Nobody has asked her.

Of course nobody has asked her. She lives in a tent on a burnt farm, and they have known her for a season, and it is their night, not hers.

Fenna gets back to work. She is mending the fence by lantern light, which is a silly thing to do on the longest night of the year, and she does it very loudly. Hammer. Hammer. Hammer. A bowl of porridge is balanced on the fence post beside her, and she eats it between nails, standing up, with snow falling in it.

"Excuse me," says somebody. "Is this seat taken?"

Fenna nearly hits her thumb.

There is a man sitting on her fence.

He was not there a moment ago. She is quite sure of that. There was no sound of him coming, no crunch in the snow, nothing. He is just there, already settled, as if he has been sitting on her fence for an hour and is starting to get comfortable.

He is tall and thin, with a long face that could be thirty or could be much older. He is wearing a rust-brown coat with a great many buttons, and none of the buttons match. One is brass. One is bone. One looks like a slice of carrot.

"It's a fence," says Fenna.

"So it is," says the man. He pats it. "Excellent fence. Very fence-ish." He has a bowl in his hands. She has no idea where it came from. There is porridge in it. "You don't mind if I join you for supper? Only it's Longnight, and everybody else is busy."

"I'm busy."

"I can see that." He takes a spoonful of porridge. Then he stands up on the fence.

Fenna stares.

The man walks along the top rail of the fence, one foot in front of the other, eating porridge. He gets to the post. He turns round, carefully, on one leg. He walks back.

"What are you doing?"

"Having supper," says the man. He hops off the fence and walks in a small circle round her, still eating. "This is how it's done, isn't it? I've been watching. You never sit. Very modern. Chairs are finished, I think. Benches. Stools. Done for." He walks backwards round the fire. "Think about it. You sit down, and then what? You're just *sitting* there. Anyone could come up and *talk* to you."

He is now eating porridge while hopping on one foot.

"You'll spill it," says Fenna.

"I won't."

He spills it. A big grey lump lands in the snow. He looks at it with deep respect.

"I meant to do that," he says. "It's a style."

"Oh, for goodness' sake," says Fenna. "*Sit down* and eat it properly."

"Like this?" The man sits down at once, on the fence, his bowl in his lap, very neat, like a boy at school.

"Yes."

"Lovely," he says. He eats a spoonful of porridge, slowly, sitting down. He looks very peaceful.

Fenna is still standing up.

There is a short silence. The snow falls on her porridge.

She looks at her own bowl. She looks at him. He is not looking at her at all. He is looking at the village, at the one lit Hall.

Fenna does not sit down. She would like to make that very clear. She does, however, lean on the fence. Just a little.

"Who are you?" she says.

"Lark," says the man.

"Is that your real name?"

"It's the one I came in," says Lark. He scrapes his bowl. "You had a blue mitten up here, once. Only the one. You wore it on whichever hand was coldest. Very loyal to that mitten, you were."

Fenna's hands go still.

She did have a blue mitten. She had forgotten it entirely until this moment. It had a hole in the thumb, and she used to swap it from hand to hand, and she lost it somewhere in the snow, the last winter before—

She turns to ask him how he knows.

The fence is empty.

There is a bowl on the post, scraped clean. There are no footprints going away in the snow. There are none coming, either.

Down in the valley, the lights of Hazelwood come back on, one by one, all over the village, like somebody waking up.

Fenna finishes her porridge.

She eats the last spoonful sitting on the fence. Only because her legs are tired. That is all.

---

She builds a coop that winter, for hens she does not have. When it is finished she leaves the little door open, in case somebody is looking for a coop.

One night a lantern comes along the road below. It moves slowly. It stops at the bottom of the track, as if it is wondering whether her smoke is a fire.

Fenna freezes. She does not move a muscle until it moves on.

A tired voice floats up: "Dry night." Then the lantern goes on round the hill.

Afterwards her hands shake. She sits on her hands until they stop. She does not know why they shook. It was only a lantern.

---

Spring again.

The ash has a green skin all over now, and the ground is soft under her boots. The green patch is not the only green thing any more. She treads softly, in case it minds.

The hens arrive from the market in a basket. They come out one at a time, inspect the coop, find three faults with it, and take over the yard. By evening, Fenna is the one being supervised.

Across the top of the hen page, written in a hurry:

*Do not name the hens. You will name the hens. —B.*

"I won't," says Fenna.

By Thursday they are called Agatha, Big Agatha and The Other One.

She hums while she works. She has noticed that she does it now, all the time, the tune from the forest edge. And she has noticed something else. At one place in the tune, the place where it turns round, every hen in the yard stops scratching. They go quiet, all at once, and look at each other.

Fenna hums straight on past it, and they start scratching again.

The first egg is still warm. The hen watches her take it with the face of someone who has views about pricing.

---

Rue comes up the hill on market day, but not to sell. She wants Fenna to see something.

It is a notch, cut in the gatepost. A new one, fresh and pale in the black wood.

"A man from the Hurst does that," says Rue. "When he starts a book on a place." The Hurst is the Lord's house, the big one on the hill above Hazelwood, behind the high wall. "Somebody up there has opened a page on you."

"What did you tell them?"

"Nobody asked me. If they had, I'd have said it was a kid with a bucket." Rue looks at the notch. "Kids with buckets aren't worth a page."

Fenna runs her thumb along the notch. She cannot un-cut it.

---

On the way down to market, just before the first houses, somebody has put a stool beside the road. A little three-legged stool. It is exactly where Fenna always stops to rest, if she were the sort of person who stopped to rest, which she is not.

She does not sit on it.

She puts her hand on it, as she passes. That is all.

---

In the village, children are skipping in the lane. They have a song for it. One line of the song has gone missing over the years, and nobody can remember what it was, so when they get to that line they just sing *la-la-la-la-la* instead, very loudly, and then carry on.

Fenna listens all the way past. She does not know why it bothers her so much, that missing line.

---

She builds a winch for the well. It squeals like a pig being asked a hard question. But the bucket comes up full every time, and she does not have to climb down for it any more. She winds it up and down twice, for the pleasure of the noise.

Her hands, she notices, have started to look like someone else's hands. Somebody who works.

---

She builds a hutch.

She does not really know why. It is a small round house of straw and sticks, soft inside, big enough for something small. She sets it down beside the green patch with the door facing the forest. She feels a bit foolish doing it.

The next morning somebody has nibbled the tops off her carrots. Neatly. All in a row. The carrots themselves are left in the ground, quite whole, only the green tops gone, and not one row more than she can spare.

A thoughtful thief.

So that evening Fenna fills a bowl with greens and sliced carrot and sets it at the edge of the green patch. Then she walks away, so as not to look like somebody who is waiting.

She looks back four times.

The fifth time, there is a bunny on the green patch.

She is small and brown, with one ear that will not stand up properly. Her nose is never still. She is sitting right in the middle of the clover as if she has always sat there, as if the whole patch was built round her, and she is eating Fenna's carrot with great concentration and no gratitude whatsoever.

Fenna does not move. She does not even breathe very much.

The bunny finishes the carrot. She looks at Fenna. She looks at the empty bowl. She looks at Fenna again.

THUMP.

It is not a frightened thump. It is a thump that means: *more.*

---

And Fenna understands.

The green patch did not stay green by luck. Somebody looked after it. Every day, for seven years, all alone, somebody kept one small piece of Moonedge alive. And the wheat stalk was nibbled bare because somebody was hungry, very hungry, and could not help it.

But one grain was left.

Left for her.

The bunny was Moonedge's, once. Fenna remembers it now, not clearly, the way you remember a dream. A brown back in a basket. A nose against her hand. A thump in the night when something was wrong.

She has come home.

"Long ears sow the clover green," says Fenna.

The bunny's crooked ear twitches.

There is more of the rhyme. Fenna can feel it there, just behind the first line, like a word on the tip of your tongue. She says the line again, and waits for the next one.

Nothing comes.

That night, from far away, she hears two soft rings of a very small bell.

*Ting. Ting.*

Fenna sits up in the dark. Outside, the bunny is asleep in the clover, her ear flopped over one eye.

So the bunny was here all along.

Then who else is?

---

Back in the room, the tea has gone cold.

"Was there really a bunny?" asks Minena.

"There was," says the old woman. "She was very rude about carrots."

"And the man on the fence? Was he real?"

"As real as the fence."

"And the bell?" Minena sits up. "Who rang the bell?"

"Shh!" say five grandchildren at once. "*That's next time!*"

The old woman smiles, and closes her eyes, and hums. Minena puts a hand on her arm. They know this story almost as well as she does.

Almost.

## Chapter Two: Homestead

Every second week the river barge ties up at the Landing below Hazelwood, and that day is Landing Day.

On Landing Day the whole village goes down to the river. The barge brings salt and nails and news and the post, and its captain, a small brown woman called Mei with a voice like a ship's horn, stands on the deck and calls out the names of every house that has a letter.

"Appleby!" "Here!"

"Hewitt!" "Here!"

And then, every single Landing Day, for as long as anybody can remember:

"Moonedge!"

And every single Landing Day, an old man called Jan has shouted "HERE!" for Moonedge. Alone. For seven years. There has never once been a letter for Moonedge. He shouts it anyway, so the name will not get lonely.

Today there is a letter.

And today Mei does not hand it over at the river. Mei walks it up the hill herself, all the way up the muddy track, which captains do not do. Behind her, puffing, red in the face, comes Old Jan.

"Wanted to be there," he wheezes, at the gate. "The first time it counted."

Fenna takes the letter. The writing is round and quick: her mother's.

*Come home when you are ready, my love. The house is small, but the table is long. Your cousin asks after you.*

*(Your father says Cobble is well and asks if you are warm. He means he saw you at the hill. —M.)*

*(Somebody thought you might miss hazel. —M.)*

A pressed hazel leaf falls out of the fold and lands on Fenna's boot.

Cobble is the family's old horse. Her father loves him more than he would ever say. *He saw you at the hill.* So the sad-eyed carter who asked Rue about the bucket came this far, and looked up the hill at the tent, and turned the wagon round, and went home.

Fenna folds the letter very small.

Mei has to be back on the river by the turn of the tide, and she waits at the gate for a reply, tapping her boot.

So Fenna writes one. The first draft has the whole farm in it: the field, the well, the hens, the bunny, the man on the fence. It is two pages long. She tears it up.

The second draft has the hens in it. She tears that up too.

The third draft has one word on it.

Mei reads it upside down, from habit.

"Is that all?"

Fenna looks at the one word for a long time. Then she folds the paper into her pocket, and nothing is sent at all, and Mei goes back down the hill to her barge with her hands empty. Old Jan goes with her. He keeps looking back.

Between two pages of the book, that evening, Fenna finds a note in thin brown ink:

*Rest the field. Rest yourself. —B.*

She reads it twice. Then she looks at her hands, which are holding a hammer, and a nail, and the edge of a plank, all at once.

---

She builds a cottage.

It takes her a long time and she does it alone, and it shows. The walls stand. One of them leans a little to the left, like somebody listening at a door. Fenna steps back and looks at it for a long time. She decides that it is perfect.

It is the first night under a roof of wood that she has had since she came, and the walls do not breathe in and out. She keeps waking up because it is too quiet.

---

The cow comes off the cart and down the plank on her own, slowly, as if the plank were a red carpet laid for her and was not quite good enough.

She looks at the burnt barn. She looks at the yard. She looks at Fenna.

She does not seem impressed by any of it.

Then she lowers her head and eats a mouthful of Fenna's best hay, very slowly, while looking Fenna straight in the eye the whole time.

That is how Fenna finds out who is in charge.

---

The bunny has questions about the cow.

Mostly the question is: *is the cow a hawk?*

The cow is not a hawk. The cow is not anything. The cow is chewing.

THUMP, says the bunny, at the cow.

THUMP, at the cow's tail, which moved.

THUMP, at the shine on the milk pail, which looked at her.

Then a very small *pad*: a sorry thump. A thump that means, *I may have been wrong about the pail.*

The cow chews through all of it.

Berit has a page on the bunny. Fenna finds it on the page about roots:

*She thumps for a hawk, for a lantern, and for bad manners. Learn which is which. You kept her in a basket when you were small. She allowed it. She still has views. —B.*

So now Fenna knows there are three thumps. One for a hawk: quick, three at once. One for bad manners: flat and cross. And one for a lantern.

She does not know yet what the lantern one sounds like. She finds that she does not want to.

---

By day the white stones at the forest edge are just stones. A row of them, half sunk in the moss, inside the first trees. There is an old oak there, too, with faded ribbons tied round its lowest branch, the kind people tie for luck and then forget.

Fenna goes and looks at the stones in daylight. She does not go past them. She puts her hand on one.

It is warm.

Not sun-warm. The trees are too thick for sun. It is warm the way a cat is warm when it has just got up.

Her hand has been shaking a little, she realises, ever since the letter. On the stone it stops.

---

A boy is waiting at the gate with a basket of herbs so big he has to hold it with both arms and look round the side.

"You're the Moonedge one," he says.

"Yes."

"You used to carry a bucket bigger than you."

Fenna opens her mouth and shuts it again.

She did. She remembers now. A bucket nearly as tall as she was, banging against her legs, all the way from the stream to the pond and back, every morning before it was light. She had forgotten that anybody saw.

"Greenstem," says the boy, putting the basket down. "My mother does the herb stall. She says do you want sage." He looks past her into the yard. "Why is that wall crooked?"

"It isn't."

"It is. It's leaning. Does it hurt when you hammer? Your thumb, I mean. When you miss." He picks up a stick and looks at it. "Did you know sage is good for a cough? And for chickens? Not to *eat*. Well, they can eat it. Do you have chickens? Is that a bunny? Why does she keep stamping?"

He asks too many questions. He asks them one after another, without waiting, like somebody shaking apples off a tree.

Fenna finds, to her surprise, that she does not mind.

She answers about half of them. He does not seem to notice which half.

---

There are so many tree stumps behind the farmhouse.

Fenna has been digging them out since spring and they keep coming. Too many, for such a small farm. Their tops are clean and flat, cut with a saw, long ago.

Somebody cut the forest back. A lot of forest. And nobody planted anything in its place.

Behind the old barn she finds the compost pit. It is lined with flat stones, its corners squared, built by somebody who cared very much about compost. It is clean, and dry, and empty. There is nothing in it at all. Not a leaf. Not a straw.

Scratched into the lid:

*Feed the ground and it feeds you. —B.*

Fenna looks at the empty pit for a long time.

Somebody sold every straw.

---

Greenstem comes again with the sage, and he wants to know why bread is so dear.

Then, before Fenna can answer, he explains it to her, the way a boy explains a thing he only half understands.

"It's the Mark," he says. "You know. The chalk." He sees she doesn't know. "Since the fire, every loaf in Hazelwood gets a chalk mark on it. A little moon. A thin one, like this." He draws it in the air with his finger. "For the farm that burnt. Nobody likes it."

"For the farm? Why?"

Greenstem shrugs. "Because the Lord still wants his share. The share the farm used to pay him. The farm burned down and stopped paying, and he still wants it, so—" He waves at the whole village. "So everyone pays it. A bit at a time. In bread. That's what the chalk is for."

Fenna wants to pay it. All of it, right now. She would pay it twice.

There is nobody to pay.

After that, she sees the chalk everywhere. On loaves. On sacks. On the gate of the mill. One thin chalk moon after another. All for her farm.

On the notice board in the square, between a recipe for parsnip jam and the lost green glove, there is a small paper pinned very square, with a grey edge:

THE MARK STANDS UNTIL THE FARM GIVES AGAIN.

Fenna reads it three times.

*Gives again.* She gives. She has given since the day she came. Porridge, soup, a basket, a whole field.

She looks around the square for someone to tell.

Nobody looks back.

---

Children are skipping in the lane. Fenna wants to get past them. One little girl stops in the middle of the rope and sings straight at her:

*"Chalk, chalk, chalk on the sack,*
*Who's to pay it? Moonedge back!"*

Her mother hushes her, fast.

The rope starts again. The next rhyme is a different one:

*"Lightning in the night,*
*Lightning in the barn,*
*The little one ran out,*
*And nobody came to harm!"*

Fenna stops in the middle of the lane.

The rope slaps the ground. Slap. Slap. Slap.

She goes on walking. She walks all the way back up the hill without noticing the hill.

---

The next morning, the weeds she pulled the day before have been laid out on the path.

Not in a pile. In a face.

There are two dock leaves for eyes. There is a long hairy root for a nose. And there is a smile, made of thistles, curving right across the path. It is a very big smile. It has gone wrong on one side, so that it looks like a smile that is trying very hard and not quite managing.

Fenna stands and looks at it.

Last time, she took the tower down. She remembers how the quiet felt afterwards.

So she does not touch it. She walks round the smile, all day, every time she crosses the yard. Round the left side, carefully, so as not to step on the eyes.

In the evening, when she comes out to shut the hens in, the smile has been straightened. Just a little. On the side where it had gone wrong.

---

She builds a kiln and lights it and shuts the little door, and for a long time nothing happens. Then the cracks round the door glow orange, like the edge of a sunrise.

In the morning she opens it, and the soft, sulky clay has turned into something that rings when she flicks it.

Clay can change its mind, it seems. It only needs a lot of fire.

---

The bread page has a note beside it that makes her laugh out loud, alone in the kitchen:

*Not so much yeast. Patience does the rest. (You will not listen. Nobody in this family has ever listened. —B.)*

Her first loaves are not good. Her fifth loaves are good. Her tenth loaves are so good that she takes them down to the bakery on market day to sell, and that is when she meets Mistress Appleby.

Mistress Appleby is the baker. She is a narrow woman with flour to the elbows and a chalk board on the wall behind her counter. Every loaf she sells is written on the board. At the end of the week, the Lord's tollman comes and checks the board, and it has to balance, to the very last crumb.

Appleby takes Fenna's loaves. She chalks them up. She chalks a mark for each loaf.

And then, beside each one, she chalks a second mark. A thin moon.

"One for the loaf," says Appleby. "One for the name."

So Moonedge pays double. So Fenna is paid less than her loaves are worth. Appleby counts the coins into her hand and does not look up while she does it.

"It's not mine to forgive," says Appleby, very quietly, to the counter. "You understand. It's not mine."

Fenna understands. She takes the coins.

On the way out, the girl who shapes the loaves at the back, Anna, with a plait down to her waist, slips a warm bun into Fenna's basket without a word and goes straight back to her dough.

---

Greenstem turns up at the cottage just as the next loaf comes out of the oven, which is either luck or a very good nose. Fenna breaks it in half. He holds his half in both hands and breathes it in with his eyes shut.

"Rosemary does that," he says seriously, about nothing in particular.

He eats the whole half in four bites.

"It's a good loaf," he says, "for a person who built that wall."

---

She sets up a stall at the market.

It is very odd. Yesterday she was a person who walked up to stalls. Today she is a person people walk up to. Somebody asks her the price of a carrot. She tells them, and her voice comes out a good deal higher than she planned.

---

The road to Hazelwood gets better. Fenna has been working on it, stone by stone, filling the holes with gravel. It goes from mud to mostly mud to almost a road.

One morning, when she has laid the last stretch of new gravel up to the gate, there is a man sitting on one of her crates.

She did not hear him come up the road. She did not hear him at all.

"You," says Fenna.

"Me," agrees Lark, as if that were a lovely thing for both of them. He has his legs crossed. He looks as if he has been sitting there since breakfast. "I came up your road. Marvellous road. A road is a wonderful idea, you know. You put down stones in a row, and then people can find you. Nobody would have thought of it."

"Lots of people have thought of it."

"Then they kept very quiet about it," says Lark.

"That's my crate," says Fenna. "I need it."

"For what?"

"Stacking."

"Ah." Lark gets up. He picks up another crate from the pile and puts it on top of the one he was sitting on. It goes on crooked. It leans to the left, like her cottage wall. He looks at it with his head on one side.

"There," he says. "Done."

"It's crooked."

"It's standing."

Fenna straightens it. Lark watches her do it with great interest, as if she were doing a magic trick.

Then he sees her list.

She pins a list to the fence every morning. It is a long list. It is always long. Lark takes it off its nail before she can stop him and holds it up, and reads it out in a voice like a stern aunt reading a letter.

"*Gravel. Hen house door. Thatch, cut. Thatch, dry. Thatch, more. Roof. Before frost.*" He lowers the paper. "Goodness. Who sent you this?"

"Nobody sent it. It's a list."

"*Before frost,*" reads Lark again, very grave. "No *please*. No *if you have a moment*. Somebody is being very strict with you." He turns the paper over, looking for a name. "Here we are. It's signed."

He holds it up so she can see the bottom.

There is no name there. Only her own handwriting.

"It's signed *you*," says Lark.

"Give it back."

He gives it back at once, very politely, and sits down on the crooked crate, which is now straight, and crosses his legs again.

"I knew a woman once," he says, "who built a lighthouse. Every night she climbed to the top to light it, and every morning she climbed down and wrote herself a list of how to make it better. Taller. Brighter. More stairs." He looks at the sky. "She was so busy making it better that she never once went outside at night to see it shine."

Fenna waits.

"And?" she says.

"And nothing. That's the story." Lark gets up. "Good gravel. Excellent. I'd keep going, if I were you. All the way to the frost."

She turns to say something back, something sharp, something about how it is very easy to sit on crates and tell stories.

The crate is empty. Both crates are still standing, one on the other. The top one is crooked again.

Fenna looks at her list. *Roof. Before frost.*

She picks up the pencil. She crosses out *frost*. She writes *soon*.

Then she looks at the crooked crate for a long time, and leaves it crooked.

---

Hilde the washerwoman has a line full of sheets strung between two posts on the lane. She hangs the last one as Fenna goes by, and it flaps in the wind like a flag from a very small country.

"I hear things about you in the lane, Fenna," says Hilde, not looking at her. Hilde ties a knot in her apron string every time she hears a rumour, and unties it when it is proved wrong. Her apron string is mostly knots. "I hear lots of things."

Fenna stops.

"Good things," says Hilde, to her sheet. "Nobody has one bad word. Not one." She sounds personally offended. "It's ruining the lane."

---

On the page about hay there are two hands in one margin.

The first is Berit's, firm and black, pressed hard:

*Hay: dry it, turn it. Lantern on the hook OUTSIDE, and never past the sill. —B.*

Under it, in a smaller, rounder hand, a hand Fenna does not know:

*Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother. —T.*

Fenna reads it three times. *T.* Berit had a daughter. Fenna's aunt. Tilda. Fenna can almost remember her. A laugh. A big laugh, from the other side of a kitchen. Then nothing else.

She turns the page quickly.

---

She brings home a hive and sets it in the sunny corner of the yard. A hum comes out of it, low and enormous, as if the box were holding a thought too big for itself.

She steps back.

Several thousand tiny creatures have just agreed to live here. They did not ask her. She did not ask them. It seems best to be polite.

Next to the bees, in the margin:

*Talk to them. It doesn't matter what about.*

And under it, in a different pen:

*I mean it. —B.*

Fenna wonders who had to be told twice. Then she goes out to the hive and tells the bees about the cow. At some length.

The first honey, when it comes, is gold and slow. She holds the jar up to the window, and the light goes into it and stays there. It is a spoonful of summer, whatever the weather is doing outside.

---

Old Jan eats a bowl of her tomato soup in small, serious spoonfuls, sitting on her bench. By the third spoon his cheeks go pink.

"This is the sort of soup," he says, "that Berit made." He taps the bowl with his spoon. "And that goat could count, you know. The Moonedge goat. Count to five. Stamped it out with her hoof. Swear it."

Nobody else has ever believed him. Fenna can tell.

Fenna rather does.

---

Otto, the old ferryman, crosses the footbridge Fenna has mended over the stream. He goes slowly, holding the rail. In the middle he stops and looks down at the water.

"Used to ferry people over this," he says. "With a pole. Before there was a bridge."

He stops. He starts again. He stops.

"The night your family left," he says, "I took you across. You were so small. You did not cry."

He looks at her. Then at the water.

"I never forgot," he says.

Fenna says nothing. Neither does he.

The river says a good deal. Then Otto goes on over the bridge, without another word.

---

The roof goes on.

Fenna has been cutting and drying thatch for weeks, and now she climbs the ladder with the bundles on her back and lays them, row by row, up towards the ridge. She wants it done before dark.

On the very last corner, exactly where the last bundle has to go, there is a hammer.

It is the size of her thumb.

It is a real hammer, with a wooden handle and an iron head, and it has been used. The handle is worn shiny. It is sitting right in the corner of the thatch, in exactly the wrong place, as if somebody very small wanted to help and did not know quite how.

Fenna holds the last bundle and looks at the little hammer for a long time.

Then she puts the bundle on the other side of it.

The roof has a bump now, at that corner. A round lump in the thatch, where the hammer is.

She decides it is a feature.

---

The stove ticks as it warms up, the way a cat ticks when it is thinking. The smell of the cottage changes. Wood. Iron. A pot that is very nearly ready to be soup.

She does not have to eat standing up any more. There is a table, and a chair.

She eats standing up anyway, mostly. But not always.

The first leaves of autumn tap on the roof, one by one, as if they are asking to be let in. Then the first rain. Fenna sits under her roof, which is real, and is finished, and is not leaking, and listens to the rain not coming in.

Then she takes a sheet of paper and writes home.

*I'm staying a bit longer.*

Her hand shakes only a little.

---

A lantern stops at the gate the next day. In broad daylight.

It belongs to a tall, stooped man with a long coat and a book under his arm. He lights his lantern in the day, so that people can see him coming. His name is Pieter. He is the night watchman, and he is also the man who carries the Lord's book from house to house.

Pieter looks at the roof for a long time. He looks at the bump in the corner. He opens his book and writes one line in it.

"Good thatch," he says. And goes.

Fenna looks up at her roof as if it might be counted.

---

The barge comes round the bend on Landing Day, and Mei waves a letter. The writing is round and quick, and there is a lot more of it than last time.

*I am not going to say "I told you so", because I never told you anything. Eat properly. Sit down when you eat. The table is still long.*

And then, in smaller letters, squashed in at the bottom:

*Ask Maud about the goat.*

Fenna does not know what that means. She puts the letter with the other one and does not ask Maud anything.

---

Frost.

Beside the frost page, small:

*Cover the beans. Cover everything. Then go and cover Maud's.*

Fenna wakes in the cottage and the light is wrong. Too white. Too quiet. At the window the whole yard has gone soft and silver. Her roof is holding up a whole cold sky, and not complaining about it at all.

She stays under the blanket a little longer than is strictly necessary.

---

On Longnight, Greenstem comes up the hill in the dark with a lantern.

"You have to come down," he says. "To the Hall. Everyone goes."

"Nobody asked me."

"*I'm* asking you."

So she goes. She has never been inside the Hall. It is long and warm and full to the rafters, and every lamp in Hazelwood has been put out except these. Everyone is crammed in together. The children are on the tables. The old people are by the fire. There is a big pot of soup and a big pot of something hot with apples in it.

And there is a custom. On Longnight, anybody who wants to can stand up and say one true thing. Just one. And the person nearest to them passes them a cup.

That is all. Nobody claps. Nobody says anything back. They just pass a cup.

Fenna watches. A man says, "I miss my brother." Somebody passes him a cup. A girl says, "I broke Mother's blue jug and said it was the cat." Her mother passes her a cup, and gives her a look, and also a hug.

Fenna does not stand up.

She takes the ladle from the soup pot instead, so that nobody will expect her to say anything, and she serves soup. She serves it all evening. She serves a great many bowls. No one has asked her to. She does it anyway.

Somebody puts a cup in her hand anyway, halfway through. She does not see who.

She does not know why.

Beside the page about sleep, when she gets home, there is a note underlined twice:

*You, too.*

---

In the winter, Fenna clears the old barn.

She has walked round it since the day she came. She has not walked through it once. But the new barn has to go somewhere, and the old one is in the way, so she pulls up the black beams one by one and stacks them for firewood.

And then she stops.

The burn marks on the beams go one way. They start low down. Very low. Not at the roof, where lightning would strike. They start in the far corner, near the ground, where the hay was kept.

Fenna sits back on her heels in the ash.

Lightning does not start low.

Something else started this.

She sits there for a long time with a black beam across her knees, and she finds that she does not want to know what. Not yet. Not this winter.

She stacks the beam with the others. She stacks them very neatly.

---

Down in the village the children are skipping. Fenna is walking past with a sack on her shoulder. They are singing the lightning rhyme:

*"Lightning in the night,*
*Lightning in the barn—"*

"It started low," says Fenna. Not loudly. Under her breath, without meaning to.

The rope slaps down.

The children look at her. She looks at them.

They swap to the chalk rhyme very fast.

---

In spring the frame of the new barn goes up, beam by beam. Against the evening sky it looks like the ribs of something very large, asleep on its side.

The old barn burned from the corner. Fenna walks all the way round the new one, just to check that every corner is only a corner.

In the ash under the old doorframe she finds the iron hook. It is the one from the doorpost, black with soot, curved at the end. It was made to hold a lantern.

She holds it for a long time.

Then she hangs it back up. On the new doorpost. Carefully.

Old Jan comes up the hill to grumble at the new frame. He likes to grumble at things. It is how he says they are good.

He sees the hook, and stops grumbling.

"That hook," he says. "Hung outside by the barn door, every night of my life. Lantern on it. Never inside. Never past the sill. Berit's rule." He laughs. "Berit's girl, Tilda, she used to say it in her sleep. *Never past the sill.* Laughed like a bucket of marbles, Tilda did."

"Then how did the fire get in?" says Fenna.

Old Jan starts to cough.

"Ask me," he says, between coughs, "when I'm not coughing."

He coughs all the way back down the hill. He seems to cough on a schedule.

---

The new barn is finished. It smells of new pine and fresh straw.

Fenna hangs a lantern for it. Not on a hook inside. On a post outside, well away from the door, with a bucket of water underneath it. She stands back and nods.

Hay and flame should live far apart.

That is as far as she lets herself think.

---

The pond is next.

There was a pond at Moonedge once. Fenna remembers it, a big round one, full of sky. Now it is a dip in the ground full of dry cracked mud, and she wants to know if it is worth digging out.

The cracks in the mud close in on each other in rings. Tighter and tighter. And at the edge of the dip there is a flat stone, worn smooth into a seat. In front of the seat there is a hollow in the ground, the shape of somebody who sat there for a very long time, looking up the hill, waiting for something to come down it.

There is moss on the stone. Only on the stone.

Fenna digs.

It takes her days. The mud is hard and then the mud is soft and then the mud is very, very soft and smells of old eggs. She digs on.

The last spadeful comes out, and the water arrives.

It is slow about it. First a dark patch at the bottom. Then a shine. Then a puddle, then a pool, then a sky lying on the ground with clouds in it.

A frog appears at once and sits on the edge and says nothing at all. It has the air of someone who has been waiting for this for years.

---

Fenna builds a little shell house in the sunniest corner of the yard. It is exactly big enough for somebody who carries their own roof. She steps back and looks at it. It looks back at her, empty and patient.

Something very slow is crossing the yard.

It is a tortoise. She is moving like a stone that has decided to travel. Her shell has moss on it. Her eyes are very old and very calm.

She does not go to the shell house. She does not even look at it. She goes straight past it, very slowly, all the way to the pond. And at the edge of the pond, exactly on the flat stone with the moss on it, where the hollow was, she stops, and sits down.

That is where the spring used to rise. Fenna knows it suddenly, the way you know where your own front door is in the dark.

She waited there. All those years. For the water to come back.

Fenna has a great deal to do. The barn needs a door. The hens need feeding. The list on the fence is as long as her arm.

She sits down beside the tortoise.

She does not mean to. She just does.

The pond fills a little. Then a little more. A breeze goes over it and makes it wrinkle, and the wrinkles go away again. Nothing is hurried. The frog does not move. The tortoise does not move. After a while, Fenna notices that she has not moved for a long time either.

And the second line of the rhyme comes back to her, as easy as breathing.

"Slow shell keeps the water clean," she says.

The tortoise blinks. Once. It takes quite a while.

Fenna stays sitting there until the pond is full.

---

Back in the room, somebody has fallen asleep on the floor with their mouth open.

"Did you find out who started it?" asks Minena. "The fire?"

"Not then," says the old woman. "I didn't want to."

"Why not?"

The old woman does not answer that.

"And the man on the fence," says Minena. "With the buttons. Was he funny?"

"Very."

"Was that good?"

"It made him hard to talk to," says the old woman. Nobody understands what she means.

That is all right.

## Chapter Three: Smallholding

Another letter comes up from the river.

*Stay if you must. Your cousin asks after you.*

Fenna reads the last line again.

Her cousin. Liv. Four years older. Thin and sharp and always watching, with eyes that missed nothing, ever. Liv was twelve the summer of the fire. Liv lived in the same house. Liv was always in the loft, reading, looking out of the little window.

*Your cousin asks after you.*

To anyone else it would sound like a kind line.

To Fenna it sounds like a warning.

---

All that day there is a hush over the yard that Fenna cannot explain. The hens go in early. The wind drops. A dog barks once, far off, and nothing answers. It is an ordinary afternoon, wearing the face of a not-ordinary one.

The next morning, her list has been nibbled.

Just one corner of it. A neat, half-moon bite, right through *Fix gate* and the top of *Stack wood*.

Fenna frowns at it.

She does not have a goat.

She writes the corner out again, on the back.

The next morning, the list is gone entirely. There is nothing on the nail but a little torn scrap. And on the ground beneath it, pressed into the mud, sits a hoofprint. A small, sharp, split one.

Fenna does not have a goat.

She writes the list again, in her head this time, where nobody can eat it.

---

On the doorstep in the morning lies a clover leaf with one bite out of it, and a pebble beside it, nudged a hair straight.

Then one polite thump, from under the step.

The bunny is asking for a job.

Fenna looks down at her. The bunny looks up, with her one crooked ear, and does her very best to look useful.

So Fenna gives her one. The clover. The bunny is in charge of the clover now: where it goes, where it spreads, which weeds are allowed and which are not. The bunny takes this extremely seriously. By evening there is clover coming up between the vegetable rows in little green stripes, very straight, and the weeds have been told.

---

Behind the fence at the top of the farm there is a wall of brambles. It is taller than Fenna. It is thicker than a hedge. It is full of thorns like a great many small opinions.

She wants to know what is behind it. So she pushes in, a little way, with the handle of the rake.

Deep in the middle, where nobody could ever get, there is a hollow. It is lined with moss, soft and green, and it is the size of something small curled up asleep. Caught on one thorn above it is a scrap of white thread. Or hair. Very fine and very white.

Fenna looks at it.

She does not reach for it.

She backs out of the brambles very carefully, and puts the rake away, and goes to do something else.

---

On the salt page there is a long note, in Berit's thin brown ink, all the way down the side:

*Salt on the table first. Then the bread. Then the rent. The Keepers, who farmed here first and made the lease with the Hurst, wrote it in, so: "never so much that the table is bare". The Hurst copied out the rest of the lease and forgot that line. I have not forgotten it.*

And smaller, at the bottom:

*(Goats lick salt. Count your salt. —B.)*

Fenna does not know what the Keepers are. She does know what a bare table looks like. She has eaten at one.

---

The tortoise sits on the flat stone by the pond every day, all day. Fenna wants to know why that stone, of all the stones.

Berit's page on the tortoise says:

*Do not argue with a tortoise. You will lose, slowly. You took her water every morning, at dawn, in a bucket as big as you, when the pond went low. She never thanked you once. That is how you know she meant it.*

Fenna looks at her bucket.

*At dawn.* Every morning. Before the grown-ups went to the far field. She can almost feel it: the weight of the bucket, the cold, the long walk, the wet legs. She had forgotten about it entirely. She had not forgotten it at all.

She puts a stone beside the tortoise to mark where she is, to see how far a tortoise goes in a day.

By evening, the tortoise is two paces past it, and looking back at the stone as if it were a little behind the times.

---

A lantern swings up the road in the daylight.

Under it walks Pieter, the night watchman, with the Lord's book under his arm. He does not look happy. He looks like a man who would like to be almost anywhere else, carrying almost anything else.

He stops at the gate.

"I am sorry, Fenna," he says. "The Lord wants his share. Now that the farm is growing." He opens the book and shows her a number. "It is not my idea. I only carry the book."

Fenna looks at the number. It is not very big. It is not very small either.

"Pay when you can," says Pieter. "I will wait at the gate."

He waits at the gate. He stands there with his lantern lit, in broad daylight, looking at his boots.

Fenna needs seed for the top field. That afternoon Rue's cart comes up the road, and Rue sees the lantern at the gate, and stops.

"Ah," says Rue. "No, love. I can't sell to a house with a lantern at the gate. Lord's share first. Pay Pieter."

"I'll pay him. I need the seed *first*. To grow the things. To pay him *with*."

"I know, love." Rue does not move the cart forward. She does not move it back, either. She turns it sideways, sits on the tail of it, takes an apple out of her eleventh pocket and starts to eat it.

"What are you doing?"

"Waiting," says Rue. "I'm good at waiting. I once waited for a goat."

Pieter has clearly walked the long way round the cart to avoid her eyes. He is now pretending to read his own book.

A hen walks out of the brush by the fence, looks at everybody, and lays an egg right in front of them, in the dust.

Everybody looks at the egg.

"Well, now," says Rue. "I could see my way to buying an egg. If you had one to sell me."

Fenna sells it to her. Pieter gets paid. Rue sells the seed. The hen walks off looking smug.

That always works, with hens.

---

Share Day comes at the end of every season.

On Share Day, Pieter goes from house to house in Hazelwood with his book and collects the Lord's share from everyone. And before he starts, the Hall feeds the whole village from the Pot, so that nobody has to pay on an empty stomach. That is the rule.

This Share Day, the Pot is poor, and the soup is thin. You could read a notice through it.

Pieter is given the first bowl. He always is. That is a rule too.

And every house he comes to sets out a stool for him by the door, so that the man who comes to take can at least sit down while he does it. Nobody says why. They just do it. It is a very Hazelwood thing to do.

Moonedge has no stool yet. When Pieter comes up the hill last of all, he eats his soup standing at Fenna's gate, holding the bowl in both hands and looking down into it, as if it were a window into somewhere better.

At the bottom of his page, in grey ink, there is one line Fenna can read upside down.

*carried down.*

"Down where?" says Fenna.

"Down," says Pieter. He does not say anything else.

---

The next Share Day, Fenna brings a pot of her own to the Hall. A big one. Leeks and beans and her own bread broken into it.

The soup in the Pot goes thick. The whole village goes quiet, tasting it.

And at the gate of Moonedge, when Pieter comes, there is a stool.

Fenna made it herself. Three legs. It wobbles only slightly.

"Sit," says Fenna.

"After you," says Pieter.

"It's for you."

"I couldn't sit while you're standing."

"Well, *I'm* not sitting while *you're* standing."

They stand there, both of them, looking at the stool.

The bunny hops past, looks at the stool, hops up onto it, and sits down in the middle with her ears flat.

So they eat on the ground, the two of them, cross-legged in the grass like children, with the bunny above them on her throne. Sitting down, Pieter looks much older. His tiredness shows in a way it does not when he is standing. He eats every drop.

Then he taps his book shut.

"Thank you," he says. "Most people hide when they see me coming."

He almost smiles. Then he walks off home down the hill, whistling a tune he does not seem to know he is whistling. It goes up, and turns round.

Fenna stands at the gate and listens until he is too far away to hear.

---

The next time Pieter comes, his book has a small, round, wet mark on the cover.

"A bush spat on it," he says. "On the way up. Up by the ridge. Only on the book. Not on me." He holds the book away from himself. "I checked."

Fenna looks up at the ridge. There is nothing there. Only a bush, and the wind.

The bush does not look like a bush that spits. But then, she has not met many bushes.

---

A small boy hangs on her gate one afternoon, swinging. He watches her dig for a while.

"My mum says it was lightning," he says.

"Did she?" says Fenna.

The boy swings. Fenna digs. Neither of them says anything else.

---

Old Odinson lights the forge in the village, and young Bo works the bellows, and they show Fenna how to hold the tongs. Odinson is as broad as a door and about as chatty.

"Good hands," says Odinson, after an hour, which is the most he has said all day.

Bo grins at the fire. Bo is her own age, with a burn on his wrist and a habit of saying "yeah" to everything, and he makes nails one at a time, very carefully, as if each one were going somewhere important.

"I carried a bucket," he says, while Odinson is out of earshot. "That night. In the line. I was little." He hammers a nail. "There was a girl gave me hers. Bigger than mine. It was empty." He looks at Fenna, and then at the nail. "Yeah," he says, to nothing, and hammers it again.

---

She builds a windmill.

It is taller than anything she has made yet, and at the top is nothing at all, a round empty room full of wind, waiting for sails. She climbs to the last step. Hazelwood lies small and clear below her, like a map drawn with great care by somebody who loved it. The Hall. The mill. The river, shining. The Hurst on its hill behind its long wall. And behind Fenna, the forest, going on and on, and somewhere very far inside it, something white.

She does not look at the white thing for long. She has sails to make.

---

There was a mill at Moonedge once, too. An old one, at the bottom of the hill. It is a ruin now, roofless, its wheel stopped and rotting in a stream that is hardly a stream at all.

Fenna goes in to clear the wall, for stone.

On a rusty nail, high up on the wall, hangs a bell.

It is far too small for a cow. It is far too small for a goat. It is far too small for anything at all, except possibly a very fancy mouse. It is grey, and it is hanging there on the nail all by itself, and it sways, just a little, though there is no wind in here at all.

In the dust under it, Fenna thinks she sees footprints. Tiny ones. Smaller than a sparrow's.

She looks the other way.

She is quite sure that she looked the other way.

Below the nail, on the old plaster, there is a drawing in charcoal. A child's drawing. A girl with a bucket nearly as big as herself. She is holding hands with something about the size of a thumb, with a ring of little dots round its middle.

Beside the drawing there are two sooty prints on the plaster. A child's hand. And next to it, a thumb. Or something the size of a thumb.

Fenna does not remember drawing it.

She stands in front of it exactly as long as it takes to stop standing in front of it. Then she finds that she is humming, and she goes out of the old mill very fast, and does not go back in for the stone.

---

She fixes the last sail on the new windmill, and steps back.

For a moment, nothing.

Then the whole thing groans, like an old person getting out of a chair, and the sails begin to turn. Slowly. Then not slowly at all.

The grinding fills the yard. Flour begins to fall into the sack in a soft white rope. The wind is working for her. As far as she knows, it has never done that before.

It was only waiting to be asked.

---

Ella from the village mill brings her great-grandfather up the hill in a wheelbarrow to see the new sails. He is very old and very small and has a very large hat.

"Put that hat on, Grandad, you'll catch your death," says the miller, who has followed them up, glaring.

The miller is Ella's father. Hob Millward. He has a face like a closed door and a mill whose wheel has hardly turned in seven years, because the stream that drives it silted up. He has never once said hello to Fenna. He glares at her windmill as if it had said something rude.

The old man takes his hat off and salutes the sails with it.

"Put it ON," says Hob.

The old man puts it on. Hob stands where he can see the sails, his arms folded. He stands there for a long time.

Ella smiles at Fenna, very quickly, when her father is not looking.

---

The pond is full now. The tortoise is happy, in the way tortoises are happy, which looks the same as every other way. And the overflow from the pond has found its old path down the hill.

The stream runs clear again. All the way down. All the way to Hazelwood.

The next week, Fenna finds Hob Millward standing on the bridge by his mill, looking down at the water. The water is high and quick and brown, and his old wheel is going round. Creaking. Splashing. Turning.

He looks at the water. Then he looks at Fenna.

He nods. Once. A very small nod.

That is his whole apology, and Fenna takes it.

Then he says thank you, fast, glaring hard at the stream. "Now I owe the Hurst a penny a sack again. Thank you."

And he walks off.

Fenna laughs out loud, on the bridge, by herself.

Then she stops laughing, and looks at the stream differently. A penny a sack. Every good thing she does down here, the Lord takes a piece of.

---

The goat comes home in the middle of a sentence.

Fenna is on the bench outside the cottage with a new list, saying it out loud to the bunny, because the bunny is a good listener. "—and then the gate. And then the—"

There is a goat on the bench.

The goat is shaggy and grey, with one horn a bit bent and a beard full of burrs. She has eyes like a schemer. She is eating the list.

"That's my *list*!"

The goat chews. She looks at Fenna as if Fenna has said something very silly.

"Spit it out."

The goat swallows.

Fenna sits there with the pencil still in her hand.

And then she notices that she is not alone on the bench. Lark is sitting on the other end of it. He is holding a scrap of paper, the bottom half of the list, which the goat has not got to yet. He is reading it carefully, with his eyebrows up.

"Where did you—" says Fenna.

"Gate," says Lark. "Excellent invention, the gate. You walk up to the fence, and there's a gap in it, with hinges. Somebody must have been very clever." He holds up the scrap. "She's left the bottom bit. Shall I read it to you? I think you'll find it very gripping."

"No."

"*Item four,*" reads Lark. "*Rest.* Eaten." He turns the scrap over. "*Item nine. Rest.* Also eaten. *Item fourteen. Rest, if time.*" He looks at the goat with enormous respect. "Gone. Every one. She's a very good editor."

"She hasn't *read* it," says Fenna. "She's a *goat*."

"Hm," says Lark. "Then it's very lucky she ate the right bits."

He folds the scrap and puts it in one of his pockets. It is a pocket Fenna did not know he had.

"I knew a king once," he says, "who made a list every morning. Very long list. Wars, mostly, and taxes, and who to marry. And at the bottom of every list, every single day, he wrote: *Go for a walk in the garden.*" Lark looks up at the sky, which is very blue. "Fifty years, he wrote it. Every day. At the bottom."

"Did he ever go?"

"He had a very nice garden," says Lark. "I'm told."

The goat finishes the list. She burps, politely, and starts on the corner of the bench.

When Fenna turns round to tell Lark that she does not need a story, thank you, she needs a *list*, the bench is empty, and there is a dent in the cushion where he sat, and the goat is eating the cushion too.

Fenna writes a new list.

It is shorter than the old one. At the very bottom, after a moment, she writes: *rest*.

Then she pins it high up on the fence, out of reach of goats.

---

That evening, her three letters from home are lying on the fence post, in a little stack. She did not leave them there. They have been sorted by length. The shortest is on top.

The shortest is the first one. *Come home when you are ready.* It is the one whose folds are the most worn, because it is the one she reads the most.

Fenna picks them up. She puts them back in her pocket, in the same order.

---

On the third Share Day, Pieter checks his book twice at the gate, with the face of a man looking for a reason to turn round.

There isn't one.

"It's the same as last time, Fenna," he says. "Only more."

He accepts a cup of water and drinks it as though it might be evidence.

Fenna wants to see the book. She wants to see the number, the real one, the one at the top. Pieter does not show her. He shuts the book.

But he says one thing. He says it to the gatepost.

"The chalk on the widows' sacks down at the Landing," says Pieter. "It's very small, these days. The marks." He coughs. "The Lord never said how small a mark has to be."

Fenna cannot thank him out loud. She knows that, somehow, without being told. So she does not.

When he has gone, a long way down the road, a lantern waves at her in the daylight. Pieter.

She waves back.

---

On market day the village buys eight loaves, and the ninth sits.

That is how it is now. The village does not have much money, and there is only so much bread people can eat. Fenna's loaves are good. So people buy Fenna's loaves. And the ninth loaf, the one left over at the end of the day, is Mistress Appleby's.

Nobody says it is Fenna's doing. Nobody says anything at all. Until Anna, from the bakery, says it quietly, looking at the floor, while she wraps a bun.

"She's saving for a new hearth. She's been saving three years."

Fenna has to decide what a good neighbour does.

She gives up her corner of the market every second week. On those weeks she sells to the Nest instead, the village orphanage at the end of the lane, for less, much less, and the children of the Nest have bread with honey on it, and Appleby's ninth loaf sells.

Appleby does not say thank you. She cannot. Thank you would mean she had noticed.

But there is an extra bun in Fenna's basket the next week. And the week after.

---

She gets a sheep. The sheep comes off the cart and stands in the yard looking like a cloud that has lost its way. She is round and white and cannot see her own feet. She regards Fenna gravely, as if Fenna were a rather puzzling hat.

She builds a loom, and the loom clacks. Over, under. Over, under. At first the cloth looks like a lie, a few crooked threads pretending. After an hour it is a hand wide. After two, it is a hand and a thumb.

Cloth, Fenna decides, is just patience that has learned to hold its shape.

In the margin of the weaving page:

*A mistake in the first row is still there in the last one. That is fine. It is how you can tell it is yours.*

---

Somebody has folded the blanket at the end of Fenna's bed, with the corners just so.

It was not her. She is fairly sure it was not her.

There is a faint smell of lavender in the room. She gets into bed without thinking about it any further, which is the best thing to do with a mystery when you are very tired.

---

The new scarecrow has a bow in its hat.

Fenna did not put it there. She checks the knot. It is her knot, a farm knot, the kind Berit taught her. Somebody has watched her tie knots.

She decides not to ask any questions that might get answered.

---

Among the old trees at the bottom of the orchard, Fenna finds a rope hanging from the thickest branch. A swing. A plank, worn smooth by somebody sitting on it, a long time ago.

The knot at the top has been retied. Not well. Not long ago.

She tests the swing with one hand. It holds.

She does not know who retied it. She does not sit on it.

Not yet.

---

Granny Maud comes up the hill to fetch her goat. Her goat is in Fenna's yard. Her goat is in Fenna's yard most days, now. It sits next to Fenna's goat and the two of them look at Fenna's washing like a pair of critics.

"She keeps coming up here," says Maud, with the air of someone solving a crime. "Do you suppose it's a clue?"

"No," says Fenna.

Maud nods, sadly. "It is, though."

She pats Fenna's arm, takes the rope and leads her goat home. Her goat looks back twice.

Fenna remembers the letter. *Ask Maud about the goat.* She still does not ask.

---

The notice board in the square has a new notice, in a very firm hand:

WELCOME BACK. WE HAD OUR DOUBTS.

It is not signed. Under it somebody has drawn a small goat. Next to it, the lost green glove is still pinned up, left hand, still waiting.

Fenna does not know who wrote the notice or what the goat means. She takes it as a compliment.

---

At harvest there is a dance in the Hall.

Fenna has not been to a dance since she was seven. She does not intend to go to this one. She intends to bring the cider, because she has made cider, and the cider has to get there somehow.

Rosa the fiddler is setting up in the corner, tightening strings. Rosa is old and brown and fierce, and her fiddle is called Gertrude, and she has been trying for years to finish a tune that has been going round in her head with no ending.

"Fill the Late Mug," says Rosa to the room, pointing her bow at a big blue mug on a hook. "For whoever comes last. And the rest of you, shush, I'm tuning."

So Fenna works the cider barrel while the village dances.

It is loud and hot and very bright. Mugs go up over people's heads and down again. Fenna fills them and passes them and fills them, as fast as she can.

And then the mugs start to float.

Just a little. Just one or two. A mug lifts off the table on its own and wobbles over the heads of the dancers, slowly, like a fat little bird. Then another. Nobody seems to notice. Everyone is dancing.

One mug wobbles too far. It tips. It falls, straight towards a small girl's head—

Fenna catches it.

She does not even think. Her hand is just there, and the mug is in it, full, not one drop spilled.

And Fenna laughs.

Out loud. A real laugh, from the bottom, the sort she has not laughed in years. It surprises her so much that she nearly drops the mug again.

Somewhere very close by, something goes *tink*.

One small sound. Like a very small bell. A sorry sort of sound.

Fenna does not look round.

The mugs do not float again all night.

---

She works through the whole dance. She is the last one there, stacking mugs, when the others have gone. So the Late Mug is hers.

She drinks it, slowly, sitting on the edge of the stage. And while she stacks the last of the mugs, she hums.

Rosa freezes, with her fiddle half in its case.

"Hum that again."

"I'm not humming," says Fenna.

"You were. That. Hum it again."

Fenna puts down the mugs and leaves. Rather quickly.

Behind her, all the way down the lane, Rosa plays the tune on Gertrude. Badly. Over and over. Never quite getting to the end.

---

One autumn day, Fenna goes down to the Landing Steps for nails, and the whole village is there.

Nobody is talking. They are standing on the steps, all the way down to the water, in a silent crowd. Looking down the river.

"What is it?" Fenna whispers to the nearest person. "What's everyone looking at?"

Nobody answers.

Round the bend comes a barge. It is not Mei's. It is long and low and painted grey, all over, even the ropes, and it has no name on its side at all. It is sitting low in the water, very heavy.

The Lord's share is on it. The sacks from Pieter's book. All of Hazelwood's chalk marks, carried down. Going down the river. To somewhere else. To someone else.

The children on the steps have been told to hold their breath until the grey barge has gone past. That is the custom. They try. They fail, one after another. Little pops of breath, all down the steps, like corks. A small boy goes purple and his sister thumps him on the back.

A hand takes Fenna's hand.

It is a child's hand. She does not see whose. It just holds on, until the grey barge has gone round the next bend and the river is empty again.

Now she knows where the share goes.

*Down.*

---

The next summer, the children of Hazelwood want to run the Chain.

The Chain is a line of buckets. On the night Moonedge burned, the whole village made a line from the stream all the way up to the barn, and passed buckets of water hand to hand, all through the night. They did not save it. But they tried.

Every summer after that, for a few years, the children played Chain on the square in memory of it. Then they stopped. Nobody wanted to remember.

But this summer the farm on the hill is smoking again, in a good way, and the children want to play Chain. And Maud wants her bucket put on the gatepost, as always.

This time more than Maud comes up the hill.

Half the village comes. With buckets. With tin leaves under the rims.

They make a Chain from the Deep, the big well in the middle of the square, all the way to the Hall and back. They pass buckets hand to hand as fast as they can, and they are very wet, and the fastest team gets a bun.

Ansel the bell-ringer rings the Hall bell. Five fast. Then one slow. Five fast means *fire*. The slow one means *all's well*.

Fenna is handed the last bucket, at the end of the Chain. She is supposed to carry it. She has always carried the last bucket.

She stands there, holding it.

Then she turns round, and passes it on.

Somebody takes it. She does not even see who. The Chain goes on without her.

Fenna stands with her empty hands and does not know what to do with them.

---

Afterwards, Tom finds her, still wet, with his sheep.

"Someone got off Mei's boat at dusk," he says. "Didn't say a word. Grey coat. Went up the hill and came straight back and got on again. Left before the buckets."

Lotte and Linus come running.

"The Moonedge ghost has got a *sister*," says Linus.

"She's grey," says Lotte.

"She leaves *rocks*."

Fenna goes up the hill and looks.

On top of the gatepost, at the end of the row of flat grey stones, there is one more stone. It is clean, and new.

Fenna does not wait at the post. She thinks about it, all evening, standing at the cottage window. She does not wait.

---

She builds a bigger barn. Maud's goat walks in, walks out, climbs up the side of it and stands on the roof, looking down at Fenna, very pleased.

Fenna has built a barn. She has been given a goat roof. It is not the same thing.

Fenna sets four buckets of water at the corners of the barn, full. Then eight at the farmhouse. Just in case. The goat drinks out of one. The tortoise sits in one. A hen lays an egg in one.

---

One evening, everyone is going in for the night. The hens, the cow, the bunny, the goats. And the tortoise is sitting at the gate.

The bunny comes and presses herself against the tortoise's shell. And inside a circle exactly one tortoise wide, nothing at all happens. Nothing hurries. Nothing worries. Nothing thumps.

Outside it, the wind blows and the trees creak and something rustles in the hedge.

THUMP, goes the bunny, as soon as she steps out of the circle.

She steps back in.

Nothing.

---

Beyond the old fence, the land goes on.

Fenna had not looked. She had been so busy looking at her own fields that she had never gone and looked past them. Long grass. A leaning post. A bird she does not know, doing something complicated with a twig. There is room here for more than she thought. It has been standing here the whole time, waiting for somebody to notice.

And there is the bramble wall, in the way of all of it.

The goat trots up the path. Her goat. Not Maud's. The grey one, with the bent horn. She walks straight into the brambles that nobody could clear.

CRUNCH.

MUNCH.

She eats thistles as if they were sweets. She eats brambles as if they were spaghetti. She eats a whole thornbush, from the bottom up, with her eyes half shut, like somebody enjoying a very good cake.

Fenna opens her mouth to stop her. She has the shears in her hand. She was going to do this herself. She was going to do it all herself, all summer, slowly, with blood on her arms.

She closes her mouth.

She lets her.

The goat eats for an hour. Fenna sits on the fence and watches. And somewhere in the middle of all that munching, the third line of the rhyme comes back to her, and she says it with her mouth still open.

"Brave goat clears the bramble bed."

The goat looks up. She looks at Fenna's hat. Her eyes are very thoughtful.

---

In the morning the scarecrow is wearing Fenna's hat.

Berit's hat. The green one. The goat had been looking at it. Now it is on the scarecrow's head, high up, out of reach of goats, at a very jaunty angle.

Nobody saw who put it there.

Under the hat, tucked into the band, there is a letter. It is from Mei.

*Your cousin asked me three times whether she might come up to your gate. Twice at the dock and once on the plank. I told her to ask you herself. She said she was asking. —Mei*

Fenna takes her hat back.

It is still warm.

---

Granny Maud comes up for her goat and sees Fenna's goat at the bramble wall, eating. She stops.

"Brave goat clears the bramble bed," says Maud. Crossly. From memory. As if the goat had cheated by doing it in front of her.

The children in the road pick it up. By the end of the week, they are skipping to it.

---

Fenna makes the spare bed.

She makes it with square corners. Then she makes it again with hospital corners. She plumps the pillow. She puts a candle beside it, and a jug, and a pie, in case somebody is hungry in the night.

For nobody.

Nobody comes.

The goat eats the pie.

---

"Were you scared of the cousin?" asks somebody from under a blanket.

"More than of any imp," says the old woman.

"What's an imp?"

"Shh!" say three children at once. "*That comes later!*"

## Chapter Four: Village Trade

A thin man stands at Fenna's gate with a ledger under his arm.

He does not smile. He does not look unkind, either. He looks like somebody who counts things, and has counted this gate already, and found it to be one gate.

"Master Quill," he says. "The Lord's steward."

He has a long grey coat, very clean, and a pencil behind his ear, very sharp. His boots have been polished by somebody who takes boots seriously.

"The old farm smokes again," he says, and looks up at her chimney. "The Lord wishes to know why."

Fenna thinks about this.

"Because somebody lives here," she says.

Quill takes the pencil from behind his ear and writes that down.

---

Quill's hat has a round dark spot on the brim. It is wet. It was not there when he started up the hill, Fenna is fairly sure, because he is not the sort of man who would walk about with a spot on his hat.

He does not mention it. Neither does Fenna.

Up on the ridge, a bush moves in the wind.

---

Quill counts everything.

He counts the cows. He counts the sacks in the barn. He counts the windows and the nails in the gate and the hens. He counts the hens twice, because one of them moved. Every number goes into the ledger, small and neat, in very sharp pencil.

Then he goes down to the pond to count the fish, and while he is bent over the water, he puts the ledger down on the bank.

When he picks it up again, two pages are missing.

Fenna finds them a little later. They have been folded, very cleverly, into a paper boat, and the paper boat is sailing across the middle of the pond with the wind behind it, quite fast, as if it has somewhere to be.

Somewhere close by, a very small bell tinkles. A quick, jingling, delighted little sound.

Quill is not amused.

Fenna is. A little. She has to turn round and look at the hens for a while.

---

Quill fishes the boat out with a rake. He unfolds the wet pages and spreads them on Fenna's fence to dry, pinning them down with stones.

The ink has run. The Lord's accounts are written in two colours, red and grey, and both colours have bled into long pink and grey smears, like a sunset that has been crying.

But down the edge of each page there is a third column. Small, faint, in pencil. It has not run at all. Every number in it is perfectly clear.

Quill looks at the pencil column for a lot longer than he looks at the ruined ink.

Fenna looks at it too.

"Notes," says Quill, and folds the pages up very quickly.

---

The next day he comes back to count the hens properly, because he was not happy with yesterday.

Granny Maud is passing, with her goat, and she stops dead in the lane.

"You can't *count hens out loud*," she says. "Everyone knows. It's bad luck. Count them out loud and one goes missing."

Quill looks at her over the top of his ledger.

He finishes the count in a whisper.

Nobody loses a hen. The village decides, when it hears about it, that Master Quill is either very brave or completely doomed.

---

The road gets cobbles. Fenna has worked on it all spring, stone by stone, and now it is a proper road, all the way to the village, with a hard edge and a ditch on either side.

In the soft earth at the side of the new road, there are hoofprints. Two of them, side by side, neat as a pair of shoes, as if something stood there for a long time.

They are at every place on the road that has a good view.

---

Because the road is good now, the letter bag no longer comes up the hill. On Landing Day, Fenna goes down to the river for her letters, like everyone else.

She does not want to be seen waiting. She asked Mei, last time, not to make a fuss. "Call me like everyone. Just call the name."

So Mei calls the names.

"Appleby!" "Here!"

"Hewitt!" "Here!"

"Moonedge!"

The crowd waits.

Old Jan is standing right at the front, and he opens his mouth to shout it, the way he has for seven years. Then he shuts it again. He looks round at Fenna.

Fenna has to say it.

"Here!"

It comes out small. But it comes out.

Old Jan beams like a lamp.

The Landing claps, the way it claps at everyone's *Here*. Just a little. Just politely. As if it were nothing special.

On the plank of the barge, a figure in a grey coat has stopped halfway down, with a bag on its shoulder. It hears Fenna's voice. It turns round, and goes back up the plank, and below deck.

Mei says nothing about it, with great skill.

---

Fenna leaves the new blanket she has woven folded over the fence, to air.

In the morning it is draped there in artful folds, one corner tucked in, as if a painter had arranged it for a picture.

Next to it, in the mud, two hoofprints.

---

In the margin of the masonry page:

*A wall is only lots of small decisions, all going the same way.*

---

One morning a man comes up the road with a bundle of sticks.

He is round and brown and smells of earth, and he is the Lord's gardener, Thorne. He looks over his shoulder four times between the gate and the door.

"Apple grafts," he says, and holds out the bundle. "From the old Moonedge orchard. Before. I took cuttings, when the trees were going. Kept them alive up at the Hurst, in the back of the glasshouse, behind the lemons." He pushes them at her. "Twenty-two of them. They're yours. They were always yours."

Fenna takes them. They are just sticks. Brown and thin and knobbly. Each one has a little label tied to it, in a gardener's careful hand. *Moonedge Russet. Moonedge Sweet. Berit's Red.*

Forty paces behind Thorne, at the bottom of the track, stands Master Quill, with his ledger.

Thorne sees him and goes the colour of a beetroot.

Quill sees the sticks. He looks at them for a long moment.

Then he writes something down, and walks on, without a word.

The orchard gets its old apples back.

---

Another day, Quill counts the hens again.

The goat follows him round the yard. Her goat. She walks two steps behind him with her head down, very interested in the ledger.

"Seven," says Quill.

STAMP, goes the goat, with one hoof.

STAMP. STAMP. STAMP. STAMP. STAMP. STAMP.

STAMP.

Eight stamps.

Quill looks at the goat. The goat looks at Quill.

He counts again, with his jaw set. One, two, three … seven.

There is a hen behind the rain barrel.

Eight.

Quill writes "8" in his ledger, and does not write down why. Then he reaches up behind his ear for his pencil.

The pencil is gone.

The goat is chewing.

---

Fenna builds a room for her books. It is only a small room, a shelf and a chair and a window, but she has never had a room just for books. Berit's farm book goes in it, and the bookseller's three books she bought on market day, and the almanac, and a book of knots, and a book about bees that she has read eleven times.

She steps into the new room, and stops.

Somebody has sorted all her books by weight.

The heaviest book is at the bottom of the shelf. The lightest is at the top. In between, they go down in a perfect, careful slope. Nothing else is different. It is just that every book is now exactly where its weight says it should be, and nowhere else.

Lark is sitting on the chair. He is sitting on the heaviest book, which he has put on the chair, and he is reading the lightest one, which is a pamphlet about turnips.

"Very good system," he says, without looking up. "Very good. Look how the heavy ones hold up all the others. You'd never know."

"How did you get in?"

"Door," says Lark. He turns a page of the turnip pamphlet. "Wonderful thing. A sort of wall that opens. I can't think why they don't put them everywhere."

Fenna opens her mouth to say several things.

"This bit about turnips," says Lark, holding it up, "is very moving."

She takes the pamphlet off him. When she turns round with it, to put it back where it was, the chair is empty, and the heaviest book is lying on it all by itself.

She looks at the shelf for a long time.

She does not put them back in the old order. She tries. She gets as far as the first two. Then she leaves them as they are.

---

Fenna wants one more plank in the coop door before dark. She is working by lantern light.

The bunny is under the fence. The bunny thumps.

THUMP-THUMP-THUMP.

Quick, and three at once. The hawk thump. The fox thump.

"In a minute," says Fenna, and waves her hand. She is holding a nail in her mouth. "Nearly done."

She hammers on.

In the morning there are feathers by the coop. Brown feathers, speckled ones, scattered across the grass.

One hen fewer. The speckled one. The Other One.

The bunny sits by the empty nest box, and when Fenna comes out into the yard, the bunny does not thump. She does not do anything. She only looks.

Fenna puts a bolt on the coop door. A strong one.

The goat sleeps on the roof of the coop that night, standing up, like a guard. And every night after.

---

She builds a glass kiln. Sand goes in. Fenna waits. Something comes out that she can see straight through, and it is the very same sand, only now it lets the light walk in one side and out the other.

She holds the first bottle up to the window. The whole yard turns upside down inside it, small and green and tipped over, and for a moment the whole world is in her hand.

In the margin of the glass page:

*I once held a bottle up to the sun and cried for no reason. Do not be surprised.*

---

Under the stones of the old glasshouse, where Moonedge once grew its tomatoes, Fenna finds a rusty tin.

Inside the tin is a paper. It is old and yellow and folded in three. It is the lease. The real one. The agreement between the Lord and the farm.

She reads it standing up, in the ruin, with the tin in one hand.

It is long and full of words like *heretofore* and *whomsoever*. And in the middle it says that the farm shall give to the Lord:

*… a half of all that the land gives.*

A half.

Fenna takes out the burnt page from the workshop, the one she found on her very first day, the one she has kept folded small in her pocket ever since. She holds them side by side.

At the bottom of the burnt page, circled twice: *one in two*.

The same number.

The Lord has always had the right to ask for half. And in the last years before the fire, he asked for it.

Pieter has never asked for half. Not yet.

Fenna puts the lease in a drawer in the cottage and shuts the drawer. She does not open it again.

She knows it is there, which is nearly as bad.

---

On Share Day, Quill wants the lease read before witnesses.

"The Book requires it," he says. The Book is the Lord's great ledger, up at the Hurst. The Book requires a great many things.

So the lease is read at Fenna's fence, in front of the village, and the village comes up the hill to hear it. Maud. Appleby, with her arms folded. Anna. Hilde. Old Jan. Ella and her father. The twins, sitting on the fence. Everybody is very quiet.

Quill has his own copy, the Hurst's copy. It is much shorter than Fenna's. He reads it out in a clear, dry voice.

"*… a half of all that the land gives.*"

The fence goes still.

Then he reads out the new things the Book has found. "One glass kiln. One cheese vat. One press. The orchard." Each one lands like a small stone in a bucket. *Thud.* And with every one, the number Pieter will carry down the hill gets bigger.

The goat eats a corner of the Lord's notice.

Fenna goes into the cottage. She comes out with the tin. She puts it on the fence rail, in front of Quill, and opens it.

Quill stops reading.

"This one," he says slowly, "is longer."

He takes the old lease out of the tin, very carefully, by its corners. He reads it. He reads all of it. He reads it right down to the bottom of the page, where nobody ever reads.

There is a line there.

He goes quiet for an official minute.

Then he sits down. On an empty nail keg, by the fence, without being asked, which Fenna would not have thought he knew how to do.

"Might I copy one line?" he says.

He copies it into his ledger, in ink. It is eleven words long. It takes him four minutes.

*never so much that the table is bare.*

And in the corner of the old lease, next to the Lord's crooked seal, there is a tiny mark in faded ink. A flourish. A letter *Q*, written in a young, careful, rather proud hand.

Quill looks at it for a long time. Then he folds the lease back into its tin, and gives the tin back to Fenna, and walks down the hill without counting anything at all.

---

Greenstem comes up the next day to tell her what is being said in the Square.

"They're scared," he says. "Of Quill. Of the counting. They think if the Lord counts you, he'll count them next."

He tells her what Maud said: "She's one of ours."

He tells her what Appleby said: "She's got a farmhouse. We've got a chalk board."

Maud and Appleby stop speaking to each other. For a whole week.

On market day the Pot is cold.

---

Quill is at the gate again.

"The Lord's share," he says. "As before. With the new lines added." He opens the ledger. "And the north field."

The north field is resting.

Fenna planted it with clover in the spring, and left it. *Rest the field.* It is the first field on Moonedge that anybody has let rest in a very long time. It is green and buzzing and full of bees. It is not growing anything to sell.

"The Book," says Quill, "has no line for a field that grows nothing."

Fenna opens her mouth.

"Oh, *pay* it," says Lark.

He is sitting on the fence. Of course he is.

"Pay it all," says Lark, delighted, swinging his legs. "Pay it twice. That's the spirit. Look at that field. *Lying about* in the sun all day, doing nothing. Disgraceful. Plough it up! Plant wheat in it. Plant wheat in it twice a year!" He waves his arms. "And the pond, look at that pond, just sitting there full of water. Drain it! Water the wheat with it! The hedges, grub them out, think of the room! Sell the straw. Sell the compost. Sell the clover. Chop down the forest, it's only standing there—"

"*No*," says Fenna.

It comes out of her before she knows it is coming.

Lark stops. He looks at her with polite interest.

"No," says Fenna again. She is looking at Lark. Her face has gone hot. "You can't. That's what they *did*. They ploughed the clover and they drained the pond and they sold every straw, and they cut the forest, and the ground went thin, and the water went, and the—" She stops. She has run out of breath. "You *can't*."

"Can't I?" says Lark, mildly.

Fenna turns round. To Quill.

"No," she says. "The north field rests this year."

Her voice is smaller than she hoped it would be. But it does not shake.

Quill's pencil has stopped moving.

There is a long silence at the gate. The bees in the clover go on buzzing.

"You've counted the cows," says Lark to Quill, quite kindly, as if continuing a conversation they had been having for years. "And the hens. Twice. And the nails. Have you ever counted the clover?"

"I—no."

"Shame. It's been counting you."

Quill looks at him. Then, slowly, as if he cannot help it, he writes that down too.

When Fenna looks back at the fence, Lark is gone, and Quill is staring at what he has just written in the ledger as if he has no idea how it got there.

---

In the middle of the newly cleared land beyond the old fence, there is one bramble left. Just one. It is the size of a kitchen table, and very thick, and very green.

The goat walks round it. She does not eat it.

Fenna reaches for the shears.

The goat steps between Fenna and the bramble. She does not butt. She does not bleat. She just stands there, in the way, chewing nothing, with her bent horn towards Fenna.

Fenna puts the shears down.

Something in the bramble rustles.

Nobody looks.

---

She builds a glasshouse, a new one, with the glass from her kiln, and steps inside and shuts the door, and the weather stays outside like a dog that has been told.

Warm air. Wet air. Green air. Rows of leaves reach up for the glass. The first tomato has turned red, whatever the season is doing outside, and Fenna takes off her hat to it.

Mulch, says the next page. And beside it:

*A blanket for dirt. Yes, dirt gets cold. Do not argue.*

---

"It's a long road," says Pieter. He has come up it for Share Day, and he sits down on Fenna's fence without being asked, and sets his lantern down beside him, still lit, in broad daylight.

Then he takes a little flask out of his coat and offers it to her. That is not in his book either.

It is cold tea, very sweet. They each take a sip.

Then he stands up, and straightens his coat, and becomes a watchman again.

"Same time next season," he says.

---

The next time, Pieter comes up the road with his lantern swinging and his book under his arm, and for the first time ever, he does not open the book at the gate.

He just stands there.

"I wanted to say, Fenna," he says. And stops.

He tries again.

"It's heavy," he says. "I know. I'm the one who carries it."

Fenna nods.

It is the nicest thing anybody has said to her all week.

Afterwards, he gives her a receipt. She has never had a receipt before. It says what she paid and what it was for, in Pieter's tall, slanting writing. And in one corner, in tiny letters, it says *Thank you*.

Somebody took the trouble. She hopes it was Pieter.

She folds it small and keeps it in her pocket with the burnt page.

---

Captain Mei holds a jar of Fenna's pickles up to the light and nods, as if it has passed an exam.

"For the long voyage," she says.

Then she puts a letter down on Fenna's table. "Your mother. She asked me to hand it over myself."

Mei does not stay for tea. She does stay for a very long look at the letter, before she goes.

The pickle jar comes back from the long voyage two Landing Days later. It is empty and clean. Inside it there is a pressed leaf, a brass button, and a folded paper in a small, round hand that Fenna does not know.

*Have you still got the bucket? —L.*

Fenna looks at the bucket.

It is in her hand. It is always in her hand. She carries it everywhere. She carries it to the well and the barn and the field and back, she carries it full and she carries it empty, she carried it all the way from Berit's cottage in the box. She did not even know she was holding it until now.

She holds it a little tighter.

Later, she hangs the old iron hook by the new cottage door, and the bucket underneath it, on a nail.

She does not look at how well they fit together.

---

The next morning, she decides she does not want to be seen holding the bucket.

So she hides it. Behind the rain barrel. It is a very large bucket and a fairly small barrel, and the bucket sticks out on both sides.

The bunny comes and sits on top of it.

The goat comes and looks at it.

Fenna goes off to work with her hands empty. She weeds a row of carrots. She keeps reaching for the bucket, and it isn't there, and her hand closes on air.

By lunchtime she has gone back and fetched it. Her hand is empty, and that will not do.

---

The paved road has reached the Hurst Gate, at the bottom of the Lord's hill.

And there, on the Lord's Mile, the smooth gravel road that leads up to the Hurst and has always been the best road in the valley, there is a puddle.

The whole village has come to look at it.

It has never had a puddle before. Not one. The Lord's Mile has always been raked and filled and gravelled, every week, from the Hurst's own great heap of gravel, beside the gate.

The children have named the puddle. It is called Gordon.

Tally, the tollman, has measured it with a stick.

Mayor Juno wants it filled at once, before anybody from the Hurst sees it. But nobody dares to fetch gravel from the Hurst's heap, because that is stealing from the Lord.

And then somebody notices that it would not be stealing, because the heap is gone.

There is no gravel. Just a flat patch of ground, where the heap used to be.

The village laughs. Then everybody goes quiet, all at once, together.

---

The goat wants the pond reeds. The tortoise is sitting on the pond reeds.

The goat tries three ways.

First, she leans on the tortoise. The tortoise does not move.

Second, she butts the tortoise, very gently, on the back of the shell. The tortoise does not move.

Third, she lies down next to the tortoise and makes a long, sad, whistling face, as if she has never been so hungry in her life. The tortoise does not move.

By evening the goat has fallen asleep beside the tortoise. The tortoise has moved four inches.

The goat wakes up and finds the reeds free, and is too embarrassed to eat them. She eats the daisy growing out of the moss on the tortoise's back instead.

It grows back by morning.

Berit's goat page has a corner bitten off. Of course it has. The rest says:

*Goats lie. Count them anyway. She can count; she will not count what she has eaten. You hid her in the cellar once. She ate the cellar door. I was busy; I only noticed the draught.*

---

On Longnight, Fenna goes down to the Hall, as she does now. She goes straight to the soup pot to take the ladle.

Greenstem is already holding it.

"I'll do it," he says.

"I can—"

"I know you can. I'll do it."

He does not give it to her.

So Fenna has nothing to do. Nothing to carry. Nothing to stir. The whole Hall is warm and loud and full, and she is standing in the middle of it with empty hands.

She sits down.

People stand up, one by one, and say their one true thing. Somebody passes a cup. Somebody else stands. Somebody passes a cup.

Fenna stands up.

She did not mean to. Her legs just did it. The people nearest look at her and wait.

She says one small, true thing.

"My feet hurt."

The person beside her passes her a cup.

It is only cider. She drinks it. It costs her more than the soup ever did.

---

The night after Longnight, Pieter asks her to come up on the town wall with him, to the Watch Walk, where he walks every night with his lantern.

"Want to show you something," he says.

Fenna wants to be home before dark. But she goes.

From the top of the wall you can see the whole valley, dark and silver. And up the valley, on its hill, at the edge of the black forest, there is a light. One small, yellow, square light.

A window.

It is her window. It is Moonedge.

She has never seen it from the outside before. She has never seen her farm the way the village sees it, from down here, in the dark: a window, lit.

She stands there for a long time.

Then Pieter holds his lantern up and looks the other way, out over the marsh below the farm, where the river spreads out into reeds.

"There's a light out there, too," he says. "Some nights. Very small. It doesn't move like a lantern." He rubs his eyes. "Moves like it's dancing."

Fenna looks. She does not see anything. Only dark reeds, and the river.

"I'm paid to notice, you see," says Pieter. "I'm not paid to say."

He says it anyway. And then he walks off along the wall, whistling, so she will not ask any questions.

She does not ask.

---

It was spring when Fenna came here, with a bucket and a plan and no idea.

She looks around the yard. The plan has changed a great deal.

The bucket is by the door, under the hook. She gives its rim a pat on her way out, the way you would pat a horse that has done a good day's work, and goes to see what the day wants.

---

The day wants a pony.

There is a pony at the gate. She is hazel-brown, with a long mane that she has clearly arranged, possibly in the pond. She is standing very still, with one hoof forward and her head up, so that she can be seen from her best side.

Fenna looks at her.

The pony turns her head, slowly, to show Fenna her other best side.

Fenna has an order to take down to the village. A big one. The biggest she has ever had. Sacks of flour, a crate of eggs, honey, cheese, cloth, three jars of pickles and a goose that is not coming quietly. She was going to carry it all herself, in four trips.

The pony lets her load the sacks onto her back. And the crate. And the honey and the cheese and the cloth and the jars. And the goose. Then the pony sets off down the new cobbled road, with her head high and her tail up, as if she owns the road and is graciously letting the rest of the valley use it.

Fenna walks beside her.

---

On the square, people stop.

They look at the pony. They look at the load on the pony's back. Then they look at Fenna.

"That's her," somebody says. "That's Fenna."

Her name. Not *the Moonedge girl*. Not *Berit's granddaughter*. Not *the one from the burnt farm*.

Fenna.

And the fourth line of the rhyme comes back to her, standing there in the square in front of everyone.

"Strong pony brings the world its bread."

She says it out loud. A few people hear her. One of them, old Agnes, nods, as if she knew that line was coming.

---

Mistress Appleby, with her chalk and her board, has to mark every sack of Fenna's big order, in front of the whole Square.

The village watches. Nobody knows whether to cheer or not.

The pony poses through the whole chalking. Head up. Hoof forward. Side on. She turns, slowly, so that each sack can be chalked from her best angle.

Somebody laughs.

Appleby's chalk breaks.

She chalks the last sack with the broken stub. The thin moon on it is very, very small.

---

The pony puts her whole head over the fence that evening, while Fenna is shutting the hens in, and breathes on Fenna, once, warm and long, as if she is checking that Fenna is still warm.

She is.

---

Quill asks, because the Book says he must, to borrow the pony and the cart for six days, to haul stone on the Lord's road. The old lease says *carriage on request.* He reads it out to her from his ledger.

The pony turns her head and shows him her other profile.

Quill looks at the pony for a long time. The pony looks back at Quill, from her best side, without blinking.

Quill withdraws the request. He writes *declined (pony)* in the ledger and underlines it twice.

For a moment he looks as if something has gone right, for once, and he is not sure what to do with his face.

---

At the end of the season, Quill rides back up to the Hurst with his ledger full.

At the gate he stops his horse and looks back at the farm. The barn. The pond with the tortoise on its stone. The orchard of little sticks with labels on. The north field, resting under its clover, buzzing.

"The farm gives back more than it takes," he says. He says it as if it were a question. "The Lord will want to know."

Then he rides away. Fenna stands at the gate and cannot tell whether that was a good thing he said, or a very bad one.

---

"Was the Lord a villain?" asks Minena.

The old woman thinks about it for a long time. So long that one of the grandchildren pokes her, to check.

"He was afraid," she says. "That's worse, sometimes."

Nobody says anything. Then the smallest one whispers, from under the bed, "Afraid of what?"

"Of a number," says the old woman. "Now go to sleep."

They do not. Not yet.

## Chapter Five: Healing

Down in Hazelwood, somebody is coughing.

Then somebody else.

The children first, as always. A dry, barking cough that goes on and on, in the night, all over the village, house after house, like dogs answering each other. Half the school is empty. Nobody knows what to do.

Wren the healer comes up the hill with a basket on each arm.

Wren is small and grey and quick, with hands stained green to the wrist and a face that has given up smiling for the time being. She lives over the hill in the next valley, and she does not come to Moonedge for a chat.

"I cannot do this alone, Fenna," she says, at the gate. She says it as if it costs her a tooth. "There are too many of them. Berit had a still-room. Behind your kitchen. Did you know?"

Fenna did not know.

"Would you look?" says Wren.

---

Quill is in the cottage when she gets back, counting her shelves.

He has been sent to count the shelves. He is not enjoying it. When Fenna comes in he stops, with the pencil in the air, and does not start again.

"I have been looking for the lease's twin," he says. "In the Hurst's records. The other copy. I did not find it." He shuts the ledger. "I found a report instead. It was made on the day after the fire, by the Reeve. A man named Rudge. It was never shown to anyone."

"What does it say?"

"I have decided not to read it aloud."

"Why?"

Quill looks at the shelves. He looks at them for a long time, as if they might add themselves up.

"It names a girl," he says. "Eight years old. Seen running from the barn. And a witness, twelve." He tucks his pencil behind his ear. "I count things, Fenna. That is what I am for. I do not like what this one counts."

He goes.

Fenna stands in the kitchen.

Then she puts the kettle on, and makes a pot of tea, and drinks none of it, and makes another.

---

The still-room door sticks. It always stuck. Fenna knows that before she touches it, the way her hand knew the notch in the well. She puts her shoulder to it and shoves, and the door gives, and the smell of old herbs rolls out and says hello.

It is a small room, with a stone floor and a high window and shelves all the way up. Jars. Bundles. Bottles with no labels and bottles with too many. A table in the middle, scrubbed white. A hearth in the corner, with a black kettle on it.

Somebody has been here.

The floor has been swept, in small, careful circles, round and round, but never quite all the way to the end, as if the sweeper got tired, or was not sure they were allowed. There are footprints in the dust at the edges. Wide ones. Flat. Patient-looking.

The kettle on the hearth is cold. The lid is off. It is sitting just where a cup would go, as if somebody had been about to make tea, a long time ago, and was interrupted.

Under the last shelf lies a page from the farm book, torn in two. Only the top half is here. In a tidy hand, not Berit's, a hand that is very neat and very small:

*Tea first. Always tea first.*

The bottom half is missing.

Somebody was here before her. And cared.

---

Wren pins a big paper to the notice board in the square. It is a map of the village. Every house on it has a little mark when somebody in it is coughing, and the marks move across the paper like a rumour.

In Wren's own hand, one name is circled. *Lotte.*

The other notices on the board have changed. Fewer ladders, more spoons.

*HONEY FOR A COUGH. ASK AT NUMBER 4.*

*IF YOUR CHILD HAS A FEVER, SEND FOR WREN. DO NOT WAIT.*

And underneath, in smaller letters, in somebody else's writing:

*and please stop eating things you find*

It seems like good advice. Fenna decides to take it.

The lost green glove is still there, at the bottom, left hand. Nobody has had time to come for it.

---

A letter comes from the river. Mei brings it up herself.

*I heard there is a cough. My mother's recipe: honey, thyme, and the water from the second boiling. It works.*

*I am not asking anything.*

*—Liv.*

Fenna looks at the writing for a long time. It is small and round.

She has seen a hand like that before. In the hay margin, under Berit's: *Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother.* Tilda's hand. Liv's mother's.

Liv writes like her mother.

Fenna folds the letter very small and puts it in her apron pocket. She does not show it to Wren. She will do this herself.

---

Wren is waiting at the edge of the forest with her arms folded.

"One rule," she says. "Only one. Bring me your basket. Every time. Before you use anything in it."

"I know what plants are."

"Do you." Wren takes a leaf out of her own basket and holds it up. Then another. "Plants have twins. Some of the twins are poison. I do not care how clever you are, Fenna Moonedge. I care that you wake up tomorrow." She almost smiles. Not quite. "It will cost you a small favour each time. A biscuit will do."

Fenna lays two leaves side by side on the still-room table that evening. Same shape. Same size. Very nearly the same green. One makes a good tea for a cough. The other would make a very bad day.

She tastes neither. She wraps them both in a cloth, and takes them to Wren, with a biscuit.

Beside the foxglove page, Berit has written in thick capitals, pressed so hard the pen went through:

*LOOK, DON'T TOUCH. I MEAN IT. —B.*

And underneath, in the smaller, rounder hand:

*She does.*

---

Berit's page about the pony is in the herb section, for some reason. Possibly the pony ate the page it was meant to be on.

*Will carry anything. That is not the same as being able to. She went lame one spring. You brought her an apple every day and told anyone who stood still. Ponies do not pretend to be lame. Remember that.*

---

Fenna goes into the forest edge every day now, with Wren's list. Only to the edge. Only where the white stones are, and a little past them.

She finds things.

A ribbon on the old oak, one of the faded ones, has turned all the way round on its branch and settled. One end of it is fresher than the rest.

One of the white stones is warm on its shady side.

On a stump in the meadow, there is a crust of bread. Somebody has eaten the middle out of it.

On the soft forest floor there are her own boot prints, from yesterday. And beside them, smaller ones. Much smaller. They walk beside hers for a while, and then go off on their own, between the roots, somewhere she cannot see.

---

Greenstem comes with her one day, to carry the basket.

"Stop whistling," he says. "You'll scare the birds."

"I'm not whistling."

"You are."

"I'm *humming*."

Greenstem stops dead on the path.

He knows the tune. He has heard it. He does not know where, or when, and it is plainly bothering him. He hums it back to her, to check. He gets it wrong. He gets the turning-round bit backwards.

Fenna says nothing.

She is not afraid of the tune. She has never been afraid of the tune. Only of what it wants.

---

The pony wants to carry everything to the still-room in one trip.

Fenna lets her. The pony poses at the gate, head up, hoof forward, magnificent. Loaded to the ears.

But her tail is clamped tight between her legs.

Fenna sees it. She looks at the pony's tail for a long moment. Then, without a word, she takes one bundle off the pony's back and carries it herself.

The tail loosens a notch.

Fenna takes another.

The pony looks round at her, over one lifted shoulder, for a long time. Then she walks on to the still-room with her tail swinging.

*Ponies do not pretend.*

---

She hangs the first bunch of herbs to dry, from a hook in the still-room ceiling. Then another, and another, until the ceiling is a forest upside down.

In a week the still-room smells like a meadow that has come indoors. The leaves crumble in her fingers, soft as old paper. A thing that was green and loud is now brown and quiet, and just as useful as before. Possibly more.

She makes a salve. It is thick and yellowy-green and smells like the inside of a hedge. She dabs a tiny bit on a scratch she has had for days. It stings for one breath. Then it just feels cool.

Greenstem, who has been watching from the doorway, writes something down. Probably it says *Works.*

---

Far off, between the trees, deep in the forest where she has never been, something shines.

It is white. It is curled round on itself, in a spiral, like a shell, like the inside of an ear. It is very far away, and very still, and very big, bigger than the stones, bigger than a house, and the green light of the forest moves across it as the leaves move, slowly, so that it seems to be breathing. It has been there a long time. Longer than the trees round it. Longer than the farm, longer than the village, longer than the Lord's hill and the Lord's wall, longer than anybody's name for anything.

Fenna holds her breath.

Then she goes back to her basket.

Some things are for later.

---

Fenna is at the forest edge, kneeling in the moss, sorting thyme into bundles of exactly twelve stalks. She is humming. She has been at it since before dawn.

Lark is talking to a tree.

It is the third oak along. A big old one, with a hollow at the bottom. Lark is standing in front of it with his hands behind his back, leaning forward politely, like somebody trying to make conversation at a party.

"Mr. Mumble?" says Lark, to the oak. "No? Mr. … Mumbo? The Honourable Mumbles?"

A very small silence comes out of the oak.

"He's very good at it," says Lark, to Fenna, over his shoulder.

"Who is?"

"Nobody," says Lark, and wanders over, and sits down on the nearest white stone. The stone does not seem to mind.

Fenna goes on sorting thyme. Twelve. Twelve. Twelve. She hums.

"What's it called?" says Lark.

"What's what called?"

"That." He nods at her. "The tune you're humming."

"I'm not—" Fenna stops. She is. "I don't know. It hasn't got a name. I don't know where it comes from."

"Oh, it's got lots of names," says Lark. "Dozens. Some people call it the Hedgehog Waltz. Out east they call it Sir Turnip's Lament, which I've always thought was a bit unfair on the turnip." He looks up at the trees. "Some people call it Washday. Some people call it Get Up, You'll Be Late."

He leans back on his hands.

"But mostly," says Lark, "it's called *yours*."

Fenna stops sorting.

"Badly hummed, mind you," says Lark. "Very badly. You keep skipping the slow bit. Still. That's the trouble with it. It only really plays when somebody stands still." He sighs. "Terrible tune for people with jobs."

Fenna stops humming. She does not hum again for the whole rest of the day.

She sorts another bundle. Twelve.

Lark yawns.

It is an enormous yawn. It goes on for a very long time. When it is finished his eyes are half shut.

"Long day," says Fenna, without looking up.

"Me? Not at all," says Lark. "I'm not tired. Never tired." His head droops. "I am resting my eyes. Upright. It's a new method. Very—"

He is asleep.

He is asleep sitting up on the white stone, with his chin on his chest. He snores, very gently, like a cat with a cold.

Fenna stares at him.

"You're *asleep*," she says.

Lark's eyes open, very wide, very fast.

"I am not," he says, with great dignity. "I'm thinking. With my eyes shut. Much quicker. You should try it."

"You were *snoring*."

"Thinking noises," says Lark. "Some people think very loudly." His eyes are closing again. "I'll just finish this thought," he says. "And then I'll help you with all that thyme."

He is asleep again.

Fenna looks at him. She looks at her thyme. She has sorted four hundred stalks of thyme into bundles of twelve since before the sun came up, and there are four hundred more in the basket.

She goes back to sorting.

When she has finished the basket and looks up, the stone is empty. There is a single bundle of thyme lying on it, tied with a bit of string. She counts it.

It has eleven stalks.

---

Greenstem stands in the still-room doorway the next day and sniffs. He sniffs the whole room, slowly, from left to right, like somebody reading a book with his nose.

"Can I learn this?" he says. To Wren.

Wren grumbles something about boys and mud and boys who track mud. Then she says yes.

Greenstem goes pink to his ears and does not ask a single question for an entire hour.

---

The kettle clears its throat and begins to sing. Nothing could be more ordinary.

Fenna wants one more pot of tea, for the Hewitts' baby, who has the cough the worst. It is very late. She has made eleven pots today.

The bunny thumps.

It is the third thump. The flat, cross one. *Bad manners.*

"I am *not tired*," Fenna tells the rabbit, and pours.

The bunny turns her back on Fenna and sits that way, facing the wall, for the whole of the rest of the day.

---

Once, only once, Fenna uses a leaf from her basket before Wren has seen it.

She is in a hurry. It is three in the morning. Lotte's breathing is bad and the leaf looks exactly right. It is exactly the right shape. Nearly exactly the right green.

Then there is the ceiling.

She is lying on her back looking at it. She does not remember lying down. There is a blanket on her, and a cup of something very bitter at her lips, and Wren's face, very close, very white.

Wren is not smiling.

"What were you *thinking*?" says Wren.

Fenna's legs are shaking under the blanket. When she can sit up, much later, she finds her purse is gone, every coin of it: spent on the antidote, and on the boy who ran for it, and on the man who drove the cart through the night to the next valley and back.

"You are well," says Wren. Her voice is quite steady now. "That is all that matters. Next time, the basket comes to me first."

She puts her hand on Fenna's head, very briefly, as if checking whether it is still there. Then she goes back to stirring her pot.

In the margin of the page about asking:

*Ask. It is not a failure to ask. The best healer I ever knew asked first.*

---

At the end of the season, Pieter comes up through a yard full of coughing children.

They are everywhere. On the bench, on the step, in the hay. Their mothers are with them. Wren is in the lean-to with a child on her knee, peering down its throat, and it is too dark in there to see.

"Hold that," says Wren to Pieter, without looking round, and points at his lantern.

Pieter holds it.

He holds it exactly where she points, for a long time. He holds it up while she looks at throat after throat. He does not open his book once.

When she has finished, he comes and finds Fenna, and speaks very low.

"The Book says a third, this season," says Pieter. "A third of everything. I'm going to write down what the till can bear instead. A quarter. No more."

"But the Book—"

"The lease has a line at the bottom," says Pieter. "*Never so much that the table is bare.* Nobody reads the bottom of the page." He looks round at the yard. At the children. "Don't tell the Hurst."

Fenna pushes her coins at him anyway.

He pushes them back.

She pushes them again.

He folds her fingers shut round them, with his own big hand, and holds her fist closed until she stops pushing.

In the end, she lets him. It is the first gift she has taken since she came. It is much harder than giving one.

"If the grey line comes up short," says Pieter, "somebody up there will ask why." He picks up his lantern. "It'll be me they ask."

---

At the bottom of Berit's old healer's chest, the big oak one in the corner of the still-room, there is a roll of paper tucked into the lid.

It is the other half of the torn page.

Same neat, small hand as the top half.

*The badger left after the fever, when nobody let the children rest.*

Fenna reads it twice.

She was one of those children.

She remembers the fever winter, the one before the fire. She remembers being hot and cold at once. She remembers lying in the dark, and somebody shaking her shoulder before it was light, and the bucket, and the cold walk to the field.

She counts it on her fingers. Who had the fever that winter.

Herself. One finger.

The two little ones, her brother and sister. Two fingers. Three.

Aunt Tilda, who laughed like a bucket of marbles. Four.

Not everyone got up again.

Fenna's hand stops at the fourth finger. She holds it there. She thinks about someone older, who was not allowed to stop either, who went out to the field with a fever because the share was due. She does not know who she means. She thinks she does.

She puts the page in the chest, and closes the lid, and goes and makes more tea.

---

Fenna makes one more tea, and one more, day and night.

She does not stop. She hums sometimes, and when she hears herself, she stops humming, and goes faster. She does not sleep much. She eats standing up, at the still-room table, with a spoon in one hand and a jar in the other.

"You never stop, do you?" says Wren, from the door.

"I'm not tired," says Fenna.

Then the pot she is holding tilts, all by itself. And the floor comes up to meet her, quite gently, like an old friend.

---

Wren finds her on the stone floor.

"*You never stop,*" says Wren, quite differently from before, and picks her up, and carries her to the bench by the stove. Wren is small, but she is strong.

Later, half awake, with the room going round, Fenna says one thing.

"The bucket's empty."

"What bucket?" says Wren.

"Hers," says Greenstem, from the doorway. He looks frightened. "By the door. Under the hook. She always keeps it full."

Wren looks at him. Then she goes out to the rain barrel, and fills the dented bucket to the brim, and carries it in, and sets it down beside the bench where Fenna can see it.

She does not ask anything at all.

---

The village wants to feed her.

Fenna, in bed, wants to send every bowl straight back down the hill, filled, with something better in it.

Wren forbids it. For three days.

So the bowls pile up. Soup from the Hewitts. Stew from Odinson. Bread from Appleby, with no chalk on it. A pie from the Nest, a bit squashed. Porridge from Rue, in a pot with forty pockets' worth of string tied round the lid. Fourteen bowls, in the end, stacked up in the yard, with cloths over them.

The goat eats a doily off the top of one.

Fenna cannot give them back. She has nothing to give. She has to lie there and be fed, which is the hardest work she has ever done.

---

That same morning, she wakes to the smell of thyme and honey.

The whole cottage smells of it.

Wren is at the stove. She has found the folded letter in Fenna's apron pocket, read it, and made the syrup, exactly as written. Honey. Thyme. The water from the second boiling. She has made a cauldron of it. She has sent Greenstem running down the hill with it in jars.

By evening, the children's coughs are easing.

In Wren's own book, in the margin beside the recipe, Fenna sees that Wren has written:

*(from L. of Weir)*

"Who is L.?" says Wren.

Fenna looks at the ceiling for a while.

"My cousin," she says.

It is the first time she has said it out loud.

---

Fenna wants to get up. Wren forbids it.

On the doorstep in the morning there are flowers. A few white flowers, small and pale, with petals like paper. They are tied together once, round the middle, with a thread.

Wren picks them up and looks at them for a long time.

"They are not in my book," she says. "And I have the biggest book in three valleys." She turns them over. "Do not eat them. Put them in water."

---

The tea set in the still-room came with two cups. Of course it did. It is a set.

Fenna is allowed up for one hour. She pours herself a cup.

And then, without deciding to, she puts the other cup on the table too, on the other side, as if somebody else were going to sit down.

Nobody comes.

Nobody needs to come. It feels better, with two.

---

There is a badger at the still-room door.

He is sitting very upright, with his back straight, the way a person sits when they have come about something important. He is old and grey and striped, and he smells strongly of dried herbs, and on his nose are a pair of small, round spectacles.

He looks at the healer's chest.

He looks at the tea set.

Then he looks at Fenna. At her face. And at her hands, which are shaking a little round the cup.

He comes in. He does not hurry. He has never hurried in his life.

He takes the second cup, the empty one, and sets it in front of Fenna. He takes her full one and sets it in front of himself. He pours a little more into each from the pot. Then he sits down on the floor on the other side of the table and looks at her over his spectacles.

*Tea first*, says the look.

Fenna sits.

For the first time in a long, long time, she does nothing. Nothing at all. She just sits on the bench in the still-room and drinks her tea, and the badger drinks his, and the steam goes up from both cups in two thin lines.

And the fifth line of the rhyme comes back, quietly, in the middle of a sip.

"Wise badger heals what aches and stings."

The badger lifts his cup, very slightly, as if to say: *Yes. Obviously.*

---

The next day a child with a cough is carried into the yard. It is one of the little Hewitts, and she is coughing so hard she cannot breathe in between.

Everybody rushes.

Her mother rushes. Wren rushes. Greenstem rushes, and drops a jar. Fenna is up off the bench before she knows it.

The badger does not rush.

He puts on his spectacles.

He sniffs the child, once, all over.

He puts the kettle on.

He gets out the cups.

He waits for the kettle.

The kettle boils.

He pours.

He sits down, and pats the floor beside him.

*Hm,* he says, which is the only sound he ever makes.

He sips.

And something strange happens. The panic runs out of breath before the tea runs out of the pot. Everybody slows down. Everybody sits. The mother sits. Wren sits. Greenstem sits on the jar he dropped. Fenna sits, without noticing she has done it. The child sits on her mother's knee, and breathes, and coughs, and breathes, and coughs a bit less, and breathes, and sips.

By the end of the second cup, she has stopped coughing.

---

Afterwards, the badger takes the tea set on his knees and lies down on the spare bed. The spare bed in the cottage, the one Fenna made up for her cousin, the only bed in the house that still has square corners.

*Tea first*, the badger seems to say, very firmly, from the pillow.

Then he gets down, and Fenna is in the spare bed instead, though she does not remember getting into it.

She sleeps until the tea is cold. She sleeps until the next tea is cold, and the one after that.

---

The next morning there are more white flowers on the step.

Fenna puts them in a cup, with the others. As she does it, from somewhere outside, very close, she hears two soft rings of a very small bell.

*Ting. Ting.*

She stands still with the cup in her hands.

The flowers stay open in the cup all that night. They glow, very faintly, in the dark of the kitchen, like the stones at the forest edge on her first night.

---

Pinned to the cottage door in the morning, held on with a thorn, there is a card.

It is a scrap of bark, flattened. On it, in careful claw-scratches, there is a picture. A basket. A bunch of leaves. A cup.

And underneath, a very small moon.

Fenna looks out into the dark yard. Two eyes look back at her from under the hedge, low down. Level. Patient. Behind spectacles.

She pins the card back up. It seems rude not to.

---

Greenstem is in the herb bed, stripping a plantain plant. He has been told plantain is good for a cough, and he is taking every single leaf off it, as fast as he can.

"Leave two," says Fenna.

She hears her own voice say it. It is not her voice. It is Wren's.

Greenstem looks up at her as if the herb had spoken.

He leaves two.

Fenna walks on down the path, a little taller, and a little frightened of how it sounded.

---

Beside the forest path a small stream runs, and today there are petals on it. White ones. They are not from any tree Fenna knows.

She follows them with her eyes until they turn behind a bend.

She does not follow them any further.

There will be time.

---

On her way home through the orchard, the old swing is moving.

There is no wind.

Fenna walks past it, slowly. She does not look back to see if it stops.

---

The pony takes the long way home from the square that week, so as to be seen. One child follows her. The next day, three. The day after, there is a drum. The pony counts them with her ears and makes the route longer.

---

Rain on the roof. Steady, in no hurry. The kind of rain that means: *stay in. Make something warm.*

Fenna stays in. She is learning.

---

A boy comes running up the hill in the rain with a letter.

The seal on it is red wax, pressed with a heavy ring. The ring has a face on it, and the face looks as if it has had a very long day.

"From the Lord," the boy pants, dripping. "From the Hurst. For you. They said to give it right into your hand."

Fenna takes it.

She does not open it.

She puts it on the table. The badger looks at it, over his spectacles. Then he looks at her.

He says nothing.

He puts the kettle on.

---

"Did you get better?" asks Minena.

"Badgers make very slow tea," says the old woman. "By the time it was ready, I was."

Somebody laughs. Somebody yawns. Somebody asks if badgers really have teapots, and somebody else says *of course* they do, and somebody else says *don't be silly*, and it takes a while for everybody to settle down again.

"And the letter?" says Minena. "From the Lord? What did it say?"

The old woman looks at the window, for a long time.

"I didn't open it," she says. "Not that night."

## Chapter Six: Master Farm

In the morning, Fenna opens the letter with the red seal.

It is short. The Lord does not waste ink on farms.

*Moonedge prospers. The share therefore returns to a half, as before.*

*—W.*

*As before.*

Fenna reads it twice. She knows that number. It is at the bottom of the burnt page, circled twice, pressed so hard the pen went through. It is the number from before the fire.

Inside her, something old sits up and stretches. A pattern. Up before light. Bucket. Field. Bucket. Field. More. More. More.

She folds the letter small. Then smaller. She opens the drawer where the lease is, and the other letters are, and puts it in with them, and shuts the drawer hard.

The badger is watching her from the hearth.

He gets up. He crosses the kitchen, very slowly. He opens the drawer.

He does not take out the Lord's letter. He takes out the other ones. Her mother's letters, every one that has come up from the river since the first Landing Day, folded small and smaller and pushed to the back. He unfolds each one and smooths it flat on the table, and puts a cup on top of it to hold it down.

Six letters. Six cups.

*Tea first,* says the badger's look. *Then read.*

So Fenna reads them in order, with a cup of tea, sitting down.

*Come home when you are ready.*

*I'm not going to say I told you so.*

*Stay if you must.*

*Eat properly. Sit down when you eat.*

*Ask Maud about the goat.*

Every letter asks for a little less than the one before. The first one asks her to come home. The last one hardly asks for anything at all. Only that she eat, and sit, and ask Maud something.

But every letter is longer than the one before.

Fenna looks at that for a long time.

Then she goes down the hill, and finds Maud at the Pot, and asks her about the goat.

"*Finally*," says Maud. She puts down her spoon. "Your mother means the old Moonedge goat. The one Jan's always going on about."

"The one that could count."

"She could count," says Maud. "Jan's right, for once. Don't tell him." She leans in. "Every Share Day the Lord's man stood in your yard and counted the sacks onto his cart. And that goat stood beside him and counted too. *Stamp, stamp, stamp.* And every single time, she stamped one short. On purpose. Looked him right in the eye and stamped one short." Maud's face creases up. "And he believed her. Every time. Wrote down what the goat said. Your mother used to laugh till she had to sit down on the step. Your grandmother pretended not to see. Only time I ever saw Berit pretend anything."

Fenna stands at the Pot with her mouth open.

"She wanted you to know there was laughing up there," says Maud. "Before. That's all. Not just the other thing." She picks her spoon up again. "Took you long enough."

Fenna walks home up the hill. On the way she folds the Lord's letter small again, because she cannot help it. But she does not put it in the drawer.

---

She wants to read the Lord's letter a third time. She goes to the drawer.

The goat has it.

The goat is standing in the middle of the kitchen, chewing, with a small strip of red wax sticking out of the corner of her mouth.

Master Quill arrives at that moment, at the open door, to explain the letter. He finds the seal on the step, and a goat chewing something red, and Fenna standing very still with her hand out.

Quill looks at the goat.

"I regret," he says, "that I cannot read to you what the Lord has written."

There is a pause.

"I did not write it," says Quill.

He looks at the goat for a long moment. He does not take the seal off her.

---

At the Pot, on market day, the village argues about the half.

Everybody has heard. Everybody knows that number.

"We *know* that number," says Maud, banging her spoon on the edge of the Pot. "We all know that number. We watched that number eat a whole farm."

"So the farm should hide its books," says somebody. "Hide the cheese. Hide the honey. Let Quill count an empty barn."

"And then he counts *us*," says somebody else.

Mayor Juno stands on a crate in the middle of it all and says that, whatever happens, the Harvest Fair is coming, and the tables need setting. She has a scroll with the order of the day on it. She has been practising it out loud all week to her goat.

And then Mistress Appleby, who has not spoken one word to Maud since the lease was read, walks across the square and stands beside her.

"Maud," says Appleby.

"Appleby," says Maud.

That is all. But everybody heard it.

The village chooses the tables over keeping quiet. Appleby gives her whole store of flour for the Pie Table, three years of saving for a new hearth, all at once. Maud puts her own spoon in the Pot, which is a thing you only do at weddings.

Nobody knows what the Lord will do.

---

A new trader has set up on the square. A broker, with a cart full of things from downriver: spices and buttons and string and a stuffed fish in a glass box. He has a wide hat, pulled low, and a very red scarf, and a coat with more pockets than anyone has counted.

"Barter?" he says, as Fenna walks past with a cheese.

"I—yes. Yes."

He winks at her.

He has a very familiar face, under the hat. Very familiar. Fenna decides to stop wondering whose.

"Cheese," says the broker, taking it off her. "Lovely. What do you want for it?"

"A fair price."

"Oh, there's no such thing," says the broker cheerfully. "But we can have a go."

He wraps the cheese in paper. And while he wraps it, he whistles.

He whistles her tune.

Exactly. Not badly. Not like Greenstem, not like Rosa, not like Pieter. *Exactly*, every note, going up, and turning round, and up again—

And at the place where the tune stops for a moment, the place where it rests, before the last part, the part nobody ever gets to—

He stops.

He picks up the cheese and studies it, with great attention, turning it round and round in his hands, as if it were the most interesting cheese he has ever met.

Fenna waits for the rest of the tune.

He goes on studying the cheese.

She waits.

"Very good cheese," says the broker at last, and gives her three spools of blue thread and a tin of pepper for it, which is not a fair price at all. It is far too much. And he will not take any of it back.

---

On the ridge above the marsh, in the evenings, there is a tall shape.

Fenna sees it as she shuts the hens in. It is standing very still on the skyline, facing down towards the reeds, with a long neck and a small head. It does not come down.

It hums. One long, low note, that goes on and on, and then stops.

The next evening, the goat goes up the ridge to talk it down. Fenna watches them from the yard. The goat and the tall shape stand nose to nose for a while.

The goat comes back down without it, chewing a ribbon.

---

She drains the old marsh meadow by the river. It takes a long time. Ditch after ditch. The water goes away, down into the river, and slowly the black mud turns to grass.

And at dusk, on the first dry evening, the flowers open.

They are white. They are small and pale, with petals like paper, the same as the flowers on her doorstep. And there are hundreds of them, all across the old marsh, opening one by one as the light goes, slowly, as if somebody were walking along a row of lamps and lighting them, one, and the next, and the next, until the whole meadow is glowing softly in the dark, silver and white, like a piece of the sky that has come down to lie on the grass for the night and has not yet decided whether to go back up. They have been here the whole time. Under the water. Waiting.

In the very middle, where the first flower opened, there is a ring of thimbles. Tiny silver thimbles, pressed into the ground. Every one of them full of water.

Somewhere in the dark, very quietly, there are bells.

Nobody comes out to meet her.

Fenna stands at the edge of the meadow for a long time and does not say a word.

---

The second orchard goes in, row after row, a green stair up the hill. The little knobbly sticks from Thorne are trees now, with leaves, and three of them have apples.

Down in the old orchard, the swing is still moving, just a little, as if somebody has only just got off it.

On the plank there is an apple core. Eaten right down to the pips.

Fenna does not need to know who. She tests the knot. It holds.

---

The seed shelf is full. Rows and rows of tiny paper envelopes, each with a name on it, in a different hand. *Carrot. Bean. Maud's good pumpkin. Do not lose.* They rustle faintly when she walks past, a shelf full of next summer.

In the margin of the seed page:

*Always save the best one for next year. And the second best for a neighbour.*

Next to the worms, Berit has written:

*They do the real work. Call them by name.*

There follows a list of names. They are all Gerald.

---

The new big oven takes six loaves at once. The whole lane smells of it. A queue forms outside the cottage door that nobody organised. Hilde is in it, pretending to be in the way. Old Jan is behind her, very much not pretending.

---

On her bed, one morning, there is a patchwork cover.

Every square of it is a different cloth. She can pick out a few. Maud's checked apron. A scrap of blue, like the cloth she dyed in the vat. A green one from the bakery. Something that smells, very faintly, of lavender. All of Hazelwood, sewn together in squares, by people she has never quite caught doing it.

---

The last letter from the river comes on a windy day. Mei hands it over herself, and waits while Fenna reads it.

*We're coming to the Fair. All of us.*

*Even your cousin.*

Fenna puts it in her pocket. She keeps touching it all day, to check it is still there. And to check it is still saying that.

---

In the square, somebody is testing a drum. Then, firmly, somebody forbids it.

The flags go up between the houses. A hundred small triangles, red and yellow and blue, and one that is nobody's colour, a sort of muddy green, because somebody's aunt made it. The wind finds them, and they all lean the same way at once, like a crowd turning to look at something good.

The notice board is so full now that the notices have started climbing on top of each other. Near the top, somebody has written WHOEVER IS DOING ALL THIS, PLEASE DON'T STOP. Fenna straightens it.

At the very bottom, half buried, is the lost green glove. Left hand. Still pinned there, since her first market day. Nobody has ever come for it.

Fenna stands in front of the board and laughs until she has to hold on to it.

The tables go out on the square. Long ones, end to end, all the way round. Everybody brings something. Pieter brings a lantern cake, round and yellow, with a candle in the top. Greenstem brings a basket of mint. Hob Millward brings a sack of his best flour, with a face like thunder, and puts it down with a thump.

The whole village works with Fenna, setting up. She does not have to do it all.

She keeps trying to anyway.

---

Quill wants to count the north field before the Fair.

The north field has rested a whole year, under its clover. And the Book still has no word for a field that rests.

Quill stands at the edge of it with his ledger, trying words.

"*Idle,*" he says, and crosses it out.

"*Waiting.*" Crosses it out.

"*Asleep.*"

"Snoozing," says Lark, from the fence. "Lollygagging. Having a little lie-down. On its holidays."

Quill crosses all of those out too.

Fenna's farm book lies open on the fence post, where she left it, at the page that says *Rest the field. Rest yourself.*

Quill reads it. He reads it twice.

Then he writes, in his ledger, *fallow.* And in the margin, very small: *per B.*

He walks out into the middle of the clover with his sharp pencil. He counts the bees. He counts the clover heads in a square yard and multiplies. He counts the worms in a spadeful of soil, and they are all called Gerald. He counts twice, because he does not believe what the pencil says.

The fence is empty when he looks round to argue.

---

On the day of the Fair, the whole village waits on the Landing Steps for the barge.

The barge with Fenna's family on it.

It is late.

A squall comes over, a short sharp rain, and everyone gets wet. The food on the tables up in the square goes cold. Fenna stands at the top of the steps and wants very badly to go and bake something, just to have something in her hands.

"No," says Maud, and takes her by the elbow, and holds on.

They wait.

Then the barge comes round the bend.

It is Mei's. *Sooner*, it says on the side, in big white letters. Mei is at the tiller, and on the deck there are people, standing in a row, holding onto the rail and on to each other.

The barge ties up. The plank goes down. The family climbs the steps.

Fenna's little sister comes first, counting the steps out loud. "Eighty-nine, ninety, ninety-*one*." She looks back. "Ninety-one steps!"

"Ninety-*two*," says Fenna's little brother, behind her. "You missed one."

"I did NOT."

They are so big. They were two and five when she last saw them every day. Now they are long-legged and loud and arguing, and they run straight past Fenna to look at the pony.

Then her mother. Then her father.

Maud steps forward and puts a bowl of hot soup into Fenna's father's hands. She holds it there with both of her own, so that he cannot give it back.

He cannot. He stands there holding it.

Then he hands Fenna something, under one arm. It is the tent. Her tent. The one she borrowed, the one that smelled of horse, the one she sent back on the barge last spring when she had a roof. It is folded very neatly.

"You patched it," he says.

That is the whole of his speech.

Then he goes and stands by the pony, with his hand on her neck, the way he used to stand by Cobble. And the pony, who poses for everyone, who has posed for the entire village, every single day, stands very still under his hand, and does not pose at all.

Fenna's mother takes Fenna's face in both her hands. She does not say anything. She does not need to. She just looks for a long time, as if she is counting something too.

And last off the barge, last of all, comes Liv.

She is tall and thin, in a grey coat that is too short at the wrists. She has a long pole on her back, a lamplighter's pole, with a little brass cap on the end for snuffing out flames. And in one fist she is holding a flat grey stone.

She stops at the top of the steps.

Maud puts a bowl into her hands too.

---

At the Fair, Mayor Juno wants the Cloth Table cleared, to make room for the pies.

Elder Agnes, who is very old and very small and sits at the end of the Cloth Table in a chair with arms, will not let one cloth on it be moved.

It is a folded cloth, plain and grey, with a pattern of tiny moons along the edge. It has been laid on that table for Moonedge at every single Fair since the fire. Seven Fairs. The stool at the head of it is for nobody. Nobody sits there. Nobody has, ever.

Fenna looks at the old folded cloth for a long time.

Then she goes and gets her own tablecloth, the blue one she dyed herself in the vat, and lays it over the top of the old one. And sets out the plates.

When all the tables are full, Ansel the bell-ringer rings the Hall bell three times, for the feast.

And from nowhere, from somewhere very small and very close, other bells answer.

Two people hear them. Ansel, whose right ear always catches small bells. And Fenna.

Neither of them says so.

---

Elder Agnes sprinkles thyme over Fenna's bread like somebody dealing cards.

"Your grandmother put this in everything," says Agnes. "She had a saying. *If in doubt, thyme.*"

Fenna waits for the rest of the saying.

There is no rest. That was the whole saying.

Somehow it is enough to keep.

---

Fenna is setting the tables in silence, because she is busy.

The whole village is humming while it sets its own tables.

The tune is hers.

Rosa has it, on Gertrude. Greenstem has it, wrong. Pieter has it, whistled. The bakers have it. The twins have it. The tune has gone round the whole of Hazelwood like the Soup Stone, from her, through the cider and the still-room and the gate and the Hall, and come back.

She stops humming.

Everyone looks at her.

She starts again.

She knows exactly where they got it.

---

That night the Wagon Players are in the square, the travelling theatre, with their painted cart and a stage that folds out of the side of it. They are doing the Ballad of Moonedge.

Fenna wants to leave.

But everybody is watching. If she leaves now, she will be the girl who left in the middle of the Ballad of Moonedge. She sits down on the end of a bench and stays.

Lil Thunder, the Players' sound girl, shakes a big tin sheet for the lightning, and every child in the square shouts "BOOM!" Linus shouts it first, and loudest, and twice.

In the Ballad there is a farm on a hill, and a storm. Lightning strikes the barn. And the hero of the Ballad is a little girl, a child, who runs out of the burning barn shouting "*The lights! The lights!*" to wake up the house and save everyone.

The whole village sings along. They know every word.

Fenna watches to the end.

At the back of the crowd, a grey coat has gone very still.

---

The Ballad ends. The village claps.

Then, at the back of the crowd, somebody stands up.

It is Liv.

She does not have the pole. She has put it down. She is just standing there, very thin, in her short grey coat, with her hands at her sides. One of her hands is a fist.

"It wasn't lightning," says Liv.

The tin sheet stops wobbling.

Liv is not looking at the stage. She is looking at Fenna. Everyone turns round to see who she is looking at.

"You were in the barn that night," says Liv. "I saw you. I saw you run out. Crying about lights."

The whole square goes quiet.

Liv's hands are shaking. In one of them there is a flat grey stone.

---

Fenna wants to run.

She has the bucket in her hand. Of course she has. It is the only thing she has ever run with.

And she does not run.

She puts the bucket down in the dust, in front of everybody. It is the first time she has ever put it down in front of anyone.

Then she sits on the nearest bench, with nothing in her hands.

Her hands are very cold.

Liv comes and stands over her.

"Where were you?" says Liv.

"In the barn."

"Doing what?"

"Playing." Fenna looks at the bucket in the dust. "I took the bucket in with me. Nobody asks a child with a bucket where she's going."

"Who with?"

"I'm not going to say." Her voice is very small. "Not here."

From somewhere in the crowd, Linus, under his breath: "I *told* you."

Lotte kicks him.

"And the lantern?" says Liv.

Fenna is quiet for a long time.

"It was on the hook," she says. "Outside. Like always. Never past the sill. And then it was in the hay." She swallows. "I think I carried it. Maybe it was the lights. I don't know."

Nobody moves.

"It went up," says Fenna, "as if it had been waiting. I had the bucket. The bucket was empty. I hit the fire with it anyway. Then I ran. I ran out shouting about the lights." She looks up at Liv. "Nobody believed me. So I stopped saying it."

---

Liv sits down on the bench.

Not close. But on the same bench.

"I saw the lights too," says Liv.

Very quietly.

"I never told anyone."

The square is so quiet that Fenna can hear the flags.

"I saw them from the loft," says Liv. "Lots of summers. Little lights in the gaps of the barn wall. I thought—" She stops. "Then that night I saw you run out with the bucket. And I thought you were running away with it. With the water. I thought you took the water and ran."

"It was empty," says Fenna.

"I know," says Liv. "I know that now."

---

Somebody passes Fenna a cup.

It is Maud. Maud's cup is the first.

Then another cup comes, from the next person along. And another, from behind. Cups come along the bench from both sides, hand to hand, like the Chain, until there are more cups than Fenna can hold, and Greenstem has to hold some.

Fenna's father gets up from where he is sitting. He walks the whole length of the long table, slowly, past everyone, and sits down on the bench on Fenna's other side. He puts his hands on his knees. He does not say anything.

At the back of the crowd, by the Players' cart, Lark is standing very still.

He does not make a joke. He does not say anything at all.

Fenna's mother comes and kneels down in the dust in front of the bench. She puts her hands on Fenna's cold hands.

"You were a child," she says.

Plainly. Like that. Like something everybody should have known.

Fenna does not answer.

She cannot.

---

Mistress Appleby walks to the bakery, which is on the square, and comes back with her chalk board. The big one, from the wall behind her counter, the one the tollman checks. Every loaf for seven years. Every thin chalk moon.

She stands it on the Pie Table where everyone can see.

"Who wants to wipe it?" she says.

Nobody moves. Wiping the Mark is not allowed. The Mark stands until the farm gives again. That is what the notice says. And the Lord has not said anything. The Lord's new paper has not even come yet. It might say *a half*. It might say anything.

"The farm gave," says Maud. "It gave soup. It gave bread. It gave the stream back. It gave my goat somewhere to go."

"That's not what the notice means," says Juno, unhappily, holding her scroll.

"Then the notice is wrong," says Appleby.

She wipes the first board herself, with her apron. One long sweep. Seven years of thin chalk moons gone in a smear.

Then every other board in Hazelwood follows. The miller's. The grocer's. The Landing's. Children run to fetch them. Everybody wipes. Somebody wipes the chalk off the mill gate with a wet sock.

The children want to know what to skip to now, if not the chalk rhyme. Nobody knows. They make one up. It is about a goat.

Master Quill arrives in the middle of all this, holding a folded paper. He looks at the wiped boards.

"I wrote the Lord's new paper last night," he says. "You are all two hours early."

Nobody thanks Fenna. Nobody needs to. They pass her another cup. They had risked it.

---

Quill wants to write his count.

His real count. The one in the third column, in pencil, down the edge of every page. The one he has been keeping for years, it turns out, about every farm in the valley, and has never shown to anyone.

But he finds he cannot do it in pencil. Not in front of the people it is about. Not here.

So he takes out a bottle of ink, which he carries only for the Lord's own letters, and tries to pull the cork.

He cannot get the cork out.

He tries with his fingers. He tries with his teeth, a little, in a dignified way. He tries with the end of his pencil.

Pieter leans over, takes the bottle, pulls the cork out with his teeth, and hands it back.

Quill writes the third column, in ink, on the Cloth Table, in front of everybody, and signs it. He takes out the old lease from Fenna's tin, and beside the young, proud "Q." that he made in the corner as a boy, he writes a second "Q.", steadier.

"I have never written a number in front of the people it was about," he says.

He hands the folded paper to Mayor Juno. Juno reads it aloud.

With Quill's numbers in hand, the Lord has set the share for Moonedge at a tenth. As in the old days, at the very top of the burnt page, before the numbers started to climb.

A tenth.

Nobody cheers, at first. Nobody quite believes it. Then Old Jan cheers, on his own, very loudly, and then everybody does.

---

Afterwards Quill sits on a stool by the Pie Table with a plate of pie on his knee, which he has not been seen to do before, ever.

"He had them all in a drawer," says Quill. To nobody. Dryly. "The Lord. All my reports. Years of them. In a drawer. He only ever read the last line." He takes a bite of pie. "Never the middle."

He chews.

"It took him a night," says Quill.

Then he takes something else out of his coat. A paper, very old and yellow. The Reeve's report. He lays it on the table in front of Fenna, and takes a ruler out of another pocket, and rules a neat straight line through two words on it.

*the girl.*

He initials it.

"Corrected," he says.

"Corrected for what?"

"An error in the Lord's records," says Quill. "It happens." He puts the ruler away. For a moment he looks very nearly pleased with himself.

---

There is an alpaca at the gate.

She is cream-coloured, and very tall, and very polite, with eyelashes as long as Fenna's finger. She has a ribbon round her neck, or half of one. The goat has eaten the rest.

She looks at Fenna for a long time with enormous dark eyes. Then she lifts her head and hums.

One long, strange, low note.

It is the note from the ridge. And it is the note from before that. Fenna's shoulders know it before she does. They go up round her ears.

It is the note that woke the whole farm, that night. The alarm. The note that got everybody out of the house and down the hill, the two little ones and Liv and her parents and Berit and Aunt Tilda's empty bed. Nobody was hurt that night. Nobody at all.

Because of her.

The alpaca stops humming. She looks at Fenna. Then she spits, very politely, on the gatepost, on principle.

And the last line of the rhyme comes back, whole.

"Soft alpaca guards all things."

And then Fenna says the whole thing. All of it. From the very first line, standing at the gate, out loud, to the alpaca, and to the goat on the roof, and to everybody.

*Long ears sow the clover green,*
*Slow shell keeps the water clean.*
*Brave goat clears the bramble bed,*
*Strong pony brings the world its bread.*
*Wise badger heals what aches and stings,*
*Soft alpaca guards all things.*

Her voice does not wobble once.

---

Berit's page on the alpaca is the very last of the animal pages. It is short.

*She hummed at the hay barn seven nights. I moved her to the far paddock. She hummed there too.*

*I was busy.*

That is all it says. It is not signed.

Fenna reads it once. She does not read it again.

---

At dusk the animals line up by the gate, as they have taken to doing. The bunny, the tortoise, the goat, the pony, the badger. In a row, facing the road. With a space at the end of the row that they have never known how to stop leaving.

The alpaca walks slowly down the row, and stands in the space.

The goat, from the nearest roof, looks down at the row, and seems satisfied.

The alpaca looks at Fenna, with enormous patience. She has seen more than she lets on.

She is not telling.

---

The supper is the long table in the square, all the way round, under the lanterns. The whole village. Fenna's family. Liv, at the far end, with her pole leaning on the back of her chair.

Fenna sits down at last.

Nobody needs her to stand. Nobody needs her to fetch anything, or carry anything, or stir anything. She sits.

And then she gets up again, just once, because there is one thing she wants to do.

There is an empty place at the end of the bench. She lays it. A plate. A cup. A spoon. A piece of bread.

Then she looks down the square. And there, sitting on the low wall by the Hall steps, swinging his legs, eating an apple, is Lark.

"There's a place," says Fenna. "Here."

Lark stops swinging his legs.

He looks at the plate, and the cup, and the bread, for a long time.

He opens his mouth, the way he always does, for the joke. It is right there, you can see it, ready to come out.

It does not come.

He gets down off the wall. He walks over. He sits down in the place, carefully, as if it might not hold him. He sits very still. His face has gone quite red, from the collar up, and he is looking at his plate.

"Nobody has ever set a place for me," says Lark.

Just that. Plainly. Not a joke at all.

Then he picks up his spoon, and looks at it, and starts very quietly to eat.

---

The goat eats the blue ribbon off the end of the table.

"Is that a problem?" says Fenna's mother, watching.

"No," says Fenna.

---

Pieter, mortified about something, has set his own lantern down on the ground by the straw bales, where the children are sitting.

THUMPTHUMPTHUMPTHUMPTHUMP.

It is the bunny. From under the table. The full roll, the one Fenna has never heard. The lantern one.

And from the gate, the alpaca hums the long note.

Fenna is up before she knows it. She reaches for the lantern.

A hand is already on it.

It is her father's.

They look at each other over the lantern. Then, together, without a word, they carry it to the post by the Hall door and hang it on the hook there. Outside. Well away from the straw.

Pieter nods once, very red.

Then Liv comes, with her lamplighter's pole. And she reaches up, and snuffs the lantern out, neat as anything, with the little brass cap.

And Fenna takes her bucket, and fills it at the Deep, and puts it on the ground under the hook.

They both look at the pole. They both look at the bucket.

"Straw bales," says Liv. "Really?"

It is very nearly a joke.

The next night, every lantern in the square has a bucket under it.

---

After the pies, Liv wants to see the book.

Fenna does not know how to give it to her. She has never given it to anybody. In the end she just puts it on the table between them and pushes it across, a little at a time, as if it were a cup that might spill.

Liv turns the pages. She does not read the farm parts. She reads the margins.

She finds the hay page. She finds the line at the bottom, in the small round hand.

*Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother. —T.*

And Liv laughs.

It is a big laugh. It comes out of her all at once, from the bottom, like a bucket of marbles tipped down the stairs. And she does not stop quite when she should. She has to put her hand over her mouth. Her eyes are wet.

Somebody passes the bread.

"I used to think the lights were her," says Liv, to the bread. "In the barn. My mother. People say the ones who die can come back as lights."

"They weren't her," says Fenna.

"No." Liv tears the bread in half. "They were somebody's, though."

Fenna does not say anything to that.

---

Somebody starts to sing.

It is the rhyme. The whole village is singing it, all six lines, to Berit's old kitchen song. Maud must have taught them. Or the children did, from the skipping. It goes round the table, and everybody joins in, even the ones who don't know the words.

Fenna knows the tune. It is Berit's. She wants to be useful. She has nothing to carry.

Her own hum slips in, under the song. The other tune, the forest one. It goes in underneath the kitchen song and finds the same notes, and fits there, as if they had always been two halves of the same thing.

She sings.

Lark, who has been sorting all the spoons on the table by how hopeful they look, all through the supper, very seriously, stops.

He lets the spoons lie where they are, in a heap, any old how.

He does not touch them again.

---

At the end of the Fair, Mei stands up on the end of the table and calls the roll, the way she does on Landing Day. Every house in Hazelwood. One by one. And every house shouts back.

"Appleby!" "HERE!"

"Hewitt!" "HERE!"

"Moonedge!"

Old Jan is first, as always. "HERE!"

And Fenna wants to answer. She opens her mouth.

Her voice will not come.

Then it does.

"Here!"

And then her mother. "Here." And her father, very low. "Here." And her sister and brother, one after the other, as loud as they can, competing. And then, from the far end of the table, Liv.

"Here."

Old Jan sits down. He sits down slowly, like a man putting down something very heavy that he has carried for a long way.

At the gate up on the hill, in the dusk, six animals stand in a row, and nobody remarks on it.

---

That night, Fenna turns to the last page in Berit's book.

She has never looked at it. She has always been afraid it would say *The End*.

It is empty. No printing. Nothing about farms.

Only, in the margin, in a hand she knows so well by now:

*Well. Look at that. You read all of it.*

*Go and put the kettle on, my love.*

---

It is late. The square is empty. The Fair is over.

Fenna walks out alone to the old marsh meadow.

The white flowers are open. Hundreds of them, glowing. The ring of thimbles in the middle. The river talking to itself beyond.

The alpaca has followed her. She walks past Fenna and stops, with her back to the flowers, facing out into the dark, towards the path, as if she will spit at anything that comes up it. Up the hill, the goat's one bramble rustles.

Fenna turns her bucket upside down and sits on it, at the edge of the flowers.

She does not look at them.

The bells are quiet.

---

"Mumyo," says Fenna.

The name comes out of her very small, like something put down after a long time carried.

"At the Fair I wouldn't say who I was with. It was you. You laughed at the bucket. You rode on the handle. One of your bells was cracked, and I never told you."

The flowers do not move.

"You made lights. I laughed. And the lantern came in. I don't know whose hands. I'd rather it was mine."

She swallows.

"You tried to blow it out. It got bigger. And I said—"

She stops. She starts again.

"I said it was all your fault. I said, *go away*. I said I never wanted to see you again."

From somewhere in the flowers, close, a single bell.

*Tink.*

"And then I told them it was you," says Fenna. "The grown-ups. That wasn't fair. I'm sorry. And then nobody believed me, and I stopped saying it, and I looked the other way. For years. I looked the other way so I wouldn't find you."

She looks at her hands.

"I never … I wasn't …"

The sentence will not finish. She lets it not finish.

She does not ask him to come out.

*Tink.*

And then, after a long time, two soft rings.

*Ting. Ting.*

She does not know what they mean.

She stands up, and picks up her bucket, and goes home. Behind her, the white flowers close, one by one.

The alpaca's ear turns, once.

---

In the morning, the spare bed has been slept in.

The covers are rumpled. The pillow is at the wrong end. On the pillow, carefully arranged, are all the buttons from the sewing tin, sorted by how shiny they are.

Fenna goes into the kitchen.

Lark is sitting at her table. He is eating porridge. He is sitting down to do it, on a chair, with his elbows off the table, like a very good boy.

"Morning," he says.

"You stayed."

"For breakfast," says Lark. "Only for breakfast. Breakfast is a marvellous idea. You get up, and before you do anything at all, you sit down." He looks at her bowl, on the stove. "Somebody really ought to tell people."

Fenna gets her porridge.

She sits down opposite him, at her own table, and eats it.

Neither of them says anything else, for quite a long time. The porridge is very good. Somebody has put too much salt in it, the way Berit used to.

On the front of Lark's rust-brown coat, there is a new button. It is white, and round, and very slightly glowing, like a little piece of one of the stones from the forest edge.

Nobody mentions it.

---

That evening, Fenna lights the fire in the yard. The first thing she ever lit here, on the first morning.

The spark takes on the first try.

The ash around the fire glows a little, at the edges, as if it remembers something.

She sets her bucket beside the fire, full, as always.

And beside the bucket, someone has set a thimble. A tiny silver thimble, full of water.

Fenna looks at it for a long time.

She does not look round.

She sits down with her back against the fence she built. The cottage with its leaning wall. The barn with its lantern outside. The pond with the tortoise on her stone. The orchard. The mill, turning. The north field, under its clover. The road going down to Hazelwood, where the lights are coming on.

None of this was here when she came.

Now she is sitting in the middle of it.

---

## The Last Evening

The grandchildren are asleep. All of them but Minena.

"Tell me the imp was real," she whispers.

The old woman is quiet for a while.

"Tonight," she says, "I'll start with the fire."

And she says his name. Mumyo. Out loud, to a living person, for the first time in all those years. And then she tells Minena the truth. All of it. The barn and the lights and the bucket and the lantern on its hook. Quietly, the way it happened.

Halfway through, she stops.

"Are you tired?" asks Minena.

"I am not," says the old woman.

She is asleep.

Minena counts to ten, very slowly, on her fingers.

The old woman wakes up.

"Where was I?"

"The fire."

"Ah," says the old woman, and goes on.

---

When she has finished, the room is very quiet.

On the windowsill stands the dented bucket. In it are Minena's stones, and her feathers, and the snail shell, and the button nobody has claimed. And a few flat grey stones that do not match the others at all.

The stones are a little warm.

On the sill outside the window, in the dark, there is a small white flower.

"Did the funny man ever come back?" whispers Minena. "Lark?"

"Every so often," says the old woman. "Usually when I've forgotten to sit down."

"Is he still alive?"

"Oh," says the old woman. "I should think so."

She closes her eyes again. Then she opens one.

"If you ever meet a little fellow with bells," she says, "tell him I was never angry."

Minena nods. Very seriously. As if she were being handed something to carry.

That night she dreams of a melody, going up and turning round, and stopping, and going on.

And in the morning, she hums it.
