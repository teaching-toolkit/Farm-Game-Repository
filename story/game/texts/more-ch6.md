## c6_letters · scene · Tea first
*When:* `seen:c6_lord + after:c6_lord:1`

Page 1. You fold the Lord's letter small, then smaller, and shut it in the drawer, hard. The badger gets up from the hearth, very slowly, and opens the drawer. He leaves the Lord's letter where it is. He takes out the others, the ones from your mother, folded small and pushed to the back.

Page 2. He smooths each one flat on the table and puts a cup on top to hold it down. Six letters. Six cups. Tea first, says his look. Then read.

Page 3. “Come home when you are ready.” “Stay if you must.” “Eat properly. Sit down when you eat.” Every letter asks for a little less than the one before. But every letter is longer. The last one says only: “Ask Maud about the goat.”

## c6_goat_count · scene · The goat that could count
*When:* `seen:c6_letters + after:c6_letters:1`

Page 1. You go down the hill and ask Maud about the goat. “Finally,” says Maud, and puts down her spoon. “Your mother means the old Moonedge goat. The one that could count.”

Page 2. “Every Share Day the Lord's man counted the sacks onto his cart. And that goat stood beside him and counted too. Stamp, stamp, stamp. And every time she stamped one short. On purpose. And he wrote down what the goat said.”

Page 3. Maud's face creases up. “Your mother used to laugh till she had to sit down on the step. She wanted you to know there was laughing up there. Before. Not just the other thing.” She picks up her spoon. “Took you long enough.”

## c6_seal · scene · The goat and the seal
*When:* `seen:c6_goat_count + after:c6_goat_count:2`

Page 1. You want to read the Lord's letter a third time. You go to the drawer. The goat has it. She stands in the middle of the kitchen, chewing, with a small strip of red wax sticking out of her mouth.

Page 2. Master Quill comes to the open door to explain the letter. He finds a goat chewing something red, and you standing very still with your hand out. “I regret,” says Quill, “that I cannot read you what the Lord has written.” A pause. “I did not write it.”

## c6_pot · scene · Flour for the Pie Table
*When:* `done:d_fair_pies`

Page 1. At the Pot, on market day, the village argues about the half. “We know that number,” says Maud, banging her spoon. “We watched that number eat a whole farm.” “Then hide the cheese,” says somebody. “Let Quill count an empty barn.” “And then he counts *us*,” says somebody else.

Page 2. Mayor Juno climbs on a crate. Whatever happens, she says, the Harvest Fair is coming, and the tables need setting. She has practised it all week, out loud, to her goat.

Page 3. Then Mistress Appleby, who has not said a word to Maud since the lease was read, walks across the square and stands beside her. “Maud.” “Appleby.” That is all. Everybody heard. The village chooses the tables.

## c6_ridge · scene · The tall shape
*When:* `seen:c6_broker + after:c6_broker:3`

Page 1. On the ridge above the marsh, in the evenings, there is a tall shape. It stands very still on the skyline, with a long neck and a small head, facing down towards the reeds. It does not come down. It hums one long, low note, and stops.

Page 2. The next evening the goat goes up the ridge to talk it down. The two of them stand nose to nose for a while. The goat comes back down without it, chewing a ribbon.

## c6_fallow · scene · A word for a resting field
*When:* `seen:c6_fair_letter + after:c6_fair_letter:2`

Page 1. Quill wants to count the north field before the Fair. It has rested a whole year under its clover, and the Book has no word for a field that rests. He tries some. “Idle.” Crossed out. “Waiting.” Crossed out. “Asleep.”

Page 2. “Snoozing,” says a voice from the fence. “Lollygagging. Having a little lie-down.” Quill crosses those out too. Then he reads the page of your farm book: Rest the field. Rest yourself. He writes one word: *fallow.* And, very small: *per B.*

Page 3. He counts the bees. He counts the worms in a spadeful of soil, and they are all called Gerald. He counts twice, because he does not believe what his pencil says. The fence is empty when he looks round to argue.

## c6_barge · beat · The barge
*When:* `done:d_fair_cloth` · *Priority:* 95

Page 1. The whole village waits on the Landing Steps for your family's barge. It is late. A squall comes, and everyone gets wet. You want very badly to go and bake something, just to have something in your hands. “No,” says Maud, and holds on to your elbow.

Page 2. Then the barge comes round the bend, grey in the rain. “Who's on the barge?” asks a small child. “Shh!” say six people. Your little sister climbs the steps first, counting. “Ninety-one!” “Ninety-*two*,” says your little brother. “You missed one.” “I did NOT.”

## c6_tent · beat · The tent
*When:* `seen:c6_barge` · *Priority:* 94

Page 1. They are so big now, long-legged and loud. They run straight past you to look at the pony. Maud puts a bowl of hot soup into your father's hands and holds it there with both of hers, so he cannot give it back.

Page 2. Then he hands you something, folded very neatly: your old tent, the one you sent back on the barge. “You patched it,” he says. That is his whole speech. He goes and stands by the pony, and the pony, who poses for everyone, does not pose at all.

Page 3. Your mother takes your face in both hands and just looks. Last off the barge comes Liv, in a grey coat, with a lamplighter's pole on her back and a flat grey stone in one fist. Maud puts a bowl into her hands, too.

## c6_cloth_hum · beat · The cloth with the moons
*When:* `seen:c6_tent` · *Priority:* 93

Page 1. Mayor Juno wants the Cloth Table cleared for the pies. Elder Agnes will not let one cloth be moved: plain and grey, with tiny moons along the edge, laid there for Moonedge at every Fair since the fire. The stool at its head is for nobody.

Page 2. You fetch the blue cloth you dyed yourself and lay it over the old one. Then you set out the plates. Ansel rings the Hall bell three times for the feast. From somewhere very small and very close, other bells answer. Only Ansel hears. And you.

Page 3. The whole village is humming while it sets its tables. The tune is yours. It has gone all round Hazelwood and come back. You stop. Everyone looks at you. You start again. You know exactly where they got it.

## c6_cups · beat · The same bench
*When:* `seen:c6_bend` · *Priority:* 90

Page 1. Liv sits down on the same bench. Not close. “I saw the lights too,” she says, very quietly. “From the loft. I never told anyone. That night I thought you ran off with the water.” “The bucket was empty.” “I know that now.”

Page 2. A small voice in the crowd: “Where was everyone?” “The far field,” somebody answers, low. “Everyone was always at the far field.” Maud's cup comes first. Then more cups, hand to hand along the bench, until Greenstem has to hold some.

Page 3. Your father walks the whole length of the long table and sits down on your other side, hands on his knees. At the back, Lark stands very still and does not make a joke. Your mother kneels in the dust and puts her hands on yours. “You were a child.”

## c6_wipe · beat · Wiping the Mark
*When:* `seen:c6_cups + after:c6_cups:1` · *Priority:* 85

Page 1. Mistress Appleby fetches the big chalk board from behind her counter: seven years of thin chalk moons. She stands it on the Pie Table. “Who wants to wipe it?” Nobody moves. “The farm gave,” says Maud. “Soup. Bread. It gave my goat somewhere to go.”

Page 2. “Then the notice is wrong,” says Appleby, and wipes the board with her apron, one long sweep. Every board in Hazelwood follows. Somebody wipes the mill gate with a wet sock. The children make up a new skipping rhyme. It is about a goat.

Page 3. Master Quill arrives in the middle of it, with a folded paper. He looks at the wiped boards. “I wrote the Lord's new paper last night,” he says. “You are all two hours early.” Nobody thanks you. Nobody needs to. They pass you another cup.

## c6_quill_pie · scene · Corrected
*When:* `seen:c6_quill_answer + after:c6_quill_answer:2`

Page 1. Quill sits on a stool by the Pie Table with a plate of pie on his knee, which nobody has ever seen him do. “He had them all in a drawer,” he says, dryly. “The Lord. Years of my reports. He only ever read the last line.” He takes a bite. “It took him a night.”

Page 2. Then he lays an old yellow paper in front of you, takes out a ruler, and rules a neat straight line through two words on it. He initials it. “Corrected.” “Corrected for what?” “An error in the Lord's records. It happens.” He looks very nearly pleased.

## c6_row · scene · The last page on the alpaca
*When:* `seen:c6_alpaca + after:c6_alpaca:2`

Page 1. Berit's page on the alpaca is the very last of the animal pages. It is short. “She hummed at the hay barn seven nights. I moved her to the far paddock. She hummed there too. I was busy.” That is all it says. You read it once. You do not read it again.

Page 2. At dusk the animals line up by the gate, as they have taken to doing: bunny, tortoise, goat, pony, badger. There is a space at the end of the row that they have never known how to stop leaving. The alpaca walks slowly down the row and stands in it.

## c6_lantern · beat · A bucket under the hook
*When:* `seen:c6_supper + after:c6_supper:2` · *Priority:* 75

Page 1. Pieter, mortified about something, has set his lantern down by the straw bales, where the children sit. THUMPTHUMPTHUMPTHUMP, from under the table: the bunny, the lantern roll. From the gate, the alpaca hums her long note. You are up before you know it. A hand is already on the lantern.

Page 2. The hand is your father's. Without a word, you carry the lantern together to the post by the Hall door and hang it on the hook, outside, well away from the straw. Liv snuffs it out with her brass cap. You fill your bucket and set it under the hook.

Page 3. “Straw bales,” says Liv. “Really?” It is very nearly a joke. The next night, every lantern in the square has a bucket under it.

## c6_liv_book · scene · The margins
*When:* `seen:c6_lantern + after:c6_lantern:2`

Page 1. After the pies, Liv wants to see the book. You do not know how to give it to her. In the end you push it across the table a little at a time, like a cup that might spill. She does not read the farm parts. She reads the margins.

Page 2. She finds the hay page, and the line at the bottom in a small round hand: “Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother. —T.” And Liv laughs. A big laugh, all at once, like a bucket of marbles tipped down the stairs. Her eyes are wet.

Page 3. “I used to think the lights were her,” says Liv, to the bread. “My mother. People say the ones who die can come back as lights.” “They weren't her.” “No.” Liv tears the bread in half. “They were somebody's, though.”

## c6_roll · beat · Here
*When:* `seen:c6_liv_book + after:c6_liv_book:2` · *Priority:* 78

Page 1. At the end of the Fair, Mei stands on the end of the table and calls the roll, the way she does on Landing Day. Every house in Hazelwood, one by one, and every house shouts back. “Appleby!” “HERE!” “Hewitt!” “HERE!” “Moonedge!” Old Jan is first, as always. “HERE!”

Page 2. You open your mouth. Your voice will not come. Then it does. “Here!” Then your mother. “Here.” Your father, very low. Your sister and brother, as loud as they can. Then, from the far end of the table, Liv. “Here.” Old Jan sits down slowly, like somebody putting down something very heavy.

## c6_eve · moment · The fire, again
*When:* `seen:c6_ajar + after:c6_ajar:2` · *Register:* W→A · *Move:* Echo · *Priority:* 80

Page 1. That evening you light the fire in the yard, the first thing you ever lit here. The spark takes on the first try. The ash around it glows a little at the edges, as if it remembers something. You set your bucket beside the fire, full, as always.

Page 2. And beside the bucket someone has set a tiny silver thimble, full of water. You look at it for a long time. You do not look round.

Page 3. You sit with your back to the fence you built. The cottage with its leaning wall. The barn with its lantern outside. The windmill, turning. The road down to Hazelwood, where the lights are coming on. None of this was here when you came. Now you are in the middle of it.
