# The farm that burned down: the story, told

A read-through of the whole story as prose, written 24 Oct 2026 from the master outline and the story bible. It leaves out the game: no triggers, no ids, no "the player". It is a retelling, not canon; where it differs from `story-bible.md`, the bible wins. Things the outline still marks as proposed are told here as settled. **It spoils everything**, the ending included.

The farm is called Moonedge. The village below it is Hazelwood. The girl is Fenna; in the game she is just "you".

---

## Before the first page

Somewhere, a very old woman lies in bed, and on the rug beside her lie her grandchildren, who have been told to go to sleep and have no intention of doing so.

"Tell us about the farm that burned down," says one of them.

She will. She will tell it over seven evenings, in pieces, with a great deal of interrupting. She hums while she tells it, a few slow notes, and doesn't notice she is doing it. The smallest grandchild, Minena, notices. Minena notices everything and says about a third of it.

There is one thing the old woman says before every telling. *I never start with the fire.*

So she starts with the ash.

## Chapter 1: Ashes

Moonedge was not much to look at. It had been a farm once, with a barn and a well and a long field running down to the road. Now it had ash, mud, and a chimney that had not been told the house was gone and so stood there anyway.

Fenna arrived on foot. She was fifteen. She carried a box that had belonged to her grandmother Berit, who had died the winter before, and inside the box were three things: a pot, a dented bucket and a book. On the first page of the book, in Berit's cramped handwriting, it said *Start small. Small is plenty.* Further on, in the margin of a page about animals, it said *Goats lie.* Fenna did not yet know what that was about. She would.

Somewhere in the ash she had found one grain of wheat. She meant to make it grow.

She did not say why. She did not say much about why, then or later, because Fenna had worked out a method, and the method was to stay so busy that nobody could ask her anything. She cleared weeds. She cleared more weeds. She built a fire, and then a smaller fire, because the first one looked ambitious. She ate her porridge standing up, which she would go on doing for a very long time.

That first night she sat in the cold and hummed a few notes she could not have named, and the old hearthstones glowed back at her. Not much. A little. Like something clearing its throat. She stared for a long while. Then she got up and tidied something.

Strange things began to happen around the farm, all of them small. A bell rang in the dark, high and tinny, where there was no bell. A pile of stones appeared by the fence, arranged with great care and no sense at all. And every time she lit a fire, a bucket of water was standing beside it. She had not put it there. She moved it. It came back.

The village, down the hill, was polite in the way of people who have decided to wait and see. Maud, who kept goats and kept to herself, was kind to her, and had apparently been told to be, by Berit, years ago. A woman called Rue, who sold things and ate standing up (Fenna respected this), became her first customer. A small boy at the fence told her, at some length, what the village was afraid of about her family. Old Jan said nothing, and said it very clearly. And somebody had been laying river stones along the burnt gatepost by the road, in a neat row, and nobody could say who.

Then she found a coin in an old coat. On one side was a man's face, and under the face it said WEXLEY, which was the Lord's name. In the ruined workshop she found a ledger with a scorched page, and on the page a column of numbers: what the Lord had taken from Moonedge, year by year. It climbed. She read it twice, put it back, and went and carried a rock.

The rocks were the trouble. She cleared a heap of them from the corner of the yard and found, under the heap, a tiny tower someone had built, and she tidied it. Of course she did. That night there were no bells. She lay awake and listened and heard nothing, and it was a different nothing from before. It lasted. It was still there the next day, and the next. Whoever had been ringing that bell had stopped, and she had stopped them, and she did not know who they were.

Winter came, and in the snow there were footprints. They went to a green patch in the middle of the ruin where nothing should have been alive. Something small had been keeping it alive. Something with the wrong feet for a bell-ringer.

She made it a hutch out of straw. She put out a bowl. She began, without admitting it, to wait.

On the last night of the chapter the bunny came home. She was grey and enormously suspicious, and she had been guarding that patch for seven years, one nibbled clover at a time, so that when the girl came back there would be something green to come back to. The bunny sat in the door of the hutch, looked at the bowl, looked at Fenna, and decided the bowl would do.

Somewhere out in the dark, two soft rings of a bell.

*Was there really a bunny?* asks a grandchild.

*There was,* says the old woman. *She was rude about carrots.*

## Chapter 2: Homestead

Fenna wanted a roof. She wanted it before winter, and she had given herself the deadline, because her mother's letters had been very careful not to.

The letters came up the river by barge. They were soft and short and full of things that were not being said. *We are well. Your brother asks about the horse.* Now and then a word in brackets, a thing her mother was not sure she should write. They never asked her to come home. They never asked her to stay. They asked, and then asked a bit less, and Fenna read every one as if it were a demand note.

In Hazelwood she found out that Old Jan had been answering "Here!" for Moonedge. Every other week, on Landing Day, the village called out the names of its farms, and somebody had to say "Here!" or the name would be crossed off. For seven years, Jan had said it, loudly, for a farm with nobody on it. He said it the way he said everything, as if it were a rude suggestion.

She learned something else about Hazelwood. The loaves in the bakery window each had a mark on them in chalk: a thin moon. That was the Moonedge Mark. It went on every loaf, sack and crossing "for the farm that burned", and the board in the square said it would stay until the farm gave again. Nobody had ever said what *gives again* meant. Appleby the baker, a woman with the forearms of someone who has been right for forty years, chalked Fenna's first loaf with a double moon and held her eye while she did it. The next day a sweet bun turned up in Fenna's bag. Nobody mentioned it.

The roof was hard. Greenstem and Hilde came up the hill to help, mostly because Hilde had decided to, and the building went faster than it had any right to. A wanderer called Lark turned up in a rust-coloured coat with buttons that did not match, and told her the date, with great confidence, and it was wrong. "Probably fine," he said. Lark was practically immortal, which made his dates a bit loose. He sorted her spoons into rows and left.

On the morning the last thatch went on, she found a hammer on the roof. It was the size of her thumb. She picked it up and turned it over. It was a perfectly good hammer, with a perfectly good handle, and someone had been using it. For the first time since she arrived she did not take something apart to see how it worked. She put it on a shelf.

A long way off, in a book, the Lord's steward wrote a line: *a roof, at Moonedge.* She did not know that yet.

And then the barn.

The village said lightning. They said it the way they said most things about Moonedge: not unkindly, not quite kindly, the way you'd say "rain". But when she poked through the ashes of the barn she saw the black hook beside the door, and the burn pattern, and it did not look like lightning to her. Lightning comes from above. This had started low. In the hay, in the corner, close to the ground.

Berit's book had a rule in the margin, underlined twice: *Lantern hangs outside. Never past the sill.* And beside it, in a rounder hand: *Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother. -T.* Whoever T. was, she had been told this often.

Fenna asked Old Jan about it. He opened his mouth, and the words that came out were "past the sill", and then he coughed, and went on coughing, and would not say another word. From the cough Fenna worked out, without being told, that T. was someone who was gone.

The tortoise came home in the last week of the chapter. Not to the house. To the pond, which had been dry since before the fire and was dry now, and which had a little worn seat in it where something had sat for years waiting for the water to come back. The tortoise went to the seat. She sat on it. She looked at Fenna with the expression of somebody who has been very patient and expects this to be noticed.

*Did you find out who started the fire?* asks a grandchild.

*Not then,* says the old woman. *I didn't want to.*

## Chapter 3: Smallholding

Her mother's next letter said *Stay if you must.* Then, as if it were an afterthought: *Your cousin asks after you.*

Fenna read it eleven times. She knew exactly who the cousin was. Liv had been twelve the night of the fire, and had been standing right there, and had told the grown-ups what she saw. Now she was asking after Fenna, and the only reason Fenna could think of was to find out whether Fenna was still there.

She worked twice as hard. This did not help, but it was familiar.

A goat was coming. The signs were all over the farm. First a corner of her to-do list went missing, with bite marks. Then a chair leg. Then the plans for the new field, all of them, eaten in the night, and the goat looked at her afterwards with the calm of somebody who has improved things. The goat was brown and had a great many opinions. Berit's margin had been right.

The first Share Day arrived. This was the Lord's tenth, collected at the gate by a quiet man named Pieter with a lantern, who counted sacks and said as little as possible. The village made thin soup for the occasion, because thin was all there was, and Pieter ate his bowl standing up. Somebody had put out a stool for him, and he sat on the ground beside it instead. Nobody said anything. Tucked into the salt page of Berit's book was a card in her cramped hand: *Never so much that the table is bare.* Fenna did not know what it was for. She kept it.

The village leaned in, as far as it could. The miller, Hob, who blamed Moonedge for the silt that had stopped his wheel, watched her dig out the pond and the stream run again and his wheel turn. He nodded once. That nod was the whole of his apology, and it cost him: a mill that runs is a mill the Lord's men tax. At a cider supper somebody played a tune she had hummed once without knowing, and a girl called Rosa played it wrong all night, with great joy.

Under the old bell in the mill she found a drawing. It was done in charcoal, crude, in a child's hand. It showed a girl with a bucket, and next to her, tiny, something with a lot of little bells on it. They were holding hands.

So the bell-ringer was real. And the drawing said, as plainly as a drawing can, that they had been friends. Fenna looked at it for a long time. Then she found something to do with a bucket, quickly, somewhere else.

Bucket Night came in summer. It was a village custom: everyone brought a bucket, and they stood in a line from the river to the dry places and passed the water hand to hand. This year the Chain was longer than Maud could remember. Someone in a grey coat was seen at the far end, and then wasn't, and by morning had become a legend. Buckets began to appear at every corner of Moonedge, set down there by nobody. That was how the fear lived now: not as a thing anyone said, but as a habit of the farm.

In autumn the Grey Breath came over the river, a fog the colour of an old coin, and in it the grey barges that carried the village's money away down the river, to someone the Lord himself had to answer to. Nobody named him. A small hand slipped into Fenna's in the fog. She didn't look down.

The goat came home properly in the end. She ate thistles that nobody had been able to clear in ten years, and Fenna, who had spent three chapters defending her lists, stood in the field and let her. It was the first time she had let anything go.

A letter came through the gate, in a hand she didn't know. Someone wanted to visit. She made up the guest bed that evening. She made it up again the next day. She made it up a third time. It is easier to prepare for someone than to meet them.

*Were you scared of your cousin?* asks a grandchild.

*More than of any imp,* says the old woman.

## Chapter 4: Village Trade

The man who came to count was thin and tidy and carried a pencil he never seemed to sharpen. His name was Master Quill. He was the Lord's steward, and he had come to find out why Moonedge, now that it had a roof and a field and a goat, was paying so little.

He counted the sacks. He counted the hens. He counted, with great seriousness, Old Jan's hen, who had already been counted by Jan, and said so. The pencil never ran. Ink runs; pencil doesn't, and Quill had a private number in his head that no pencil would agree to.

On Landing Day, for the first time, Fenna said "Here!" herself. Out loud, in the square, with everybody listening. Jan beamed like a lantern. She was shaking so hard she nearly dropped the name board. And in the jar by the gate that same afternoon was a note: *Have you still got the bucket? -L.* She read it, and shoved the bucket behind a barrel, and went on with her day, and the whole village could see exactly what she had done.

Someone had seen her that night, with the bucket. And whoever it was, they were afraid of her, too. That was the thing she hadn't thought of.

On Share Day Quill stood at the gate and read the lease aloud. It was long and dull and formal, and in the middle of it there was a number. *A half.* The Lord claimed half of everything the farm made. The village had always known it, the way you know a thing you've decided not to look at. But it had never heard it said. By the end of the reading half the square was angry at the Lord and the other half was angry at Moonedge, for making the Lord notice. Maud and Appleby, who had been friends for thirty years, did not speak to each other for a week.

Late that night Quill took a small tin from his coat. In it was a copy of the lease, and in the corner, small as a speck, a single letter. *Q.* He had written out that half himself, years before, as a junior clerk, copying what he was told. He didn't say so. He put the tin away.

Then there was the pony. She came home after the harvest, big and glossy and pleased with herself, and she posed before every load. She arranged her mane. She waited for the audience. She was magnificent, she knew it, and she would carry your flour to market anyway if you asked nicely. That autumn, for the first time, Moonedge had enough to sell. The pony hauled the first big order down the hill with all of Hazelwood watching, and Fenna walked beside her, and was seen.

That week Quill asked to borrow the pony for the Lord. Fenna said no. It was the first time she had said no to anything. Quill wrote *Declined (pony)* in his book, and looked, to her surprise, relieved.

On the Wall, on a clear winter night, Pieter showed her Moonedge from the outside. A single lit window on a dark hill. A small light over the marsh that might have been a star. She had never seen it from there. She thought: somebody lives there. She was surprised it was her.

Quill rode out the next morning. At the gate he turned. "A farm like this," he said, "gives back more than it takes." He said it like a question. Then he went.

*Was the Lord a villain?* asks a grandchild.

*He was afraid,* says the old woman. *That's worse, sometimes.*

## Chapter 5: Healing

The cough began with the children.

It went round Hazelwood in the autumn, house to house, small to big, and the village healer, Wren, who was exact and grumpy and had never in her life asked anybody for help, went round after it with a basket. Fenna did what she always did. She made medicine. She made it herself, from Berit's book, from the herbs in the old still-room, and she did not sleep.

Quill came once more that autumn, just once, and he looked unwell. He had a report with him. He wasn't going to read it aloud. He left it open on the table, and she read it upside down: a list of names from the year of the fire. *A girl, eight. A witness, twelve.* She read it twice. Then he put his hat on and went away, and did not come back until the Fair.

Behind a shelf in the still-room she found a page that had been torn from Berit's book. It was about the badger. He had been the farm's healer, and he had left in the winter after the fever. The page was clear. He had asked that the sick children be allowed to rest. Nobody had listened. He had gone. And near the bottom, in Berit's hand: *The girl was one of them.*

The girl was her.

A letter came, in a round hand, from Weir. It was from Liv. *Honey. Thyme. Boil the water a second time. I am not asking anything.* Fenna folded it small, put it in her apron, and did not answer. She just kept it there.

She went on. She made more medicine, and made it faster. She stopped eating, and then she stopped noticing, and one afternoon in the still-room she looked at the bucket, and it was empty. "The bucket's empty," she said, to nobody. She sat down on the floor and did not get up again.

Wren found her. Wren filled the bucket without a word, and asked nothing.

By morning half the village had come. Fourteen bowls of soup climbed the road to Moonedge, one at a time. The goat ate a doily. That same morning Wren found the letter in the apron and made the syrup exactly as Liv had written it. Flowers appeared on the doorstep, two mornings in a row, and nobody had put them there.

In the forest, one grey day, she saw a white spiral between the trees, a shell as tall as a house, quiet. She did not go any closer. She did not need to. Lark was there, as he always seemed to be, sorting pine cones, and he named the tune she'd been humming. "Mr. Mumble," he said, and then, "Yours." He seemed to think that was a joke, and she did not know why it wasn't.

The badger came home last. He arrived in the middle of a cold night, looked at her, and made her sit down. Then he made tea. It took an hour. He would not hurry. Tea first, said his whole face, before anything else. She had a pile of work to do, and she sat there with her hands round a cup, and for the first time in years she asked for help. Out loud, in words.

Pieter, meanwhile, had done a quiet thing. In cough week, with the collector at the gate and two numbers on his list, he took the smaller one. Nobody was supposed to know. His job depended on it.

At the end of the week a boy brought a letter up to Moonedge. It had the Lord's crooked seal. It was not in Quill's hand.

*Did you get better?* asks a grandchild.

*Badgers make very slow tea,* says the old woman. *By the time it was ready, I was.*

## Chapter 6: Master Farm

The Lord's letter said a half.

It said it plainly, and it said it would start from the next Share Day, "as before". Fenna read it with her whole body. Then the goat ate it. The goat had been waiting for an excuse.

At the Pot, the village's shared soup in the square, the argument was loud and long. In the end, to everyone's surprise, the village decided it would rather keep its tables full than keep its head down. Appleby brought flour. Maud put in a spoon. And Quill, who had come to see how the letter was taken, said quietly: "I did not write it."

Below the farm, Fenna drained the river meadow, and in the evening white flowers opened there. There was a ring of thimbles laid in the grass. Faint bells, very soft. Nobody came. She stood very still, and she did not tidy anything.

The alpaca, meanwhile, stood on the ridge facing the reeds, and would not come down. She hummed one long note. It carried.

Then came the Fair. It had no date; it was called when the tables were full, and now they were. Quill wrote *fallow, per B.* in the Lord's own book, which was Berit's word, from the dead. Before he counted the plates, he asked for the names of the people who would eat from them.

The family came up the Landing steps. Fenna's little sister, taller than anybody remembered. Her brother, who ran straight for the animals. Her mother. Her father, thinner, holding a folded tent. Maud put a bowl in his hands before he could say anything. He took it. Then he gave Fenna the tent. "You patched it," he said, and that was all he said, and she held it for a long time.

Liv came up last. She had a long pole across her back, the lamplighter's, and a grey stone in her fist. She was too tall for her coat. She stopped at the top of the steps and looked at Fenna and did not smile, and did not not-smile.

In the square the long table stood where it had stood at every Fair since the fire, with a stool at the head that was for no one. Fenna laid her cloth over the old one. Then everybody began to hum. It was her tune, the one she'd always hummed. She stopped, and everyone looked at her. She started again.

In the evening the Players arrived with a ballad about the fire. It had a hero in it, a child, who ran out of the barn shouting "The lights! The lights!" The village joined in. They had been singing it for years. At the back, a grey coat went very still.

Liv stood up. It was quiet enough that the pole creaked.

"It wasn't lightning," she said. "You were in the barn that night."

There was no way out of it, and nothing to tidy.

Fenna sat down. She told it, in her own words, to the whole square, up to the place she could not say. She said there had been a game. She said there had been a lantern. She said she had run out with the bucket, and it had been empty. She did not say who she had been playing with.

When she finished, Liv said, very quietly: "I saw the lights too. I never told anyone."

Fenna's mother said, across the table, "You were a child." Fenna did not answer. She did not need to.

The next morning, before any paper reached the square, the village wiped the chalk. Appleby did the first board herself. Quill arrived two hours later and found it done. "You are," he said, "two hours early." He had come to sign a number in ink: a tenth, witnessed, for as long as the farm stood. While he wrote he told her about the drawer. The Lord kept the grey letters in a drawer, and he had read the last line of each one and never the middle. Then Quill took a ruler, drew a line through a word on his own paper, and wrote *Corrected.*

The alpaca came down from the ridge that afternoon. She walked straight into the yard, past the pony, past the goat, looked at the house, and made one polite noise, the one she had made once before, on the night of the fire, which had woken the whole farm. The rhyme was whole.

That night they all sat down to supper. Lark was given a place laid for him, which unsettled him a great deal. The goat ate a ribbon. When somebody went to hang the lantern on its hook outside, by the bales, there was already a hand on it. It was her father's. Liv, with her pole, put the light out. She set a bucket under the hook. The next night, every lantern in the square hung outside.

Afterwards Liv read Berit's margin notes aloud. *Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother.* She stopped. "I used to think the lights were her," she said. "My mother."

"They weren't," said Fenna.

"They were somebody's, though," said Liv.

Then Fenna went out to the garden alone, in the dark, among the thimbles and the white flowers, and told the rest.

"Mumyo," she said. It was the first time she had said it in seven years.

She said he had been her friend. She said she had told him to go away. She said she had told them it was him. She said it quietly to the dark, and she didn't ask for anything.

There was a small sound, a *tink*, and then two soft rings of a bell. And beside the bucket, in the grass, there was a thimble that had not been there before.

## The last evening

The old woman has stopped. The grandchildren are very quiet. Even Minena has stopped asking things.

"Tell me the imp was real," says Minena.

"Tonight," says the old woman, "I'll start with the fire."

She says his name. She says it to a living person, out loud, for the first time. Then she begins to tell it, and halfway through a sentence, in the middle of the barn, she falls asleep.

Minena counts to ten. She counts slowly. She is very fair about it.

"Where was I?" says the old woman.

"The fire."

"Ah."

On the windowsill there is a bucket, and in the bucket a handful of Minena's stones. A feather. A few flat grey stones that do not match the others, and that nobody remembers adding. And a white flower, in a place where nothing has grown for years.

The old woman cannot say the last thing herself. She tried once, long ago, and found it wasn't hers to say. So she says it to the child.

"If you meet a little fellow with bells," she says, "tell him I was never angry."

That night Minena dreams a melody. It is a new one. It belongs to a different story.

## What the book never says

Four things stay unsaid, on purpose.

- **Whose hands carried the lantern in.** Hers? His? Both? The book never decides.
- **What Berit felt.** The only trace is a note beside the alpaca's page: *I moved her to the far paddock. I was busy.*
- **What the Count is.** The barges are grey. The Lord answers to someone above him. That is as far as it goes.
- **Whether Mumyo forgives her.** There is a sound, and there is a thimble. The rest is up to you.

## The truth under the story

You could read the six chapters and not know it. This is what happened the night of the fire, as the story's architects have it.

Fenna was a lonely child on a farm where every grown-up was busy. The Lord's share had risen so high that every hand was in the far field, and the well had gone dry. Her only playmate was Mumyo, a hand-sized imp with white hair and a vest of tiny bells, who could not speak and made small lights.

They were playing in the hay barn when a lantern that should have hung outside by the door, by Berit's rule, was suddenly inside. Nobody knows whose hands brought it in. The hay caught. Fenna ran out with the family's old bucket, empty because the well was dry. Her last words to Mumyo were *"This is all your fault! Go away! I never want to see you again!"* She told the grown-ups it was him. Liv, from the loft window, saw the lights, and thought they were her dead mother, and saw Fenna running out with the bucket. She told the authorities the second thing and kept the first to herself for seven years.

Nobody ordered the fire. It came from many small ordinary things at once: the share, a warning animal moved away, a child left alone, a lantern that came in. That is why the book is not about who started it. It is about a girl who did nothing but work so that no one would ask, and about learning to stop, sit down, and tell it.
