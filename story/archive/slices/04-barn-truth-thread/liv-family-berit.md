## 1.2 What she saw, did and kept 🔒

| Thing | What happened |
|---|---|
| The lights | From the loft window, on many evenings that summer, small lights in the barn gaps. She watched them every night. She thought they were her mother (Tilda was gone; the folk-belief says the dead may come as lights). She told nobody. |
| The night | The lights leapt; a flare; **Fenna ran out with the bucket in her hand**, sooty, crying "the lights!" and dropped it at the door. Liv carried the two-year-old down the ladder and dragged the five-year-old after. (**She got the little ones out.** Nobody thanked her.) |

## 1.3 Why she stands up at the Fair 💡

The Players give the Ballad: the child-hero runs out shouting "the lights!" and the tin sheet shakes for lightning. To Liv **the one private truth she kept is being turned into the weather in front of her family.** She stands to stop the lie. It comes out as an accusation because the half she can say is the half about Fenna. She did not come to hurt; she came to make the lie stop and to hear Fenna say it.

## 1.5 What she gets and gives up

She gets: to be told the lights were real; to hear what the running meant; to see her mother's hand. She gives up: the lights as hers alone; the belief that one person can be blamed. **She keeps the cost:** her mother is still gone, the lights were not her mother, and the bruise (Berit chose the child) is only answered by the margin, where Berit had kept her daughter's cheek.

## 3.1 Why Berit took Fenna 🔒

The father could not look at the child; the mother could not choose between her husband and her child's table; the Reeve's book had a line about the girl and Berit meant to be a hill away from it; and Berit was guilty (she had moved the alpaca, kept the bucket, believed the child, said nothing). She could not give Fenna the truth, so she gave her chores. She could not take two granddaughters (one bed; the mother needed Liv with the little ones).

## 3.2 The funeral and "a while" 💡 (settles 01's X8, X9)

Hard winter. Two neighbours dug the frozen ground. A pagan funeral: a bowl of milk, a ribbon on the old oak, a word to the well. The family came by cart. **Fenna held the horse** (Cobble, a heavy bay) and did not go to the graveside. Liv stood at the tail of the cart and looked at her across the horse's back, meaning to say "I saw the lights too" and saying nothing; the farmer read the look as an accusation. The mother said: "Come to Weir." Fenna said, to the horse's neck, "In a while." The father's hand was on Cobble's neck beside hers; he heard. *"A while" is about the cousin's look, not the table.*

# 5. The father and the siblings

**The father** 💡. A carter in Weir; tired, decent, ashamed. He lit the lantern and left the children; then said "Not a word." He speaks through the horse, the tent, a lantern. His moments: *"Look for the bucket"* (via Rue, 01; it means **I saw you try**; the farmer hears **I know what you did**) · Cobble · *"You patched it."* (the tent, his whole speech) · his hand on the lantern (the supper). 🔒 He needs most to see the farm alive. *(The pencil note and the order are dropped.)*

**The siblings** (unnamed ❓): they barely remember. The drawing in the tent (a very large chicken); at the Fair they run to the animals; during the telling the younger takes the farmer's hand without being asked.
