## 1. Five rites and seven habits a child can repeat

**Rite cap (review F8, manager ruling):** the village has only **five rites**, things that have a fixed form that people say or do the same way: **the Open Bowl (1), the Cup (8), "Here!" (9), Bucket Night (5), Hook outside, hay inside (12)**. Everything else in this file is a habit, a game or a joke, with **no formula and nobody made to**. The Cup is optional; nobody is asked for a true thing; nobody is made to speak. The word "prayer" appears nowhere. Mark in the table: **R** = rite, **h** = habit or game.

Each has: **the rule in the child's words** · what it looks like · why it exists · game hook.

### More customs, shorter

- **Naming Spoon.** A new baby gets a spoon carved by every neighbour who is asked. One spoon per household; a bad spoon is kept longest. B35.
- **Threshold Loaf.** At a wedding the couple break a loaf over the threshold; every guest takes a crumb to put under their pillow. B36.
- **Birthday Bumps.** A birthday child is lifted by the elbows and bumped on the ground once per year of age. After ten, the rest are "by promise".
- **Nut Week.** The first hazelnut of the year is thrown over the Hall roof by the youngest child who can reach; whoever finds it keeps it for luck.
- **Boot-share.** Two children, one pair of boots. No one is teased.
- **Hatching Day eggs.** Dyed with onion skin or beet; the winner of the pancake toss names the next calf.
- **The window opened** when someone dies, so that they can leave. (Berit; nothing more.)
- **Ask the mill.** Whoever wants a secret kept tells it to the miller, who tells no one (the miller is the village's safe).
- **The thank-you rule.** You do not thank the house spirit aloud; a thank-you makes it feel it is leaving. You leave the saucer and say nothing.
- **The Grey Breath.** On the day the Count's grey barge comes, everyone on the Landing Steps holds their breath until it rounds the bend; the children try and fail (E-3.L06). A game, not a rite.

## 6. Superstitions that make jokes 💡

- **Counting hens aloud** is bad luck. **Quill counts hens aloud**, twice (shipped `c4_count`: "He counts the hens twice, because one moved"). The village winces. He does not lose a hen. Later he counts in a whisper (E-4.L01).
- **Whistling in the forest.** Tom whistles. Everybody tells Tom.
- **Spilled salt.** You throw it over your shoulder; the cleaner is Crust the dog.
- **A mug that floats** at Cider Night: "That's the Late Mug, welcoming itself." (Mumyo's floating mugs, shipped `c3_dance`, have an in-world cover.)
- **Lantern at noon** means a share is due; a lantern at midnight means nothing at all. Pieter finds this unfair.
- **Left boot first** on Marketday for luck; the Solemates disagree.

## 7. What the sayings are for in the writing

They are the voice of the place. Each should appear in someone's mouth at the moment it fits, **never explained**. The three that carry the book's meaning (10 "Start small. Small is plenty", 16 "More is a mouth. Enough is a meal", 35 "Say a true thing and pass the cup") should each appear first as a joke, then as a comfort, then as a payoff, and never be explained by a narrator.
