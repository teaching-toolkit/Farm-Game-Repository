It is Landing Day, when the river barge ties up at the Landing below the village and brings the post, every second week. Mei from the barge walks the letters up the hill herself. Behind her, puffing, comes Old Jan. Every Landing Day, when Mei calls out the names, Jan has shouted “Here!” for Moonedge, alone, for as long as anyone remembers. He wants to be there the first time it counts.

The writing is round and quick: her mother's. “Come home when you are ready, my love. The house is small, but the table is long. Your cousin asks after you.” Then, in brackets: “(Your father says Cobble is well and asks if you are warm. He means he saw you at the hill. —M.)” And: “(Somebody thought you might miss hazel. —M.)” A pressed hazel leaf falls out. Cobble is the family's old horse. Fenna folds the letter very small.

Greenstem, the boy with the herbs, wants to know why bread is so dear, and explains it himself, the way a child would. “Since the fire, every loaf in Hazelwood gets a chalk mark for the farm that burnt. @@ 2.18 | c2_kiln | W | no
Fenna lights the kiln and shuts the little door. For a long while nothing happens. Then the cracks in the door glow orange, like the edge of a sunrise.

In the morning she opens it, and the soft, sulky clay has become something that rings when she flicks it. Clay can change its mind, it seems. It only needs a lot of fire.

Fenna wants to sell her first loaves, and to be liked. Mistress Appleby, the baker, has a chalk board that must balance when the Lord's tollman checks it at the end of the week. The rule is one mark for the loaf and one for the name. So Moonedge pays double, and Fenna is paid less than the loaf is worth. Appleby is miserable about it. “Not mine to forgive,” she says. On the way out, Anna, who shapes the loaves at the bakery, slips a bun into Fenna's basket without a word.

@@ 2.24 | E-2.M09; c2_lark | S-rev | no

A man walks up the new gravel road as if he had always walked it. He sits on Fenna's fence and watches her work. “Lark,” he says. Then he says nothing at all for a while.

Then he is sitting on her crate. She wants him off it. He finds her own list pinned to the fence instead, and reads it aloud like a stern letter from an aunt. “‘Roof. Before frost.’ Who sent this?” He turns it over. “It's signed: you.”

She stacks a crate. He stacks one too, a little crooked. “Probably fine,” he says. It is. Mostly. When she looks again, the fence is empty and both crates are still standing.

Otto, the old ferryman, crosses the footbridge Fenna has mended, slowly, and stops in the middle, looking down at the water. “I used to ferry people over this,” he says. “With a pole.” He stops. He starts again. He stops.

“The night your family left, I took you across. You were so small. You did not cry.” He looks at her, then at the water. “I never forgot.” Fenna says nothing. Neither does he. The river says a good deal. Then he goes, without another word.
