# The farm that burned down

*Assembled story, before repairs: the master outline in play order, built from the game texts and the outline seeds. Baseline for comparison with story-repaired.md.*

## Before the first page

Evening. The room smells of tea. Minena has brought it in a small tin pot with a bent handle, and spilled half of it. The others sit on the floor, on the bed, on the old woman's feet: her grandchildren, all of them. A thin brown book lies on the blanket. By the bed stands a dented bucket full of Minena's stones and feathers.

“Tell us about the farm that burned down!” “Again?” she says. “Yes!” “Moonedge,” she says. “That one.” “And don't forget the bells,” whispers someone very small. “Shh! That comes later! In order!”

She closes her eyes. She is not sure she remembers it right. “I never start with the fire,” she says. “I start with the ash. I had borrowed a tent. It smelled of horse.” Then she hums, a few slow notes, without noticing. “You're humming,” says Minena. “Am I?”

## Chapter 1: Ashes

Moonedge. Early spring. The tent smells of horse. Outside it, everything is grey. Ash, mud, black posts where a house once stood. The farm of Fenna's family is gone.

But look. One green patch in the ash. On it stands one ripe wheat stalk. Every grain has been nibbled off. All but one.

Fenna picks up the last grain. Somebody was here. And somebody left it for her.

In the tent is the box her grandmother left. In it are a book, a tin pot and a dented bucket, smaller than she remembers. Her grandmother's old hat hangs on the tent peg. On top of the book lies a note in thin brown ink: “You are fifteen. That is old enough to go. If you are reading this, you went. Good. Do not apologise to the bucket. —B.”

The book in the box is old. On the first page, in thin brown ink: “Start small. Small is plenty.”

Fenna reads it twice. Then Berit's kitchen song comes back, and a line of words: “Long ears sow the clover green.”

The first night. The sky is huge. At the edge of the dark forest, white stones glow, very faintly.

The tune that has followed her all week is coming from those stones. She finds she is humming it. The stones grow a little brighter, for exactly as long as she stands still. She does not go in.

So many stars. Nobody told her there would be so many. Nobody was here to tell her.

The last of the bread is wrapped in a corner cut from the tent canvas, with a wheel burnt into it and the letters WEIR. Fenna eats it standing up, in four bites. Her mother's note is folded inside: “The house is small. The table is long. Come when you're ready. Everyone sends love. (Everyone means everyone. —M.)” There is a drawing too, of the farm, with a very large chicken. Fenna pins it up inside the tent flap. She told them she would stay a while.

The tent breathes in and out with the wind. Fenna is getting used to living inside something that breathes.

The forest starts where the field gives up. The trees stand close and very still, like people who stopped talking when she walked in. A path goes in between them. She does not. Not today.

In the margin of the page about fire, in thin brown ink: “A fire is a guest. Never leave it alone in the house, and never blame the house. — B.”

The first spark sulks. The second one dies of embarrassment. The third one catches, and Fenna leans over it until her eyebrows complain. A fire, in this place. The ash around it glows a little, as if it remembers something.

Then she fills the bucket, sets it beside the fire, and sits as far off as the warmth allows.

Somewhere in the dark: THUMP. THUMP. THUMP. A short roll, which stops when she banks the fire. She makes the porridge anyway.

Weeds. Of course. They came back to a burnt field before anything else did, which tells you something about weeds. Fenna pulls one. Its root is longer than her arm, and very proud of it.

Next to the weeds in the book, small and slanted: “Weeds are only plants nobody invited. Some of my best friends were weeds. — B.”

When the last weed is out, she hears it: a tiny bell, high in the air. She does not look up. Then nothing. Behind her, three sticks lie in a very silly pile. Nothing is missing. She uses them as kindling.

In the margin of the page about water: “An empty bucket is a hat. A full one is a promise. Fill it at dusk, not at dawn. —B.” Fenna looks at her bucket. She fills it at once, and sets it where she can see it.

The old bucket clanks against the stones. It sounds almost proud.

An old woman comes up the track after a goat that is faster than she is and very fond of Fenna's weeds. Granny Maud. She sees the hat before she sees the face. “I'd know that hat on a scarecrow,” she says.

A seller comes along the track by the field and sniffs the air. “Is that porridge?” Fenna hands her a bowl. She eats it standing up, very fast, the way Fenna does.

“You're from Moonedge, aren't you?” she says. “Good porridge. I'll tell all of Hazelwood somebody is cooking up here.” She looks Fenna up and down. “You're hardly more than a child.” She will come by again, she says, when the road is dry.

“Do you ever stop?” calls a cart driver from the track. Fenna waves. She does not stop.

A boy stands at the fence with his sheep, shivering. Tom. He wants to sit by a fire, but he will not step inside the field. “The sheep don't like it,” he says, “and neither do I.” Fenna hands a log over the fence.

What is left of the barn is a black rectangle in the ash, with one doorframe still standing. On its post hangs an empty iron hook. Fenna walks round it. She does not walk through it. The bucket in her hand clanks, and she has a great deal to do about the woodpile. She re-stacks it. She re-stacks the re-stacked pile, faster.

Fenna needs more things to carry water in. By the burnt post of the old gate stands a row of old buckets. Each has a small tin leaf nailed under the rim, too tidy to be rubbish. The oldest are rusted, the newest bright. She takes them, and uses them.

On the flat top of the gatepost lies a short row of small, flat, grey river stones. Not the white stones of the forest. Not from here. The oldest are mossy, the newest bright. Fenna picks one up. It is smooth and cool. She puts it back exactly where it was.

A very quiet man comes out of the forest edge for the stumps. Old Cole. He wants them for charcoal, and says four words about it. Fenna wants to cook for him, and keeps trying. He leaves a sack of charcoal at the fence and escapes before the soup.

Under the black boards of the old workshop Fenna finds a page, burnt at the edges, headed MOONEDGE. Down the side runs a row of sacks. At the bottom, in red: “The Lord's share.” One sack in ten. Then one in five. Then one in three. Then one in two, circled twice. One edge is burnt away, where another column had been. Every time, the Lord's share is bigger. She folds the page small and keeps it.

The workbench is three logs, one plank and a great deal of confidence. Fenna sets a stone on it. The stone stays. This, she decides, is a very good bench.

The road to Hazelwood is mostly mud, with a few rumours of gravel. In the nearest rut lies one boot. Not Fenna's. It looks as if it gave up and sat down.

The walk to Hazelwood takes a while, and her boots have opinions about it. A dog barks. A child stares until somebody pulls her away by the sleeve. Fenna buys what she came for, and she says nothing else at all.

The market is small and loud and smells of onions. The woman at the next stall looks at Fenna's hat, and then, carefully, at something else. Nobody says the word Moonedge. Everybody is thinking it.

In the book, squeezed in beside the prices: “A fair price is one both sides complain about equally. — B.”

A coin. A real one, small and dented, with a face on it that looks as if it has had a long day. Around the rim: WEXLEY. “That's the Lord,” says the woman who hands it over. “He likes to be on things.” Fenna puts it in her pocket. She takes it out to check. She puts it back.

A notice board, full of little papers. Somebody needs a basket. Somebody has lost a goat. Fenna reads every one twice, and keeps her hat low.

The book cart smells of dust and the inside of a hat. The bookseller looks up. “Reading is the one thing you cannot do wrong,” he says, “unless it is upside down.” Fenna checks her own book. The right way up. Good.

Tom the shepherd boy has stopped shivering. He presses a bent horseshoe into Fenna's hand. “Lucky,” he says. “It never worked for me.” She hangs it by the tent flap anyway.

Ella from the mill wants a basket, and her father must not know it comes from Moonedge. Fenna makes it. Ella gives her a brass bell, bright as a button. “For luck,” she whispers, with one eye on the mill. Fenna shakes it. Bong. She listens for the other bell, the small high one. This is not that bell.

The goat is not Fenna's goat, which disappoints everyone, the goat included. Granny Maud takes Fenna's face in both hands. “Oh, Fenna,” she says. “Berit all over. Berit's stubborn self.” Her eyes are wet. She blames the goat.

“She raised you on that hill,” says Maud, “and she told me she was sending you back. I said, Berit, you cannot send a person. She said, watch me.”

Summer. The ash has gone green at the edges, a thin soft green, like the fur on something asleep. Fenna walks the field slowly, in case it is a mistake.

The air above the field wobbles in the heat like jelly. Somewhere a cricket is making a complaint.

Chip, chip, chip. The flake that comes off is sharp enough to split a hair. Her first axe. It fits her hand as if it had been waiting for one.

She cuts the stalks and tips the grains into her hand. Count them. One grain went into the ground. Look at them now. She closes her fingers round them, carefully, the way you would hold a baby bird.

At the bottom of the ash page, in the same brown ink: “Ash feeds the soil. The soil does not hold a grudge. Take notes. — B.”

Rocks. Fenna lifts one. Under it is a smaller rock, looking surprised. Under that, another. Whatever lives down there has been collecting rocks for a very long time.

Fenna heaps the rocks at the edge of the field. By morning the heap is a tower, each stone balanced on the one below, the smallest on top like a hat. Nothing is missing. She looks around. Nobody. Only a very small sound, high and bright, like a bell.

She takes the tower down, rock by rock, for the hearth. High up, a small sound that was there before goes out, like a candle. The quiet stays.

Something rustles in the long grass. It stops when she looks.

The quern grinds with a noise like a cart on a bad road. What comes out is flour. Fenna tastes a pinch off her thumb. It tastes of work and, a bit, of somewhere she has been before.

The seller comes back, and gives her name this time: Rue. She wants a sack of flour for her stall. The miller tasted a pinch, she says, and said “adequate”, like a man swallowing a bee. Then, lightly: “A carter in Weir asked if I'd seen you. ‘Look for the bucket,’ he said. I said you were busy.”

At the end of summer Granny Maud comes up with a goat on a rope and a bucket with a tin leaf under its rim. She sets it on the gatepost. It stays there till morning, she says, as it does every late summer. Fenna wants the water for the wheat, right now. She leaves it alone. For her, that is very hard. In the morning the bucket is empty. Nobody says who drank it. Maud says one thing: “We came with buckets, that night. Too late.”

Autumn. Smoke from the village chimneys drifts over the hill, and for a moment the wind smells like other people's suppers. Fenna stops what she is doing. Then she starts again, a little faster.

The scarecrow is two sticks and a sack. By noon the crows are holding a meeting on its shoulder. Minutes are taken. Nothing is decided.

A crow lands on the fence and looks over Fenna's work. It makes a small dry sound. She decides it was a review.

The spade rings on old stones, a curve of them. Fenna's hand goes to a worn notch in the rim before she knows why: the notch where she stood on a crate to reach. Then the hole goes dark and wet. Water. She lowers the bucket and drinks from her hands. It tastes of stone and cold and, faintly, of morning.

Two children come up the track, very hungry, daring each other to touch the burnt gatepost. Lotte and Linus. Linus is sure there is a ghost. Lotte is sure it is a cat. Everybody knows, they say, that the farm used to ring by itself when it was empty. Fenna feeds them porridge.

No thistles. No rocks. No stumps. Where the weeds stood there is only soil, brown and soft and a good deal bigger than she expected. It is a field. Fenna says the word out loud, to see how it fits.

The village wants soup. Fenna will not borrow a cart, so she carries two pots down the hill. The hill wins.

On the square stands the Pot, a big iron pot on a tripod. Maribel Kettle keeps it on market days. She wants Fenna to sit and eat before anything is asked. “Taste before you talk.” Fenna wants to hand over the soup and go back to work. At her family's name the Pot goes quiet for a breath. Then Maribel passes the salt, and Fenna sits on an upturned bucket for one minute. From the far side, Marta Hewitt hands over a smooth river stone, the Soup Stone, “so nobody's pot is the only pot.”

The soup goes round in bowls. People take it without a word, and come back for more without a word. A woman tastes it. “Needs salt,” she says, and takes a second bowl.

Behind her, a child whispers, “Is it the burnt farm soup?” The woman does not turn round. “It's soup,” she says. The child has a second bowl too.

Snow came in the night, without a sound. The green patch is the last green thing for miles. Round it, in the snow: long prints, short prints, three times round. The prints stop at the edge of the forest.

It is cold enough for the water bucket to grow a lid. Fenna chips it off and drinks the chips. Today's porridge is mostly an excuse to stand near the fire. It is a very good excuse.

The tent flaps in the wind. Fenna pulls the blanket up to her nose.

On Longnight, far below, the village's lights go out one by one, all but the Hall. A hum comes up the valley. Nobody has asked her. The tent flaps. Fenna gets back to work, louder.

The stick fence wobbles when Fenna looks at it. It will not stop anything bigger than an argument. But now there is an inside and an outside, and she is standing on the inside.

Down to Hazelwood again. The dog by the first house has stopped barking and only looks at her now. In dog, this is a welcome.

The coop is finished. Nobody lives in it. Fenna leaves the little door open anyway, in case somebody is looking.

One night a lantern comes along the road below, slowly, as if it wants to know whether her smoke is a fire. Fenna freezes until it has passed. A tired voice says, “Dry night,” and does not stop. Afterwards her hands shake.

Spring again. The ash has a green skin now, and the ground is soft under her boots. The patch is not the only green thing any more. She treads softly, in case it minds.

The hens come out of their basket one at a time, inspect the coop, find three faults, and take over the yard. By evening Fenna is the one being supervised.

Across the top of the hen page, written in a hurry: “Do not name the hens. You will name the hens. — B.”

Fenna hums while she works. At the same place every time, the hens go quiet and look at each other. She hums on past it.

The first egg is still warm. The hen watches Fenna take it with the face of someone who has views about pricing.

Rue wants Fenna to see something: a new, clean notch cut in the gatepost. “A man from the manor, the Lord's house up the hill, does that when he starts a book,” she says. “I said it was a kid with a bucket.” Fenna cannot un-cut it.

Somebody has left a stool beside the track, just before the first houses, exactly where Fenna would stop to rest. She does not sit on it. She puts her hand on it as she passes.

Two carts meet on the narrow bit of road by the Hop. Each driver wants the other to back up. Tom's sheep settle it by walking through.

In the village, children skip to a song. One line has gone missing, so they sing that line all “la”.

The winch squeals like a pig being asked a hard question. But the bucket comes up full, and Fenna no longer has to climb down for it. She winds it up and down twice, for the pleasure of the noise.

Her hands have started to look like someone else's hands. Someone who works.

She has built a hutch of straw and sticks, round and soft, big enough for something small. She does not know who it is for. She puts it beside the green patch, with the door facing the forest.

Somebody has nibbled the tops off the carrots. Neatly, all in a row, and the roots left alone. A thoughtful thief.

A bowl of greens and sliced carrot, set at the edge of the green patch. Fenna walks away, so as not to look like someone who is waiting. She looks back four times.

A bunny sits on the one green patch. She sits like someone who has always sat there.

Now Fenna understands. The patch stayed green because somebody looked after it. The stalk was nibbled because somebody was hungry. And one grain was left for her.

Now the first line of the old rhyme makes sense: “Long ears sow the clover green.” She says it out loud. The bunny's ear twitches. There are more lines. Fenna says it over, and waits for the next one. Nothing comes.

That night, from far away: two soft rings of a very small bell. Fenna sits up in the dark. The bunny is asleep in the clover. So who else is still here?

Back in the room, the tea has gone cold. “Was there really a bunny?” asks Minena. “There was,” says the old woman. “She was very rude about carrots.”

“And the bell? Who rang the bell?” “Shh!” says everybody at once. “That's next time!” The old woman smiles, and hums. Minena puts a hand on her arm. They know this story almost as well as she does. Almost.

## Chapter 2: Homestead

It is Landing Day, when the river barge brings the post, every second week. Mei from the barge walks the letters up the hill herself. Behind her, puffing, comes Old Jan. Every Landing Day, when Mei calls out the names, Jan has shouted “Here!” for Moonedge, alone, for as long as anyone remembers. He wants to be there the first time it counts.

The writing is round and quick: her mother's. “Come home when you are ready, my love. The house is small, but the table is long. Your cousin asks after you.” Then, in brackets: “(Your father says Cobble is well and asks if you are warm. He means he saw you at the hill. —M.)” And: “(Somebody thought you might miss hazel. —M.)” A pressed hazel leaf falls out. Fenna folds the letter very small.

Mei has to be back on the river by the tide, and waits for a reply. Fenna wants to answer without answering. She writes three drafts, each one shorter. The first has the whole farm in it. The second has the hens. The third has one word. Mei reads upside down, from habit. “Is that all?” Nothing is sent. Mei goes back down to her boat with her hands empty.

Between two pages of the old book there is a note in thin brown ink: “Rest the field. Rest yourself. — B.” Fenna reads it twice. Then she looks at her hands.

The walls of the cottage stand. Fenna built them alone, and it shows. One leans a little to the left. She steps back, looks at them for a long time, and decides that they are perfect.

The cow comes down the plank, looks at the barn, looks at the yard, looks at Fenna. She does not seem impressed by any of it. Then she lowers her head and eats a mouthful of Fenna's best hay, slowly, while holding her eye. That is how Fenna finds out who is in charge.

The bunny wants to know whether the cow is a hawk. The cow wants nothing, which is no help. THUMP at the cow. THUMP at the cow's tail. THUMP at the shine on the milk pail. Then a small *pad*: a very sorry bunny. The cow chews through all of it.

Somewhere behind the barn the cow sighs, a long, deep sigh like a door being shut on a bad day.

Fenna wants to know what the white stones are. By day they are only a row of white stones inside the first trees, and an oak with faded ribbons. She puts a hand on one. It is warm, and her hand stops shaking.

A boy waits at the gate with a basket of herbs bigger than his arms. “You're the Moonedge one,” he says. “You used to carry a bucket bigger than you.” She did.

“Why is that wall crooked? Does it hurt when you hammer? Did you know sage is good for a cough?” He asks too many questions. Fenna finds, to her surprise, that she does not mind.

So many stumps, for such a small farm. Their tops are clean and flat. Someone cut the forest back, long ago, and nobody planted anything in its place.

Behind the barn is a compost pit, clean and dry, its edges squared with flat stones. Someone cared about this. Scratched on the lid: “Feed the ground and it feeds you. — B.”

Greenstem wants to know why bread is so dear, and explains it himself, the way a child would. “Since the fire, every loaf in Hazelwood gets a chalk mark for the farm that burnt. Nobody likes it.” Fenna wants to pay it. There is nobody to pay. After that, she sees chalk everywhere.

New notices have appeared on the board in the square. One is a recipe. One is a lost glove. One says, in large letters, “WHOEVER TOOK MY LADDER, THANK YOU, KEEP IT.” Fenna decides that Hazelwood is a place that runs on notices.

Between the lost glove and the recipe is a small paper, pinned square, with a grey edge: THE MARK STANDS UNTIL THE FARM GIVES AGAIN. Fenna gives. She has given since she came. She looks for someone to tell.

Children are skipping on the road. Fenna wants to pass. One child sings the chalk rhyme to her face: “Chalk, chalk, chalk on the sack, who's to pay it? Moonedge back!” Her mother hushes her. The next rhyme is a different one: “Lightning in the night, the little one ran out.” Fenna goes still.

On the page about roots, Berit has written about the rabbit, and about Fenna. “She thumps for a hawk, for a lantern, and for bad manners. You kept her in a basket when you were small. She allowed it. She still has views. —B.”

The weeds Fenna pulled have been laid out on the path. They are smiling. It is a very bad smile, gone wrong on one side. She walks round it all day.

Fenna lights the kiln and shuts the little door. For a long while nothing happens. Then the cracks in the door glow orange, like the edge of a sunrise.

In the morning she opens it, and the soft, sulky clay has become something that rings when she flicks it. Clay can change its mind, it seems. It only needs a lot of fire.

A proper bench, at last, with a vice, a dozen hooks and a groove for the pencil. Fenna puts a plank in the vice and it stays there. Nothing rolls away. Nothing needs a foot on it. She stands there for a moment feeling like a person who owns a vice.

Beside the bread page, in a hand she has only just got to know: “Not so much yeast. Patience does the rest.”

Fenna wants to sell her first loaves, and to be liked. Mistress Appleby has a chalk board that must balance when the Lord's tollman checks it at the end of the week. The rule is one mark for the loaf and one for the name. So Moonedge pays double, and Fenna is paid less than the loaf is worth. Appleby is miserable about it. “Not mine to forgive,” she says. On the way out, Anna slips a bun into Fenna's basket without a word.

The boy with the herbs turns up as the loaf comes out, which is luck or a very good nose. Fenna breaks it. He holds his half in both hands and breathes it in with his eyes shut. “Rosemary does that,” he says seriously, about nothing much.

He eats the whole half in four bites. “It is a good loaf,” he says, “for a person who built that wall.”

Fenna sets up the stall and stands behind it. It is very odd. Yesterday she was a person who walked up to the market. Today she is a person people walk up to. Somebody asks the price of a carrot, and she tells them, and her voice comes out a little higher than planned.

A man walks up the new gravel road as if he had always walked it. He sits on Fenna's fence and watches her work. “Lark,” he says. Then he says nothing at all for a while.

Then he is sitting on her crate. She wants him off it. He finds her own list pinned to the fence instead, and reads it aloud like a stern letter from an aunt. “‘Roof. Before frost.’ Who sent this?” He turns it over. “It's signed: you.”

She stacks a crate. He stacks one too, a little crooked. “Probably fine,” he says. It is. Mostly. When she looks again, the fence is empty and both crates are still standing.

Hilde hangs the last sheet, and it flaps like a flag from a very small country. “I hear things about you in the lane, Fenna,” she says, not looking at her. “Good things. Nobody has one bad word. It is ruining the lane.”

On the page about hay there are two hands in one margin. Berit's, firm: “Hay: dry it, turn it. Lantern on the hook OUTSIDE, and never past the sill. —B.” Under it, small and round: “Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother. —T.”

Fenna carries the hive to the sunny corner of the yard and sets it down. A hum comes out of it, low and enormous, as if the box were holding a thought too big for itself. She steps back.

Several thousand tiny creatures have just agreed to live here. They did not ask her. She did not ask them. It seems best to be polite.

Next to the bees, in the margin: “Talk to them. It doesn't matter what about.” Under it, in a different pen: “I mean it, B.” Fenna wonders who had to be told.

Fenna holds the first honey up to the window. It is gold, and slow, and the light goes into it and stays. A spoon of summer, whatever the weather outside. Somewhere in it is every flower the bees visited, and she can almost taste the list.

Old Jan eats the tomato soup in small, serious spoonfuls, and by the third spoon his cheeks go pink. “This is the sort of soup,” he says, “that Berit made.” He taps the bowl. “And that goat could count, you know. Moonedge goat. Count to five, I swear.” Nobody believes him. Fenna rather does.

She sniffs the saucer. She sniffs the straw. She walks round the bed three times, sits in the middle, turns her back to Fenna and begins to wash. This means yes. Nobody has said a word, and Fenna stands very still so as not to spoil it.

On the page about play, a drawing in the margin: a goat balancing on a barrel. Under it: “Ten out of ten.”

Otto crosses the mended bridge slowly and stops in the middle, looking down at the water. “I used to ferry people over this,” he says. “With a pole.” He stops. He starts again. He stops.

“The night your family left, I took you across. You were so small. You did not cry.” He looks at her, then at the water. “I never forgot.” Fenna says nothing. Neither does he. The river says a good deal. Then he goes, without another word.

Fenna wants the ridge done before dark. On the corner, exactly where the last bundle of thatch goes, lies a hammer the size of her thumb. It has been used. It is in exactly the wrong place. She puts the last bundle on the other side. The roof has a bump now. She decides it is a feature.

The stove ticks as it warms up, the way a cat ticks when it is thinking. The smell of the cottage changes. Wood, and iron, and a pot that is nearly ready to be a soup. Fenna does not have to eat standing up any more.

The first leaves tap on the roof, one by one, as if asking to be let in.

A real roof, before the frost. Fenna sits under it while the first rain taps on the tiles. Then she takes a sheet of paper and writes home: “I'm staying a bit longer.” Her hand shakes only a little.

A lantern stops at the gate, in daylight. Pieter looks at the roof for a long time, adds one line to his book, says “Good thatch,” and goes. Fenna looks at her roof as if it might be counted.

The barge comes round the bend, and Mei nods as before, and hands over a letter. The writing is round and quick, and there is a lot more of it than last time.

“I am not going to say ‘I told you so’, because I never told you anything. Eat properly. The table is still long.” Under that, in smaller letters: “Ask Maud about the goat.” Fenna does not know what that means. She puts the letter with the other one.

Frost. Written beside it in the book, small: “Cover the beans. Cover everything. Then cover Maud's.”

Fenna wakes in the cottage and the light is wrong. Too white, too quiet. At the window, the yard has gone soft. The roof she built is holding up a whole cold sky and not complaining. She stays under the blanket a little longer than is strictly necessary.

On Longnight, Greenstem wants her to come down to the Hall. The village wants nothing from her. Fenna takes the ladle, so that she never has to say a true thing, though no one has asked her to. She serves a great many bowls and says nothing. Mrs. Wiggins the pig is the only one who notices. Somebody puts a cup in her hand anyway, and she does not know why.

Beside the page about sleep, underlined twice: “You, too.”

Fenna clears the last black beam from the barn. Then she stops. The burn does not start at the roof, where lightning would strike. It starts low, in the corner where the hay was kept.

She sits back on her heels. Lightning does not start low. Something else began this. She does not know what. And she finds that she does not want to know. Not yet.

In the village the children skip to a rhyme about lightning and the quiet one who ran out. Fenna stops mid-step and says, under her breath, “It started low.” The rope slaps. They swap to the Mark rhyme very fast.

The frame of the new barn goes up, beam by beam. Against the evening it looks like the ribs of something very large, sleeping on its side. The old barn burned from the corner. Fenna walks all the way round the new one anyway, just to check that every corner is only a corner.

In the new barn frame Fenna finds an old iron hook. It is black with soot, and it was made to hold a lantern. “If it was not lightning,” she thinks, “then what?” She hangs the hook back up. Carefully.

Old Jan comes up to grumble at the new frame, kindly. “That hook hung outside by the door every night of my life. Never past the sill. Berit's girl, Tilda, said it in her sleep. Laughed like a bucket of marbles.” Fenna asks how the fire got in, then. Jan coughs. “Ask me when I'm not coughing.” He coughs on a schedule.

The barn is finished. It smells of new pine and fresh straw. Fenna hangs a lantern on a post outside, not on a hook inside, and stands back and nods. Hay and flame should live far apart. That is as far as she lets herself think.

Fenna wants to know whether the pond is worth digging. In the dry silt is a round, cracked dent. At the edge, a flat stone has been worn into a seat, and in front of it is a hollow the shape of someone who sat a long time looking up. The cracks close in a ring, tighter every year. There is moss only on the stone.

The last spadeful comes out, and the water arrives. It is slow about it. First a dark patch, then a shine, then a sky lying on the ground with clouds in it.

A frog appears at once and says nothing at all. It has the manner of someone who has been waiting for this for years.

Fenna sets the little shell house down in the sunniest corner of the yard. It is exactly big enough for someone who carries their own roof. She steps back and looks at it. It looks back, empty and patient.

Something very slow is crossing the yard. A tortoise, moving like a stone that has decided to travel. She goes straight to the pond and sits down where the spring first rose.

Fenna sits down beside her and waits. The pond fills a little, then a little more. Nothing is hurried. And the second line of the old rhyme comes back to her: “Slow shell keeps the water clean.”

Back in the room, somebody has fallen asleep on the floor. “Did you find out who started it?” asks Minena. “Not then,” says the old woman. “I didn't want to.”

“And the man on the fence? Was he funny?” “Very.” “Was that good?” “It made him hard to talk to,” she says, and nobody understands. That is all right.

## Chapter 3: Smallholding

Another letter comes from the river barge. Her mother writes: “Stay if you must. Your cousin asks after you.” Fenna reads the last line again. To her it sounds like a warning.

All day there is a hush in the yard that Fenna cannot explain. The hens go in early. The wind drops. A dog barks once, far off, and nothing answers. It is only an ordinary afternoon, wearing the face of a not-ordinary one.

Fenna's list has been nibbled at one corner. She has no goat. She frowns at the list. The list does not explain itself.

Fenna finds herself counting. Things she has. Things she owes. Things in the barn. The numbers do not add up to anything alarming. She counts again anyway. Then a hen walks across the paper, and she has to start over, and it somehow feels better.

The next time, the list is gone entirely, and a hoofprint sits in the ink. Fenna has no goat. She writes the list again, in her head.

A sparrow sits on the tortoise for a whole song. She is the only still place in the yard.

On the step lies a clover leaf with one bite out of it, and a pebble beside it, nudged a hair straight. Then one polite thump. The bunny is asking for a job. Fenna gives her one.

Fenna wants to know what is behind the fence. A wall of bramble, taller than she is, with thorns like a great many small opinions. Deep in the middle is a hollow lined with moss, the size of a small thing curled up, and a scrap of white thread caught on a thorn. She does not reach for it.

On the salt page, in thin brown ink: “Salt on the table first. Then the bread. Then the rent. The Keepers wrote it into the lease so: never so much that the table is bare. The Hurst copied the rest.” And, smaller: “(Goats lick it. Count your salt. —B.)”

Fenna wants to know why the tortoise sat on that stone. Berit's page says: “Do not argue with a tortoise. You will lose, slowly. You took her water at dawn in a bucket as big as you. She never thanked you. That is how you know.”

Fenna puts a stone where the tortoise is, to check. By evening the tortoise is two paces past it.

A lantern swings up the road in the daylight. Under it walks Pieter, the night watchman. He does not look happy.

“I am sorry, Fenna. The Lord wants his share. It is not my idea. I only carry the book.” He shows her a number. “Pay when you can. I will wait at the gate.”

Fenna wants seed. Rue's cart will not sell to a house with a lantern at its gate. “The Lord's share first, love. Pay Pieter at the gate.” She turns the cart round and sits on the tail with an apple. “I'm good at waiting. I once waited for a goat.” Pieter has plainly walked the long way round to avoid her eyes. A hen walks out of the brush by the fence and lays an egg in front of everybody. “I could see an egg,” says Rue, “if you had one to sell me.” Fenna sells it. That always works.

On Share Day the Hall feeds everyone before Pieter writes, so that nobody pays on an empty stomach. The Pot is poor, and the soup is thin. Pieter is given the first bowl, as always. Every house sets out a stool for him, so that the man who comes to take can sit while he does it. Nobody says why. Moonedge has no stool yet. So Pieter eats standing, and looks into the bowl as if it were a window. On his page, at the foot, there is one line in grey: “carried down.” “Down where?” asks Fenna. “Down,” says Pieter.

The next time, Fenna brings a pot of her own. The soup goes thick, and the village goes quiet. At the gate there is one stool now. Pieter will not sit while Fenna stands. Fenna will not sit while Pieter stands. The bunny takes the stool. They eat on the ground, and his tiredness shows.

Pieter taps his book shut. “Thank you. Most people hide when they see me.” He almost smiles. Then he walks home, whistling a tune he does not know he is whistling.

Pieter comes back to apologise, and his book has a small, round, wet mark on it. “A bush spat on the book,” he says. “Only on the book. Not on me. I checked.”

Far down the road, a lantern waves at Fenna. Pieter. She waves back.

Old Odinson lights the forge and young Bo works the bellows. They show Fenna how to hold the tongs. “Good hands,” says Odinson, which is the most he has said all day. Bo grins at the fire.

Somewhere down the road, a hammer rings, and stops, and rings again. Somebody is making something. It is a good sound to live next to.

The shovel goes in like a hand into water. No catch, no clunk. Fenna digs a whole row before she notices she has forgotten to be tired. She looks at the shovel. The shovel does not look at her. It is only a shovel, and very happy.

Beside the wind page: “Stand in it for a minute before you build. It will tell you which way it likes.”

The mill tower stands, taller than anything Fenna has made yet, with nothing at the top. A round, empty room full of wind. She climbs to the last step. Hazelwood lies small and clear below her, like a map drawn with great care. Behind her, the forest, and beyond that, more.

In the old mill, on a rusty nail, hangs a bell. It is far too small for a cow. It is far too small for anything. It sways, just a little, though there is no wind.

In the dust beneath it Fenna thinks she sees tiny footprints. She looks the other way. She is quite sure that she looked the other way.

Fenna wants to clear the wall. Below the nail, on the old plaster, is a charcoal drawing: a girl with a bucket nearly as big as she is, holding hands with something the size of a thumb with dots round its middle. Beside it are two sooty prints, a hand and a thumb. Fenna does not remember drawing it. She stands there exactly as long as it takes to stop. Then she finds she is humming, and goes out.

Fenna fixes the last sail and steps back. For a moment, nothing. Then the whole thing groans, like an old person getting up, and begins to turn. Slowly. Then not slowly.

The grinding fills the yard, and the flour begins to fall in a quiet white rope. The wind is working for her. It has never done that before, as far as she knows. It was waiting to be asked.

Ella wants her great-grandfather to see the new sails. The miller wants him home before he catches cold. The old man wants his hat. He gets it, and salutes the sails. “Put that on, you'll catch cold,” says the miller, and stands where he can see them. Ella smiles.

The sails slow, creak, and stop, like someone who has forgotten the next word. Then the wind comes back, and they carry on.

On the bridge, the miller glares down at the stream. It is still very low. He glares at Fenna, too, just in case.

The stream runs clear again. On the bridge the miller looks down at the water. Then at Fenna. He nods once, a very small nod. That is his whole apology, and she takes it.

Then he says thank you, fast, glaring at the water. “Now I owe the Hurst a penny a sack again. Thank you.” And he walks off. Fenna laughs. Then she looks at the stream differently.

Lark wants nothing. Fenna wants her list back. He is holding the bitten page. “Item four, ‘rest’: eaten. Item nine, ‘rest’: eaten. She's a very good editor.” Fenna writes a new list.

Pieter arrives with his lantern, in daylight, as usual. This time he stops at the gate and checks his book twice, with the face of a man looking for a reason to turn round. There isn't one.

“It's the same as last time, Fenna,” he says, “only more.” He accepts a cup of water and drinks it as though it might be evidence.

Pieter wants to tell her one true thing he is allowed to. Fenna wants to see the book. He shows her nothing. But he says that the chalk on the widows' sacks at the Landing is very small, because the Lord never said how small a mark must be. Fenna cannot thank him aloud.

A lantern swings, far down the road, in the daylight. Fenna stands up very straight, as if it were someone she knew.

On market day the village will buy eight loaves, and the ninth sits. Appleby's loaf is the ninth. Nobody blames Fenna, but Anna says it, quietly. Fenna has to decide what a good neighbour does. She gives up her market corner every second week and sells to the Nest instead, for less. Appleby does not say thank you. She cannot. She puts an extra bun in the basket.

The sheep comes off the cart and stands in the yard looking like a cloud that has lost its way. She is round, and white, and cannot see her own feet. She regards Fenna gravely, as if she were a rather puzzling hat.

The loom clacks. Over, under. Over, under. At first the cloth looks like a lie, a few crooked threads pretending. After an hour it is a hand wide. After two, it is a hand and a thumb. Cloth, Fenna realises, is just patience that learned to hold its shape.

In the margin of the weaving page: “A mistake in the first row is still in the last one. That is fine. It is how you can tell it is yours.”

Somebody has folded the blanket on the end of Fenna's bed with the corners just so. It was not her. She is fairly sure it was not her.

There is a faint smell of lavender. She gets into bed without thinking about it, which is the best sort of thing to do with a mystery.

Fenna goes down seven steps into the cellar, and the air changes. It is cool and still and smells of earth that has been thinking. Her voice comes back to her a moment late.

This is a place for keeping things safe, and it knows it, and it does not mind keeping her safe too.

Fermenting. In the margin: “If it smells like a goat, it is wrong. If it smells like the Goat, it is perfect.” Fenna is not sure which goat is meant.

Fenna puts another stone where the tortoise is.

The new scarecrow has a bow in its hat. Fenna did not put it there. She checks the knot. It is her knot. She decides not to ask any questions that might get answered.

Among the old trees Fenna finds a rope hanging from the thickest branch. A plank, worn smooth. Somebody once sat here. The knot at the top has been retied, and not well, and not long ago.

She tests the swing with one hand. It holds. She does not know who retied it. She does not sit down. Not yet.

Next to the pests: “Everything eats something. Do not take it personally. Do take the slugs somewhere else.”

Granny Maud's goat sits in the yard looking pleased with itself. “She keeps coming here,” says Maud, with the air of someone solving a crime. “Do you suppose it's a clue?” Fenna does not, and says so.

Maud nods sadly. “It is, though.” She pats Fenna's arm, takes the rope, and leads the goat home. The goat looks back twice.

The blanket is very small. Fenna had not thought how small. The baby grips a corner of it in one fist, and does not let go, and goes to sleep in the middle of what it was doing. Fenna would not mind being that sure about something.

The board in the square has a new notice, in a very firm hand: “WELCOME BACK. WE HAD OUR DOUBTS.” It is not signed. Under it someone has drawn a small goat. Fenna is not sure what it means. She takes it as a compliment.

At the harvest dance, Rosa the fiddler wants the Late Mug filled for whoever comes last, and she wants the missing end of her tune.

Fenna works the cider while the village dances. Mugs float above the heads. One falls, she catches it, and she laughs out loud. Somewhere close by, something goes tink, like a very small bell. She does not look.

Fenna has worked through the whole dance, so she is the last, and the Late Mug is hers. She hums while she stacks the mugs, and does not know she is doing it. Rosa freezes. “Hum that again.” “I'm not humming,” says Fenna. She leaves in a hurry. Rosa plays the tune badly all night.

The air is thick and gold. Everything is humming, even the stones.

The sun is on the pond, and the pond is giving it back. Dragonflies write quick, blue letters on the air. Fenna sits with her feet in the water and thinks about nothing. Nothing is very good for her.

One autumn day Fenna is on the Landing Steps by accident, and the whole village is there, silent, looking down the river. She wants to know what everyone is looking at. The grey barge comes round the bend. The children are told to hold their breath until it has passed. They fail, one after another, comically. One small hand takes hers. Now she knows where the money goes.

In the margin of the water page: “Water goes where you show it, and then where it likes. Both are fine.”

In summer the village children want to run the Chain for the first time in a long time, now that the farm is smoking again. Maud wants the bucket put on the post. Fenna wants to carry water to the wheat. This time more than Maud turns up. A Chain of buckets runs from the Deep to the Hall and back, and the fastest team gets a bun. Ansel rings five fast, one slow. Fenna is handed the last bucket, and for once she passes it on.

Afterwards, Tom says: “Someone got off Mei's boat at dusk. Didn't say a word. Left before the buckets.” The twins say: “The Moonedge ghost has a sister. She's grey. She leaves rocks.” Fenna goes and looks. On top of the row of grey stones on the gatepost there is one fresh stone. She decides not to wait at the post.

The bigger barn is finished. A goat walks in, walks out, climbs on the roof, and looks down at Fenna, very pleased with itself. She has built a barn. She has been given a goat roof. It is not the same thing.

Four buckets, full, stand at the corners of the barn. Then eight at the farmhouse. The goat drinks from one. The tortoise sits in one. A hen lays in one.

Everyone wants to go in for the night, and the tortoise is at the gate. The bunny presses against her shell. Inside a circle exactly one tortoise wide, nothing at all happens. Outside it: THUMP.

The tortoise is at the cottage step, looking in, and the stones are a path from the pond to the house that Fenna did not mean to make.

Beyond the old fence the land goes on. Fenna had not looked. Long grass, a leaning post, a bird she does not know, doing something with a twig. There is room here for more than she thought, and it has been standing here the whole time, waiting for someone to notice.

A goat trots up the path and straight into the brambles nobody could clear. Crunch. Munch. She eats thistles as if they were sweets. Fenna opens her mouth to stop her. Then she lets her.

The third line of the old rhyme comes back, and Fenna says it with her mouth still open: “Brave goat clears the bramble bed.” The goat looks at Fenna's hat. Her eyes are thoughtful.

In the morning the scarecrow is wearing Fenna's hat. Nobody saw who put it there. Under the hat there is a letter, from Mei: “Your cousin asked me three times if she might come through the gate. Twice at the dock and once at the plank. I told her to ask you herself. She said she was asking.” Fenna takes her hat back. It is still warm.

Granny Maud sees the goat on the path and says the third line aloud, crossly, from memory. The children in the road pick it up.

Fenna wants everything perfect for someone who has not come. She makes the spare bed with square corners. She makes it again with hospital corners. She plumps the pillow, and the goat's opinion of it. A candle, a jug, a pie. For nobody.

“Were you scared of the cousin?” asks somebody from under a blanket. “More than of any imp,” says the old woman. “What's an imp?” “Shh!” say three children at once. “That comes later!”

## Chapter 4: Village Trade

A thin man stands at Fenna's gate with a ledger under his arm. He does not smile, and he does not look unkind either. He looks like someone who counts. “Master Quill,” he says. “The Lord's steward.”

“The old farm smokes again,” he says, and looks at her chimney. “The Lord wishes to know why.” Fenna tells him: because somebody lives here. He writes that down.

Quill's hat has a round dark spot on the brim. He does not mention it. Neither does Fenna.

Quill counts everything. Cows, sacks, windows, nails. He counts the hens twice, because one moved. Every number goes into his ledger, written with a very sharp pencil.

Later Fenna finds two ledger pages folded into a paper boat on the pond. Somewhere, a very small bell tinkles. Quill is not amused. Fenna is, a little.

Quill wants his folded pages back. He fishes the paper boat out of the pond with a rake and spreads the pages on Fenna's fence to dry. The red and grey inks have run to smears. The faint pencil down the margin, a third column, is perfectly clear. He looks at the pencil longer than at the ruin. Fenna looks at it too. “Notes,” says Quill.

Quill wants an exact number of hens. Granny Maud, passing with her goat, wants him to stop, because counting hens aloud is bad luck. He catches the look and finishes the count in a whisper. Nobody loses a hen. The village decides he is brave, or doomed.

In the soft verge of the new road there are two hoofprints side by side, neat as a pair of shoes. They are at every spot with a good view.

The road is cobbled now, so the letter bag no longer comes up the hill. On Landing Day Fenna goes down to the river for her letters, like everyone else. She wants not to be seen waiting. She has asked Mei to call her name like everyone's. “Moonedge!” calls Mei. The crowd waits. Fenna has to say it. “Here!” Old Jan beams. The Landing claps, as it claps at everyone's Here.

On the plank of the barge a coat in grey stops, hears her voice, and goes below deck. Mei says nothing, with great skill.

Fenna folds the finished blanket over the fence. In the morning it is draped in artful folds, one corner tucked as if by a painter.

In the margin of the masonry page: “A wall is only many small decisions in the same direction.”

Thorne, the Lord's gardener, comes up the road with a bundle of sticks: apple grafts he saved from the Moonedge orchard, and wants to give back. Quill is forty paces behind, counting, and must not see. He sees. He writes it down. The orchard gets its old apples back.

Quill wants an exact number of hens. The goat follows him, stamping. “Seven,” says Quill. Stamp, stamp, stamp, stamp, stamp, stamp, stamp, stamp. Eight. He counts again, his jaw set. There is a hen behind the barrel. Eight.

Quill writes “8” and does not write why. He reaches for his pencil. The goat is chewing.

Fenna steps into the new library room and stops. Somebody has sorted all her books by weight. The heaviest lies at the bottom, the lightest on top. Lark sits on the heaviest, reading the lightest. “Probably fine,” he says.

Fenna wants one more plank in by lantern light. The bunny wants her to look up: a quick triple thump, the hawk-and-fox kind. Fenna waves a hand and hammers on.

In the morning there are feathers by the coop and one hen fewer, the speckled one. The bunny sits by the empty nest box and does not thump when Fenna comes. She only looks. The coop door gets a bolt. The goat sleeps on the coop roof that night, and every night after.

Sand goes in. Fenna waits. Something comes out that she can see through, and it is the same sand, only now it lets the light walk straight in and out.

She holds the first bottle up. The yard turns upside down inside it, small and green and tipped, and for a moment it is the whole world.

In the margin of the glass page: “I once held a bottle up to the sun and cried for no reason. Do not be surprised.”

In a rusty tin under the glasshouse stones lies the old lease: “… a half of all that the land gives.” A half! Fenna takes out the burnt page from the workshop and holds it beside it. The red number is the same.

The lease is in the drawer. Fenna does not open the drawer. She knows it is there, which is nearly as bad.

Share Day. Quill wants the lease read before witnesses, because the Book requires it. The fence fills up: Maud, Appleby, Anna, Hilde, Jan, Ella, the twins. Fenna wants it over. Quill reads the Hurst's short copy aloud: “… a half of all that the land gives.” The fence goes still. Then he reads the lines the Book has found: “One glass kiln. One cheese vat. One press. The orchard.” Each is a small thud, and the number Pieter will carry rises. The goat eats a corner of the notice.

Fenna puts the tin on the rail. Quill stops. “There is a line at the bottom of this one,” he says.

Greenstem tells her what is being said in the Square. Quill's counting has made people afraid the Lord will count them next. “She's one of ours,” says Maud. “She's got a farmhouse,” says Appleby. “We have a chalk board.”

Maud and Appleby stop speaking for a week. On market day the Pot is cold.

Quill looks at the tin for an official minute. In the corner of the old page there is a small flourish, “Q.”, in a young hand. He sits down on a nail keg without being asked. He asks to copy one line from the bottom: “never so much that the table is bare.” He takes four minutes over eleven words.

Quill is at the gate again. “The Lord's share,” he says, “as before.” Fenna looks at the North Field, resting under clover. “No,” she says. Her voice is smaller than she hoped, but it does not shake.

Quill's pencil stops. On the fence, Lark swings his legs. “You've counted the cows,” he tells Quill. “Have you counted the clover? It counts you.” Quill writes that down too, by mistake.

The field is clear. In the middle stands one bramble the size of a table. The goat walks round it. Fenna reaches for the shears. The goat steps between. Fenna puts the shears down. Something in the bramble rustles. Nobody looks.

Fenna steps in and shuts the door, and the weather stays outside like a dog that has been told. Warm air. Wet air. Green air. Rows of leaves reach for the glass. The first tomato has chosen to be here, whatever the season outside, and she takes her hat off to it.

Mulch. Written beside it: “A blanket for dirt. Yes, dirt gets cold. Do not argue.”

“It's a long road,” says Pieter, and sits on Fenna's fence without being asked. He sets his lantern down beside him, still lit in broad daylight. Then he takes a flask from his coat and offers it to her, which is not in his book either.

It is cold tea, very sweet. They each take a sip. Then he stands, straightens his coat, and becomes a watchman again. “Same time next season.”

Pieter comes up the road with his lantern swinging and his book under his arm, and for the first time he does not open it at the gate. He just stands there.

“I wanted to say, Fenna,” he says, and stops. “It's heavy. I know. I'm the one who carries it.” Fenna nods. It is the nicest thing anyone has said all week.

Pieter gives Fenna a receipt. She has never had one. It says what she paid and what it was for, and in one corner, in tiny letters: “Thank you.” Somebody took the trouble. She hopes it was Pieter. She folds it small and keeps it with the burnt page.

Captain Mei holds the jar of pickles up to the light and nods, as if it had passed an exam. “For the long voyage,” she says.

Then she sets a letter on Fenna's table. “Your mother. She asked me to hand it over myself.” She does not stay for tea. She does stay for a very long look at the letter.

The pickle jar comes home from the long voyage with a pressed leaf in it, a brass button and a folded paper in a small, round hand: “Have you still got the bucket? —L.” Fenna looks at the bucket. It is in her hand. It is always in her hand. She holds it a little tighter. Later she hangs the old hook by the new door, and the bucket under it. She does not look at how they fit.

Fenna wants not to be seen holding the bucket, and nobody is looking. She puts it behind the rain barrel. It is a very large bucket and a small barrel. The bunny sits on it. Lark stands beside it with great politeness. “Probably fine,” he tells the barrel. Then Fenna fetches it, because her hand is empty and that will not do.

The new window goes into the school wall, and the children press their noses to it at once. “You can see everything,” says one. “You can see the pig,” says another.

They do not seem to mind that the pig is the only thing to see. Later, the teacher stays behind and cleans the pane with her sleeve, though it is already clean.

Children's voices, down the lane. Someone is singing the wrong words to a right tune, very loudly.

The cake leans a little, in the middle, where the berry cake meets the apple pie. Nobody mentions it. The couple cut it together, and the knife goes in with a small, soft sound.

Everyone cheers as if they had been waiting all day. Fenna stands at the back with a plate and a crumb on her nose. It is her cake they are cheering. Nobody tells her about the crumb.

On the village square the builders eat pizza at long tables under paper lanterns. Fenna sits between Bo and Greenstem. Someone passes her the biggest slice. Tonight, nobody asks whose family she is.

Fenna's paved road reaches the Hurst Gate. The village is standing round a puddle on the Lord's Mile, the road that never had one, as round a visitor. Children name it. Tally measures it. Juno wants it filled. Nobody dares fetch gravel from the Hurst's pile, because the pile is gone. The village laughs, and then goes quiet together.

The board is so full now that notices have started to climb on each other. One of them, near the top, simply says “WHOEVER IS DOING ALL THIS, PLEASE DON'T STOP.” Near the bottom, the lost glove is still pinned there. Fenna straightens the notice. She leaves the glove.

The third scarecrow has a flower. Just one. Where it came from, no one will say. It is not a scarecrow flower. It is a real one, a little yellow, put there on purpose, by someone who thought the scarecrow looked lonely.

The goat wants the pond reeds. The tortoise is sitting on them. The goat tries three ways, and abandons each with great dignity: lean, butt, and lie down with a whistling face. By evening the goat is asleep, and the tortoise has moved four inches.

The goat wakes, finds the reeds free, and is too embarrassed to eat them. She eats the daisy off the tortoise's back instead. It grows back by morning.

A page with a corner bitten off. Berit has written: “Goats lie. Count them anyway. She can count; she will not count what she has eaten. You hid her in the cellar. She ate the door. I was busy; I only noticed the draught.”

In the margin of the steel page: “Steel is iron that has been through something.” A little drawing of a hammer. It looks pleased.

Frost on the glass in the greenhouse, ferns drawn in white. Fenna could swear they are better than the ones on the plants.

On Longnight Greenstem hands her the ladle and says, “I'll do it.” The village waits for nothing. Fenna would rather do anything. She sits. She says one tiny true thing: “My feet hurt.” A cup is passed to her. She drinks it. It costs her more than the soup did.

Pieter wants to show her the new lantern in use. Fenna wants to be home before dark. He asks her up onto the town wall, to the Watch Walk, and to look up the valley. For the first time she sees Moonedge from outside: a window, lit.

Pieter holds the lantern up and looks out over the marsh. “There's a light out there,” he says. “Some nights. Very small. It doesn't move like a lantern.”

He rubs his eyes. “I'm paid to notice, you see. I'm not paid to say.” He says it anyway, and then walks off whistling, so she will not ask. She does not ask.

It was spring when Fenna came here with a bucket and a plan and no idea. She looks around. The plan has changed.

The bucket is still by the door. She gives its rim a pat, the way you would pat a horse that has done a good day's work, and goes out to see what the day wants.

A pony stands at Fenna's gate and poses, one hoof forward. Then she lets Fenna load a sack, a crate and a big order for the village. She walks down the road as if she owns it.

On the square, people stop. They look at the pony, then the order, then Fenna. Somebody says her name. Her own name, not her family's. The fourth line comes back: “Strong pony brings the world its bread.”

Appleby, with chalk and board, has to mark every sack of the big order in front of the Square. The village watches, unsure whether to cheer. The pony poses through the whole chalking. Someone laughs. Appleby's chalk breaks. She chalks the last sack very small.

The pony puts her whole head over the fence and breathes on Fenna, once, as if checking she is still warm.

Quill asks, because the Book says he must, to borrow the pony and the cart for six days on the Lord's road. The old lease says “carriage on request”. The pony shows him her other profile. He withdraws the request, writes “declined (pony)” and underlines it twice. For a moment he looks as if something has gone right.

The goat is on the coop roof. Later, she is on top of the woodpile, as if on a stage.

Quill rides back to the manor with his ledger full. At the gate he stops. “The farm gives back more than it takes,” he says, as if it were a question. “The Lord will want to know.” Then he goes, and Fenna cannot tell what he meant.

“Was the Lord a villain?” asks Minena. The old woman thinks about it for a long time. “He was afraid,” she says. “That's worse, sometimes.”

Nobody says anything. Then the smallest one whispers, “Afraid of what?” “Of a number,” she says. “Now go to sleep.” They do not. Not yet.

## Chapter 5: Healing

Down in Hazelwood somebody is coughing. Then somebody else. The children first, as always, and nobody knows what to do.

Wren the healer comes up the road with a full basket. “I cannot do this alone, Fenna,” she says. “Berit's old still-room is behind your kitchen. Would you look?”

Quill is counting Fenna's shelves. He stops. “While looking for the lease's twin I found a report, made the day after the fire by the Reeve, Rudge. I have decided not to read it aloud.” “Why?” “It names a girl, eight, seen running from the barn. And a witness, twelve. I count things. I do not like what this counts.” He closes the ledger. Fenna makes another pot of something.

The still-room door sticks, as it always has. Fenna pushes it with her shoulder, and the smell of old herbs rolls out and says hello.

Fenna wants to make the still-room hers. It has been swept, in small circles, never to the end. The footprints are wide, flat and patient. The kettle on the hearth is cold, lid off, set where a cup would go.

Under the last shelf lies a page, torn in two. The top half is in a tidy hand: “Tea first. Always tea first.” The bottom half is missing. Somebody was here before her, and cared.

Wren wants every house marked on a paper pinned to the board. The cough moves along it like a rumour. In Wren's own hand one name is circled: Lotte. Half the school is closed. Fenna wants to help, and she has only a still-room.

The notices on the board have changed. Fewer ladders, more spoons. “HONEY FOR A COUGH, ASK AT NUMBER 4.” “IF YOUR CHILD HAS A FEVER, SEND FOR WREN.” Someone has also written, smaller: “and please stop eating things you find.” It seems like good advice, and Fenna takes it.

A letter comes by Mei, signed this time: “I heard there is a cough. My mother's recipe: honey, thyme, and the water from the second boiling. It works. I am not asking anything. —Liv.” Fenna looks at the writing a long time. It is the hand from the hay margin. She folds the letter very small and puts it in her apron pocket. She will do this herself.

Wren is waiting at the edge of the forest with her arms folded. “One rule,” she says. “Bring me your basket. Every time. Before you use anything in it.”

“Plants have twins, and some twins are poison. I do not care how clever you are. I care that you wake up tomorrow.” She almost smiles. “It costs a little favour. A biscuit will do.”

Fenna lays two leaves side by side on the table. Same shape, same size, nearly the same green. One makes a good tea. The other would make a very bad day. She tastes neither. She wraps them in a cloth and takes them to someone who knows.

Beside the foxglove, in thick capitals, pressed hard: “LOOK, DON'T TOUCH. I MEAN IT. — B.” Underneath, in another hand, smaller and rounder: “She does.”

Fenna stops to look at a plant, as Wren taught her. She does not pick it. It will keep until Wren has seen it. It looks almost disappointed.

Berit's page about the pony: “Will carry anything. That is not the same as being able to. She went lame in the spring. You brought her an apple a day and told anyone who stood still. Ponies do not fake lameness. Remember that.”

In the forest, a ribbon on the oak turns by itself and settles. One end is fresher than the rest.

The moonstone is warm on its shaded side.

On a stump in the meadow lies a crust, eaten.

On the forest floor are Fenna's own boot prints, and small ones beside them that go off on their own.

Greenstem wants her to stop whistling in the forest. She is not whistling. She is humming. He stops dead, because he knows the tune and does not know why. He hums it back, wrong. Fenna says nothing. She is not afraid of the tune. Only of what it wants.

Fenna wants everything taken to the still-room in one trip. The pony wants to be magnificent about it. She poses, but her tail is clamped. Fenna sees it. She takes a bundle off her. The tail loosens a notch. Fenna takes another. The pony looks at her over a lifted shoulder.

Fenna hangs the first bunch of herbs to dry. In a week the still-room smells like a meadow that has gone indoors. Dried leaves crumble in her fingers, soft as old paper. A thing that was green and loud is now brown and quiet and exactly as useful as before. Perhaps more.

The salve is thick, yellow-green and smells of the inside of a hedge. Fenna dabs a tiny bit on a scratch she has had for days. It stings for one breath, and then it just feels cool. Greenstem, who has been watching from the door, writes something down. Probably it says: “Works.”

Far off between the trees, something white and spiral shines in the green. It does not move. Fenna holds her breath. Then she goes back to her basket. Some things are for later.

Fenna wants to finish sorting the thyme. She is humming. Lark is talking to the third oak. “Mr. Mumble? Mumbo? The Honourable Mumbles?” A very small silence. “He's very good at it,” says Lark. “Who?” “Nobody.”

Later he asks, “What's it called?” Fenna does not know what he means. “It has lots of names,” says Lark. “The Hedgehog Waltz. Sir Turnip's Lament.” He looks at the sky. “Yours. Badly hummed, mind. And it only plays when somebody stands still. Terrible for people with jobs.” Fenna stops humming for the rest of the day.

Greenstem, the boy from the market, stands in the doorway of the still-room and sniffs. “Can I learn this?” Wren grumbles something about boys and mud. Then she says yes.

The kettle clears its throat and begins to sing. Nothing could be more normal.

Fenna wants one more pot of tea. The bunny wants her to look up: a third kind of thump, flat and cross, the one for bad manners. Fenna tells a rabbit that she is not tired, and pours. The bunny turns her back and sits that way for the rest of the day.

The ceiling. A blanket. A cup of something bitter. Wren sits by Fenna's bed, and she is not smiling. “What were you thinking?” she says. Fenna's purse is gone. Her legs shake.

“You are well,” says Wren. “That is all that matters. Next time, the basket comes to me first.” She puts a hand on Fenna's head, very briefly, and goes to stir her pot.

In the margin of the page about asking: “Ask. It is not a failure to ask. The best healer I know asks first.”

At the end of the season Pieter comes up through a yard of coughing children. Wren, in the dim lean-to, needs light to see a throat. “Hold that.” He holds the lantern where she points, for a long time.

Then, low, to Fenna: “The Book says a third. I'll write what the till can bear, a quarter and no more. The lease has a line at the bottom: never so much that the table is bare. Nobody reads the bottom of the page. Don't tell the Hurst.” Fenna pushes her coins at him. He pushes them back. In the end she accepts. It is the first gift she has taken. “If the grey line comes up short somebody will ask,” says Pieter. “It will be me.”

The healer's chest is full. In its lid, rolled up, Fenna finds the other half of the torn page: “The badger left after the fever, when nobody let the children rest.” She reads it twice. She was one of those children.

She counts the children of that winter on her fingers. Herself. The two small ones. The aunt who laughed: not everyone got up. She stops at the fourth finger, at someone older, who was not allowed to stop either.

Fenna makes one more tea, and one more, day and night. Then the pot tilts and the floor comes up. Wren finds her on the stone. “You never stop, do you?” she says, and carries her to the bench.

Later, half awake, Fenna says one thing. “The bucket's empty.” “What bucket?” says Wren. “Hers,” says Greenstem. “By the door.” Wren fills it from the rain barrel and sets it by the bench. She asks nothing.

The village wants to feed her. Fenna, in bed, wants to send every bowl back filled. Wren forbids it for three days. Fourteen bowls pile up in the yard. The goat eats a doily. Fenna cannot return them. She has to be fed.

That same morning she wakes to the smell of thyme. Wren has found the folded letter in her apron pocket, read it, and made the syrup exactly as written. The children's coughs ease. In Wren's book, in the margin, it says: “(from L. of Weir)”. “Who is L.?” asks Wren. “My cousin,” says Fenna. It is the first time she has said it aloud.

Fenna wants to get up. Wren forbids it. On the doorstep lie a few white flowers, tied once with a thread. Wren looks at them a long time. “They are not in my book. Do not eat them. Put them in water.”

The tea set came with two cups. Of course it did: it is a set. Fenna pours one, and then, without deciding to, she puts the other on the table too. Nobody comes. Nobody needs to. It feels better with two.

At the door of the still-room sits a badger, with his back very straight. He looks at the chest. He looks at the tea set. Then he looks at Fenna, and at her hands.

He sets out two cups. “Tea first,” he seems to say. Fenna sits down. For the first time in a long time, she does nothing at all. The badger pours.

A child with a cough is carried in. Everyone rushes. The badger does not. Spectacles. Sniff. Kettle. Cups. Wait. Pour. Sit. Hm. Sip.

The panic runs out of energy before the tea does. By the end the child has stopped coughing, and everyone is sitting down, Fenna too, without noticing.

The badger, with the tea set on his knees, lies down on the spare bed, the only bed in the house with its corners still square. “Tea first,” he seems to say. Then Fenna is in it. She sleeps until the tea is cold.

The next morning there are more white flowers on the step. As Fenna puts them in the cup, two soft rings. The flowers stay open in the cup all night.

Pinned to Fenna's door, held on by a thorn, is a card. On it, in careful claw-scratch: a basket, a bunch of leaves, and a cup. Underneath, a very small moon. Fenna looks into the dark yard. Two eyes look back, level, patient. She pins the card back up. It seems rude not to.

Greenstem wants every plantain leaf off the plant, because he has been told it is good for a cough. Fenna hears a voice in her mouth that is Wren's: “Leave two.” He looks at her as if the herb had spoken. Fenna walks on, a little taller, and a little frightened of how it sounded.

The lamp-maker pours Fenna's oil into a lamp and lights it. The light is round and yellow and steady.

“It's for the marsh road,” he says. “There is a very small light out there some nights, and it is no help at all. People shouldn't have to feel their way.” He says it lightly. He has clearly thought about it a great deal.

Ferdinand wants her to float a light on the river, for someone. Fenna is afraid of flame. But paper boats on water do not spread. She sets one afloat and watches it all the way to the bend. Ferdinand cannot see it, but he says it is lovely.

Beside the forest path a small stream goes by with petals on it. White ones. They are not from any tree Fenna knows. She follows them with her eyes until they turn behind a bend, and then she does not follow them any further. There will be time.

On her way home, the swing in the orchard is moving, though there is no wind.

The pony takes the long way home from the square, so as to be seen. One child follows her. Later, a drum. She counts them with her ears and makes the route longer.

Rain on the roof, steady, in no hurry. The kind that means stay in and make something warm.

A boy runs up the road with a letter. The seal is red wax, pressed with a heavy ring. “From the Lord,” he pants. Fenna does not open it yet. The badger looks at it, and then at her, and says nothing.

“Did you get better?” asks Minena. “Badgers make very slow tea,” says the old woman. “By the time it was ready, I was.” Somebody laughs. Somebody yawns. Somebody asks if badgers have teapots.

## Chapter 6: Master Farm

A rider brings a letter with a red seal. Fenna's farm prospers, it says, so the share goes back to a half, “as before.” She reads it twice. Inside her, an old pattern sits up and stretches.

Fenna wants to fold the new letter smaller than the last. The badger wants the whole drawer. He takes the folded letters out one by one and presses each flat under a cup. Tea first. Fenna reads them in order. The asks get smaller and smaller. She folds the last one small again.

Fenna wants to read the Lord's letter a third time. The goat has it. Quill arrives to explain it and finds a seal on the step and a goat chewing something red. “I regret that I cannot read what the Lord has written,” he says. A pause. “I did not write it.” He looks at the goat for a long moment and does not take the seal.

At the Pot the village argues about the half. “We know that number,” says Maud. Some say the farm should hide its books. Juno wants the tables set for the Fair. Appleby, who stopped speaking to Maud, speaks to her. The village chooses the tables over keeping quiet. Appleby gives her whole flour store for the Pie Table. Maud puts a spoon in the Pot. Nobody knows what the Lord will do.

A new trader unpacks his cart on the square. He has a hat, a tune and a very familiar face. “Barter?” he says. “Yes!” He winks. Fenna decides to stop wondering who he looks like.

Fenna wants a fair price for her cheese. The Broker wraps it and whistles her tune, exactly. At the place where the tune rests he stops, and studies a cheese with great attention. Fenna waits. He goes on studying.

A tall shape stands on the ridge above the marsh, facing the reeds. It hums one long, low note, and does not come down. The next time, the goat tries to talk her down, and eats her ribbon.

Where the marsh was, white flowers open at dusk. Hundreds of them, glowing softly. Somewhere in the dark, very quietly, bells. Nobody comes out to meet her. Fenna stands there a long time and does not say a word.

Dusk. White flowers open, one by one, as though somebody were turning the lights up on a small, private party.

The second orchard goes in, row on row, a green stair up the hill. Down in the first orchard, the old swing is still moving, just a little, as if somebody has only just got off.

On the plank lies an apple core, eaten down to the pips. Fenna does not need to know who. The knot holds.

Rows of tiny envelopes, each with a name in a different hand. Carrot. Bean. “Maud's good pumpkin.” “Do not lose.” They rustle faintly when Fenna walks past. A shelf full of next summer, put in order. She finds herself walking quietly, the way you would in front of sleeping people.

In the margin of the seed page: “Always save the best one for next year. And the second best, for a neighbour.”

The big oven takes six loaves at once and rises to the occasion. The whole lane smells of it. A queue forms that nobody organised. Hilde is in it, pretending to be in the way. Old Jan is behind her, very much not pretending.

The wind pump spins. The pole sings a small, silly song, and the water comes up out of the ground as though it had been waiting for its turn. Fenna stands there with her hands on her hips, listening. From the coop, the hens give an opinion about the noise. It is not kind.

Fenna lifts the cloth out of the vat, and it is green, an ugly, wet, mud-sort-of green. Then the air touches it, and it turns blue. Slowly. Like a thought arriving. She holds it, dripping, and watches it become the colour of a sky that has been waiting its turn.

Next to the worms: “They do the real work. Call them by name.” A list follows. They are all called Gerald.

“A handful of living soil has more creatures in it than there are people. Remember that when you feel small.”

Spread across the linen on Fenna's bed, a patchwork cover, every square a different cloth. She can pick out a few: Maud's checked apron, a scrap of blue, something that smells faintly of lavender. All of Hazelwood, sewn together by people she has not quite caught doing it.

Elder Agnes sprinkles the thyme over Fenna's tapenade as though dealing cards. “Your grandmother put this in everything,” she says. “She had a saying. ‘If in doubt, thyme.’” Fenna waits for the rest. There is no rest. That was the whole saying. Somehow it is enough to keep.

At the long table by the orphanage window, the jam goes round on thick slices of Fenna's bread. Nobody speaks, because everyone's mouth is full. One small child puts the empty jar to her ear, as though a little of the summer might still be in there. Matron Elsie pretends not to see.

The well stands finished, a ring of grey stone, and the first bucket comes up on its rope. The water is so clear Fenna can see her own face in it. Behind her face, a row of other faces, leaning in. All of Hazelwood is trying to look into the same hole at once.

The astronomer at the telescope waves Fenna over. “Look,” Vega says, and steps back. At first, black. Then a smear of silver, and then it is not a smear. It is hundreds. Fenna says nothing. After a long time Vega says, quietly, “Yes. That is what I say, too.”

Kit wraps the rainbow scarf three times round and declares it “every colour of every thought I almost had.”

Then Kit writes it down. Then Kit reads it aloud, to Fenna, to the cow, and to a passing man who was only asking the way. He says it is very good, and that the way is left.

The painter mixes the saffron into a little pot, and the pot goes gold. She paints one stroke across the paper, and a field is suddenly there, in the afternoon, with the light on it. “Thank you,” she says. She looks as if she means it for the whole season.

The travelling theatre's play is about a cheese. It is a great cheese. It gets lost, and found, and mistaken for the moon. The lead actor holds Fenna's cheese wheel very still for the whole second act. Fenna has never seen anyone so serious about a cheese.

The board in the square is crowded now with notices in a dozen hands. Near the bottom is the old one, the lost glove. It is still there. Nobody ever claimed it. Fenna finds this very funny.

The long evening light lies across the fields like a cat that owns them.

A last letter from the river barge: “We're coming to the Fair. All of us. Even your cousin.” Fenna puts it in her pocket and keeps touching it all day.

Somewhere in the square a drum is being tested, and then, firmly, forbidden.

The flags go up between the houses, a hundred small triangles, red and yellow and blue and one that is nobody's colour. The wind finds them and they all lean the same way at once. It makes the whole square look as if it were about to say something good.

The tables at the Fair fill up. Everybody brought something: Pieter a lantern cake, Greenstem a basket of mint, the miller, with a face like thunder, a sack of flour. The whole village works with Fenna.

Quill wants to count the North Field before the Fair, and the Book still has no word for a field that rests. He tries “idle”, “waiting”, “asleep”. Lark, from the fence, offers worse. Berit's book lies open on the fence post at “Rest the field.” Quill reads it, writes “fallow”, and in the margin, small, “per B.” He stands between the rows with the sharp pencil and counts twice, because he does not believe what the pencil says.

Quill wants to count plates at the pie table. He cannot count what he has not been told, so he asks each person's name before he writes down the plate, and the queue gets long. The goat eats the pencil. He has a second. He has more than anyone knew. Pieter comes through with his book open and stops at the Hall door: no collector crosses a laid table. It is a custom older than the lease. He sets his lantern down in the doorway, still lit. Children guard it. He is given a plate.

The whole village waits on the Landing Steps for the barge with the family. It is late. A squall comes over. The tables go cold. Fenna wants to bake something, to have something to do, and Maud forbids it.

Then the barge comes in, and the family climbs the steps. Fenna's sister counts ninety-one. Her brother counts ninety-two. Maud puts a bowl in Fenna's father's hands with both of hers, and he cannot refuse it. He hands Fenna the tent, folded. “You patched it.” That is the whole of his speech. He stands by the pony with his hand on her neck, as he stood by Cobble's, and she, who poses for everyone, stands still. Fenna's mother takes her face in both hands. Liv is last off the barge, a snuffing pole on her back and a stone in her fist. Maud puts a bowl in her hands too.

Mayor Juno wants the Cloth Table cleared. Elder Agnes will not let the folded Moonedge cloth on it be moved. The table has been laid at every Fair since the fire, and the stool at its head is for no one. Fenna sets her cloth over the old one. When the tables are full Ansel rings three, and small bells answer from nowhere. Two people hear them. Neither says so.

Fenna wants to set the tables in silence. The whole village hums while it sets its own. The tune is hers. She stops humming. Everyone looks. She starts again. She knows where they got it.

That night Fenna wants to sleep. From the forest edge, once, comes a long, low note she has not heard and has heard before. Her shoulders are up before she knows why. It does not come again.

The Wagon Players want to give the Ballad of Moonedge. Lil Thunder shakes a tin sheet for lightning, and the children shout BOOM. Linus shouts it first. In the Ballad, the child hero runs out of the burning barn shouting, “The lights! The lights!” to wake the house, and the village sings along. Fenna cannot leave. Leaving would look like guilt. She watches to the end. At the back, a grey coat has gone very still.

The Fair is bright and loud. Then Fenna's cousin stands up in front of everyone. Liv wants the lie to stop. “It wasn't lightning,” she says. The tin sheet stops. “You were in the barn that night. I saw you run out, crying about lights.” The whole square goes quiet. Her hands shake. One of them holds a stone.

Fenna wants to run. The bucket is in her hand, the only thing she has ever run with. Instead she puts it down in the dust. It is the first time she has set it down in front of anyone. She sits on the nearest bench with nothing in her hands. Her hands are cold. Liv stands over her. “Where were you?” “In the barn.”

“Doing what?” “Playing. I took the bucket in with me. Nobody asks a child with a bucket where she's going.”

“Who with?” “I'm not going to say. Not here.” Linus, under his breath: “I told you.” Lotte kicks him.

“And the lantern?” “It was on the hook, outside. Then it was in the hay. I think I carried it. Maybe it was the lights. I don't know.”

“It went up as if it had been waiting. The bucket was empty. I hit it anyway. Then I ran. I shouted about the lights. Nobody believed me, so I stopped.”

Liv sits down. “I saw the lights too,” she says. “I never told anyone.” Then: “You had the bucket. I thought you were running away with it.” “It was empty.”

Somebody passes Fenna a cup. Maud's is the first. Fenna's father gets up, walks the length of the long table and sits down on her other side, his hands on his knees. At the back, Lark stands very still and makes no joke. Fenna's mother says, plainly: “You were a child.” Fenna does not answer.

Appleby wants to wipe her chalk board before anyone has seen the Lord's new paper. Mayor Juno wants it read out in the Hall first. The children want to know what to chant instead. Appleby wipes the first board on the village's word, and every other board follows. Quill, arriving, says he wrote the paper last night and they are two hours early. The skipping rhyme is dropped. Nobody thanks Fenna. A cup is passed. They had risked it.

Quill wants to write his count, and he cannot do it in pencil in front of the people it is about. He takes out a bottle of ink, which he carries for the Lord's letters, and cannot get the cork out. Pieter does it with his teeth. Quill writes the third column in ink, in front of everyone, on the Cloth Table, and signs it. Beside his old flourish on the lease he sets a second, steadier “Q.” “I have never written a number in front of the people it was about,” he says. With his numbers in hand, the share is set at a tenth, as in the old days.

An alpaca stands at the gate, very tall and very polite. She lifts her head and hums one long, strange note, the note that woke the whole farm that night. Nobody was hurt because of her.

She spits, politely, on principle. The last line of the rhyme comes back, whole: “Soft alpaca guards all things.” Fenna says all of it, from the very first line. Her voice does not wobble.

The row of five animals has a space at the end, and has not known how to stop leaving it. The alpaca walks to the end of the row. The goat, on the nearest roof, looks down.

The long table, the lanterns, the whole village. Fenna sits down at last, and nobody needs her to stand. She sets a place for Lark without a word. He has to sit very still for a moment.

The goat eats the blue ribbon.

Liv wants to see the book. Fenna does not know how to give it. Liv finds the hay margin and reads: “Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother. Yes, Mother.” She laughs, and does not stop quite when she should. Somebody passes the bread. “I used to think the lights were her,” she says to the bread. “They weren't,” says Fenna. “No.” Liv tears the bread. “They were somebody's, though.”

Somebody starts to sing. The village is singing the rhyme to Berit's kitchen song. Fenna knows the tune. She wants to be useful, and has nothing to carry. Then it does. “Here!” Then her mother and father, her sister and brother, her cousin. Jan sits down. Six animals stand in a row in the dusk, and nobody remarks on it.

Fenna turns to the last page in the book. It is empty. Only, in the margin, in a hand she knows so well by now: “Well. Look at that. You read all of it. Go and put the kettle on, my love.”

Night. In the garden where the marsh was, the white flowers are open. The alpaca walks behind Fenna and stops with her back to the flowers, facing the dark. She will spit at anything that comes up the path. The goat's one bramble rustles. Fenna turns the bucket over and sits on it, and does not look at the flowers. The bells are quiet.

“Mumyo.”

“At the Fair I said I wasn't alone. It was you. You laughed at the bucket. You rode on the handle. One of your bells was cracked, and I never told you.”

“You made lights. I laughed. The lantern came in. I don't know whose hands. I'd rather it was mine.”

“You tried to blow it out. It got bigger. I said go away. I said I never wanted to see you again.” A single tink.

“I told them it was you. That wasn't fair. I'm sorry. I looked the other way so I wouldn't find you.”

“I never … I wasn't …” The sentence will not finish. She does not ask him to come out. A tink. Then two soft rings. She does not know what they mean.

She stands, takes the bucket, and goes. Behind her the flowers close. The alpaca's ear turns, once.

Evening. Fenna lights the fire in the yard, the first thing she ever lit here. The spark takes on the first try. The ash around it glows a little, as if it remembers something.

She sets her bucket beside the fire, as always. Beside the bucket, someone has set a thimble of water. She sits with her back against the fence she built. None of this was here when she came. Now she is sitting in the middle of it.

## The last evening

The grandchildren are asleep, all but Minena. “Tell me the imp was real,” she whispers.

“Tonight,” says the old woman, “I'll start with the fire.” She says his name, Mumyo, to a living person for the first time. Then she tells Minena the truth, all of it, quietly, the way it happened.

Halfway through, she stops. “Are you tired?” asks Minena. “I am not,” says the old woman. She is asleep. Minena counts to ten. The old woman wakes. “Where was I?” “The fire.” “Ah.”

On the sill stands the dented bucket. In it are Minena's stones, a feather, and a few flat grey stones that do not match. The stones are a little warm. On the sill outside the window there is a white flower.

“If you ever meet a little fellow with bells,” says the old woman, “tell him I was never angry.” Minena nods, very seriously. That night she dreams of a melody, and in the morning she hums it.

## Reference (not part of the chapters)

### What the book never says

Five things stay unsaid, on purpose.

- **Whose hands carried the lantern in.** Hers, his lights, or both. The book never decides.
- **What Berit felt.** Her guilt is written in one place only, her page about the alpaca: “She hummed at the hay barn seven nights. I moved her to the far paddock. She hummed there too. I was busy.”
- **What Fenna's father does about it,** beyond a hand on a lantern and a folded tent.
- **What the Count is.** The barges are grey, the salt is grey, the Lord owes someone above him. That is as far as it goes.
- **Whether Mumyo forgives her.** There is a sound, and there is a thimble, and the last request goes through Minena.

### The truth under the story

Fenna grew up at Moonedge, a farm on a hill at the forest edge above the village of Hazelwood, on land leased from the Lord, Wexley of Hurst Manor. The lease claims half of all the land gives. For years the Lord took more each year (a tenth, a fifth, a third, a half), because he owes more to someone above him, the Count downriver. To pay it, the family drained the pond, ploughed the clover, cut the scrub and worked the children. One by one the six Helpers left: the pony, the bunny, the tortoise, the goat, and, after the fever winter in which the children were not let rest and Fenna's aunt Tilda died, the badger.

In the dry late summer when Fenna was eight, every grown-up was at the far field. The well was dry. The alpaca had hummed a warning at the hay barn for seven nights, and Berit, busy, had moved her to the far paddock. Fenna's only playmate was Mumyo, a hand-sized imp with white hair and a grey vest of tiny bells, who cannot speak and makes small lights. They were playing in the hay barn. The lantern hung on its hook outside the door, by Berit's rule. During the play it came in. Nobody knows whose hands. The hay caught. Fenna ran out with her own bucket, which was empty. Her last words to Mumyo were “This is all your fault! Go away! I never want to see you again!”, and she told the grown-ups it was him. The alpaca's alarm woke the house; nobody was hurt. The farm burned.

Liv, Fenna's cousin, then twelve, saw both things from the loft: the lights, which she took for her dead mother, and Fenna running out with the empty bucket. She told the Reeve the second and kept the first. Nobody believed Fenna about the lights, so she stopped saying it. The village said “lightning”. There was no order from the Lord. The fire came from many ordinary things at once: the share, a warning animal moved away, a child left alone, a lonely maker of lights, a lantern that came in.

The Lord ended the lease. Fenna's parents, her younger brother and sister, and Liv went to the river town of Weir; Fenna went with Berit to a cottage on a hill, where Berit raised her from memory and the book. Seven years later Berit died and left Fenna the box.

### Who is who

| | |
|---|---|
| **Fenna** | The farmer. Fifteen at the start. Works so she will not have to talk. Eats standing up. In the game she is “you”; in the frame she is the old woman. |
| **Minena** | Her granddaughter, the smallest listener. Notices everything and says about a third of it. |
| **Berit** | Fenna's grandmother, already dead. Speaks only through the farm book's margins, her box and other people's memories. |
| **Mumyo** | A hand-sized imp with white hair and a grey vest of tiny bells. Never speaks, never shows his face. His bells say what he feels: a quick jingle for a prank, one *tink* for sorry, two soft rings for “I'm here”, silence for shame. |
| **Lark** | A wanderer in a rust-coloured coat with odd buttons. Practically immortal. Says “Probably fine.” Tells true things as jokes and never pushes. |
| **Liv** | The cousin, nineteen, Tilda's daughter. Lamplighter in Weir, with a snuffing pole. Dry, exact and afraid. |
| **Tilda** | Berit's daughter, Fenna's aunt, Liv's mother. Died in the fever winter before the fire. The second, rounder hand in Berit's margins. |
| **Master Quill** | The Lord's steward. Counts everything. Honest, which is the problem. |
| **Pieter** | The night watchman who carries the Lord's book and collects the share, with a lantern lit in daylight. |
| **The Lord (Wexley)** | Never seen. A face on a coin, a crooked seal, a drawer full of letters. |
| **The village** | Granny Maud (Berit's oldest friend), Old Jan, Rue the seller, Appleby the baker and Anna, Greenstem, Wren the healer, Mei of the barge, the miller and Ella, Tom, Lotte and Linus, and others. One rung warmer each chapter. |

The six Helpers, in the order they come home: **bunny**, **tortoise**, **goat**, **pony**, **badger**, **alpaca**.

### The rhyme

Berit sang it to Fenna to the tune of her kitchen song. It comes back to Fenna one line at a time, as each Helper comes home.

> Long ears sow the clover green,
> Slow shell keeps the water clean.
> Brave goat clears the bramble bed,
> Strong pony brings the world its bread.
> Wise badger heals what aches and stings,
> Soft alpaca guards all things.
