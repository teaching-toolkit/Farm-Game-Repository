# Verdict

1. As a design this is the strongest slice so far: one account of the night, five holders with five reasons for silence, one object (the bucket) that carries the spine, the joke and the bend. The mystery is fair and the emotional logic is true.
2. The slice fails in three places, all fixable without redesign: the **chapter 6 aftermath** is a parade of closures (about ten pieces in a row, every silence mended in one sitting); **Liv's payload (Tilda, "my mum") is held back so long it lands unplanted in one page**; and **chapter 4 carries three reveals**.
3. The telling is right in shape (two rooms, the name alone on a page, the unsaid sentence going to Minena) but too long and partly told twice; it should be **enacted as an exchange with Liv, not delivered as two monologues**, and it needs one plain adult sentence that gives the farmer back the absolution she gives Mumyo.
4. Three things need the manager, not the author: the **story clock** (stones that grow per summer make game time countable and age Liv to about 30), the **lantern ruling** in DECISIONS (it says "outside"; this slice says "inside by order"), and the **garden's critical path**.
5. Verdict: **accept with revisions.** Revise (F1 to F8 below), then it is ready for 08a/08b/08c. It is not a polish-only state yet: a six-year-old can hold this book; the middle of chapter 4 and the end of chapter 6 are where she or he would wander off.

# What holds (and must not be lost)

- **Five pieces, five silences** (father, mother, Berit, Liv, Fenna), with the Lord, Pieter, Maud and the Helpers around them. Nobody is a villain; every adult has a reason that a child can say back. This is the book's moral shape and it is carried by objects, not speeches.
- **The bucket as one object used twice** (live-wire R2): a pass ("nobody asks a child with a bucket"), then empty, then the dent that fits the hook, then "Have you still got the bucket?" / "Look for the bucket" (two meanings of one fact), then set down at the Fair, then a seat in the garden, then a basket of Liv's stones. Everything in the thread can be told by pointing at it.
- **Liv's misreading of the running** ("I thought you were running away with it" / "It was empty"): the same fact with two meanings is the cleanest piece of mystery craft in the run. The eight-year-old can hold it; the six-year-old can hear it.
- **Lights to lightning** (Maud's kind word, grown by the Players): a lie that is half true and kind. It also supplies a real *cause* for Liv standing up, which the shipped `c6_crisis` lacked.
- **The father's four gestures** (Cobble's neck, the tent "You patched it", the pencil "Mother said no. I said yes.", his hand on the lantern) and **the mother's brackets**. Speech in the margin, then out of it.
- **"Yes, Mother" three times** (funny and sad in one margin) and Berit's last page "put the kettle on" with the absolution never written. Both are right and small.
- **The garden's craft**: the name alone on a page, her refusal to ask him to come out (two spines locking), the unsaid sentence, the reason it must go through Minena. And the quiet reversal in frame 6, "Tonight I'll start with the fire".
- **The absolution flows outward**: "It was my arm. Not yours." The farmer gives Mumyo the sentence that nobody gives her. (This is why F4's one added sentence matters.)
- **Held silences** (night-account §10) and the rule that planted layers say less than the table. The *real* final surprise is not "what happened" (the player knows by chapter 4) but "I said go away" and "I told them it was you". The thread protects exactly that. Keep it.
- **Cruelty stays offstage**: "Not a word" and "he could not look at the child" are never a scene; they arrive as a pencil line. Good.
- Linus's whisper as the one deflating joke; Lark's *no joke*; the guest bed as a three-time Loop whose third turn puts the farmer in it; the speech ladder (A while / Here! / No / The bucket's empty); the doorframe and the woodpile (a snag that pays three times).

# Ranked fixes

Each: symptom, upstream cause, concrete change, who. Ranking is by damage to the child's experience, not by size.

## F1. The story clock: stones count the years, and Liv is thirty at the Fair (manager, then author)

**Arithmetic first.** Fire Y0, Fenna 8, Liv 12. Game start Y+7: Fenna 15 ✓, Liv 19 ✓ (a stated "older by four years" ✓), siblings 12 and 9 ✓, Berit 62 at the fire and 69 at death ✓, Tilda dead at Y-1 when Liv was 11 ✓, helper-leaving ages (3 to 8) ✓. Six stones by the start (Y+1 to Y+6) ✓. **All consistent at the start.**

**The fault is later.** The digest (1.4) and the premise say the engine runs nine to twelve game years. This slice makes the passage of years *visible*: E-1.B01 and E-3.B09 grow the stone row by picture "keyed to `seasons>=`" ("Bucket Nights 2 to 4"), so a child can count the row. D11 says game time is never counted; a counting row of 15 stones breaks it. It also ages everyone: at the Fair Liv is about 29, Fenna about 25, the "two small ones" who run to the animals and take the farmer's hand (B27, B33) are about 21 and 18, and "the youngest" of the report is long gone. Liv "lamplighter's *apprentice*" and "a girl in grey" (Tom, B09) stop being true.

**Cause.** The slice reads game seasons as story years in the stones and as hours in the cast.

**Change.** (a) The manager rules one sentence in DECISIONS: *story time is shorter than the game's seasons; the farmer is still young at the Fair.* (b) Then the stone row **does not grow with game summers**; it is fixed at the start and keyed to **chapter** (ch1 a weathered row; ch3 one fresh stone on top; ch6 the stones in the bucket). (c) No text ever says "a girl", "apprentice" or the siblings' ages after chapter 3; use "your cousin", "your brother and sister". (d) Art note: draw Liv and the siblings ageless, at the Fair no younger than a tall child and no older than a young adult. **Who:** manager (a); author (b to d).

Two facts, both fair. **Who:** author; hand the ch5 line to 08b with `c5_page`.

## F4. The telling: told twice, too long, a monologue; and nobody absolves the farmer (author)

**Symptom.** Half 1 (8 pages, about 120 farmer words) and Half 2 (12 pages, about 150) together run to roughly 600 to 650 words with narration; shipped chapter 1 is 577 words in all. Half 2 repeats the lantern, the arm and the lights (P5, P6) that Half 1 already told (P4 to P6); frame 6 then retells "all of it" a third time offstage. Half 1 P4's sketch says "where the order said", which (a) puts the Lord's order and the father's hand in her mouth in front of her father, against the sketch's own "no mention of the Lord", and (b) is exactly what the tin (E-4.B14) is for. And the farmer's "It was my arm" is the last word on blame; the other twelve links are held only by the player, through optional discoveries.

**Cause.** The author fears a player who missed optional clues, so the telling recaps everything; and the confession is written as speech, not as an exchange.

**Change.**
1. **Enact it as an exchange.** Liv's short questions pull each fact from the farmer ("Where?" "Doing what?" "And the bucket?") and the farmer's answers are one line each, with stops where she cannot go on. This is the digest's own E-6.12 ("and then?"), it serves Liv's want (to hear it from her), it keeps the texture dial dialogue-forward, and it turns monologue into the bend as a shared act. Alternate page types: action, one line, reaction.
2. **Each half says only what only it can say.** Half 1: where, what she was doing, the arm, the empty bucket, the running, the lights, "nobody believed me". Half 2 (to him): the name, his lights as a gift, "I said go away", "I told them it was you", "I looked the other way", the unfinished sentence. Cut Half 2's repeat of the lantern and the arm to the single line "It was my arm. Not yours."
3. **Lengths.** Half 1 six or seven pages; Half 2 **eight**, not twelve (merge P3+P4, P5+P6, P8+P9, P10+P11; cut P12). Fewer than 54 words each, mostly 20 to 35.
4. **Strike "where the order said"** from P4. The order reaches the player through the tin and the father's pencil; she says "the lantern was on the hook" and her father never hears himself named.
5. **One plain adult sentence, no brackets, for the farmer**: the mother's single unbracketed line at the Fair (currently "optional" in `liv-family-berit.md` §4) becomes mandatory and fixed: *"You were a child."* (or the equivalent; no age counted). It is not announced growth (an adult speaks, the farmer does not answer) and it closes the circle: she gave Mumyo "not yours"; her mother gives it back. Berit's absolution stays unwritten; her daughter-in-law's carries it.
6. **Child test, as the author asks**, but add one: hear "my arm hit it" and "go away" with the wording "I turned too quickly and it fell" as the alternative for the six-year-old. If the six-year-old is not carried by the sentence that has the object doing the verb, use it in Half 1 only and keep "my arm" for the garden.

**Is it enacted or narrated?** The *act* is enacted well (bucket down, bench, then bucket turned over as a seat). The *words* are narrated. F4.1 repairs that. **Is it warm, not cruel?** Yes, and the child is not punished in the game (no consequence beyond what the village already gave). **Too heavy for a six-year-old?** Three items carry weight: the lights as a dead mother (keep "my mum" and "somebody's though"; never say "died"), "go away" (universal, and answered by a *tink*), and the Reeve's report naming a child (a paper, ruled through by Quill's ruler). With F2's early planting and F4's exchange, the load is carried. The fire itself is already in the baseline.

**Who:** author; the mother's line goes to the manager as a 💡 for the parent.

# Answers to the brief, by point

**(1) Mystery craft.**
- *One reveal per chapter, one open question:* ch2 (not lightning, an order) and ch6 pass; ch3 is two small reveals (the small one is real; the cousin is a person) but both feed the hook "your cousin may come"; ch4 fails (F5); ch5's open question is thin ("a letter with the Lord's seal, what bucket?" is mostly 07's seal; the thread's own hook is weak). Suggest the ch5 hook be Liv's unanswered folded letter in the apron, which Wren has read.
- *Three layers real and fair:* yes for Q5, Q12, Q13, Q17, Q14 (dent). Weakest: Q16 (the second hand needs three pieces and a name the player never gets until F2) and Q14 (hook-and-dent is picture-only; the telling says it plainly, which is the right redundancy). The "telling says everything once more" rule is a deliberate safety net against missable clues and is acceptable provided F4.2 stops it from becoming a recap of all nineteen strands. The clues make it land, not make it legible: keep that sentence in the design.
- *Nineteen strands:* too many labels for the child to hold; about six are live at once. Treat Q15 (stones), Q16 (hand), Q18 (why lightning) as texture echoes in the table, not as questions.
- *Explained mystery or lecture:* Pieter's glow (B15) explains a character; Half 2 repeats Half 1; the tin and the report are short documents, fine. Otherwise the rule "an object or a person doing something, never narrator summary" is kept.
- *Red herrings, honest and resolved?* RH1 (lightning): by chapter 2 the player already knows it is false, so it is dramatic irony, not a trail; resolved well at B34. RH2 (charcoal burners, resolved in 02): fine. RH3 (the Lord set it): honest, resolved by the tin and frame 4. RH4 (the imp did it alone): the only true false solution; resolved in the garden, good. RH5 (the cousin is the enemy): a misreading the player sees through from chapter 4; honest. RH6 (girl in grey): a mystery, not a herring; fine as a comic rumour. RH7 (careless family): honest but only half-false, since the father *did* hang it; the book resolves it as "carelessness is what fear looks like". All seven are gentle. Note: §16's claim "one misunderstanding only" is not true (the Mark, "Look for the bucket", the cousin are three misreadings); name them as **one family** (the farmer reads kindness as accusation).

**(2) The emotional spine.** Five pieces, five silences holds (see "What holds"). Also repair B11's logic: she walks to the *gatepost* every year, so "may I visit?" must mean "may I come through the gate", otherwise the asking is absurd. In ch6 Liv does too many jobs in few beats (accuser, listener, reader of the margin, joker); trim her joke to one.

Length: 8 + 12 pages is too many for a story box built for 54-word pages and a two-to-four sentence rhythm; see F4.3. Enacted rather than narrated: F4.1. "Nobody punishes the child": respected; the only consequences are the village's silence and the Cup. Heavy for six: see F4. Warm: yes; the one joke inside the Fair (Linus) is the correct deflation and does not mock the speaker.
