## 1. Verdict in five lines

1. As a **world bible** this is excellent: the valley can be walked in the head, the village demonstrably goes on without the player (the open-threads table is the best single idea in the slice), and the food, money and calendar are tied to real game data.
2. As a **story layer** it has spent the village's distrust too early: the locked stance (the village distrusts the whole family and is poor, and is won by kindness) is replaced by a village that has already been holding the farm for seven years (buckets at the post, Jan answering "Here!", the Open Bowl, the Cup, the Ballad as a mercy). There is almost no one for the player to win. The thaw has no steps.
3. **Charge by chapter falls instead of rising**: 31 of 51 seeds are giggle-led, only 5 are tension-led, and the village strand has **no tension seed at all in chapter 4 or the whole closing stretch of chapter 6**, exactly where the Lord's pressure is highest. About 25 seeds are one joke (a villager offers kindness, the spine blocks it, she yields, warm).
4. Of the three inventions, **Small Ones and the Cup is the keeper** (it pays the shipped "Somebody passes you a cup"; trim what surrounds it), **Bucket Night is good but too early, too countable and too warm**, and **the Moonedge Tick is the risky one**: its "shrinks as the farm pays" logic breaks against the shipped `c6_lord` (a half) and `c6_quill_answer` (a tenth), gives the player a perverse reason to pay, and its name is a bug to a six-year-old.
5. The cast is **too big and too alike to hold**: about 20 people matter, about 30 are texture, about 35 are dead weight a child cannot hold (and there are real collisions: Rue/Tansy against slice 01, Rue the child, Lottie/Lotte, seven Hazels, two Hushes). Fixable inside the slice; nothing needs the parent except one rule choice on the Tick.

## 2. What holds (and must not be lost)

- **The six-sentence valley** (atlas §1) and the rule of "no compass points, only toward the forest / down the road / across the water". A six-year-old can say it back.
- **The geography mostly adds up.** I checked the times: gate to Square 30+2, Square to Landing 12, gate to Landing 46, gate to Wren 1 h 50 via the Crossroads Stone, gate to manor 51. They sum. The two-waters solution to C15 (the Hazel stream and the Alder river) is clean, cheap, and deletes nothing from canon. The wall that is knee-high and used as a bench; the 91 steps down and "92 going up"; the Stork crane with one leg longer: real places.
- **The open-threads table (almanac §6)** and its rule: "no favour is a deadline, the village never fails because the player is busy; if ignored it mends itself badly and charmingly, if helped it mends well and specifically." This is the lived-in test passed in one table. It is also a ready list for ticket 05.
- **The almanac's engine fit** (§1): pieces show once, barks come back; never lock a chapter to a season word; the Fair "called by full tables" so the `done:d_fair_*` triggers work in any season. Practical and honest.
- **The Rule of Three Dresses**: rumour about the farmer is always more flattering than the truth, which is why her reputation outruns her sense of herself and her spine cannot catch up. A joke engine that is also characterisation. Keep.
- **Small Ones and the Cup**, as a form: "Say the small one and pass the cup." It is repeatable by a child, it pays a shipped line, and it gives the bend a shape without a speech. (Trim around it, see F3.)
- **"Here!" and Landing Day**: tiny, physical, speakable, and it makes "Moonedge" a thing a person can claim aloud. Old Jan answering alone is the best single image of a village that kept a place.
- **Hook outside, hay inside** (custom 12): a custom that doubles as evidence for `c2_hook`. This is how background becomes clue without exposition.
- **Pieter's lantern as courtesy** ("so people can see him coming and have time to put the kettle on") and the stool standoff (E-3.L02, both stand). Warm, comic, and it humanises the collector without softening the share.
- **The charcoal burners** (E-1.L06): Cole, four words, a sack of charcoal, gone before the soup. A red herring resolved by kindness; a neighbour who watched seven years and wanted nothing.
- **Thorne's grafts and Quill's pencil** (E-4.L03); **Ella's secret gearing** and the grandfather's sails (E-3.L07); **the Empty Table** (E-6.L01); **the Moonedge bucket the other way** (E-6.L09); **"Was it like that?" "Mostly."** (E-6.L05). Each is a want meeting an obstacle with a pay-off in a shipped piece.
- **The egg rule for prices, "Eight loaves fill the square; the ninth sits politely", and §1 of foodways** (why a burnt-farm girl's food is what the valley is missing). It makes "dishes are the research" a village fact.
- **The cautions** (almanac §9, gossip §9): do not make the village the villain; do not fix the year; fire played gently; Mumyo's bells are the only small bells that ring by themselves.
- **The Hall bell code**, "five fast, one slow": a six-year-old will say it back.

## 3. Ranked fixes

Each: symptom, upstream cause, concrete change, who.

### F1. The village has no distrust to win (stance drift; the thaw has no steps)
- **Symptom.** DECISIONS C5 and the 10-12 Oct line say the village **distrusts the whole family and is poor**, and the farmer wins it with kindness and a turned-around farm. By the slice's own list the village has, before the first spring is out: left six buckets at the Moonedge post every year, had Jan answer "Here!" for Moonedge for years, taken the family's name aloud at the Pot "and the Pot does not go quiet" (E-1.L07), made the Open Bowl a law, and (secretly) built a lie to shelter the girl. The only actual cold shoulders are a miller who nods once and children who chant a rhyme they do not understand. The distrust is a chalk mark. Slice 01 expects "wary, not hostile; a child names the fear" ("who blames us?"); the premise's chapter 1 row says "strangers; the village sniffs the smoke". Slice 02 gives a village that is already on her side.
- **Cause.** The author resolved C5 by splitting it: grievance = the Tick, affection = the buckets ("refined, not changed", dossier §3). Then every custom was written to *receive* the farmer. The thaw (the book's village strand) was never laid out as a staircase.
- **Change.**
  1. Add a **stance ladder** (one word per chapter for the *whole* village, plus who breaks it first): ch1 *polite and watching* (they nod, nobody comes); ch2 *curious* (Greenstem, Hilde); ch3 *grudging and fair* (the miller's nod, Pieter's stool); ch4 *split* (some side with Quill, some with the farm); ch5 *needs her*; ch6 *home*. Every seed then declares which rung it sits on.
  2. **Shrink the arrival gifts.** At arrival the buckets at the post are **Maud's alone** (and Hoop's tin tags are hers). The whole-village Bucket Night becomes something the village *grows into* (the Chain forms in ch3 or ch4, the reversal in ch6), or the village-wide chain stays only as a story Maud tells. Jan answering "Here!" can stay; one old man is not a village.
  3. **Give the distrust three faces with wants that cost the player something** (a real seed each): (a) ch1/2: Weighmaster Tally or Mistress Appleby will not take the first loaf without chalking it ("Moonedge pays double"); the farmer wants a sale, he wants his sums to balance; (b) ch3/4: the farm's success hurts a neighbour (the eighth-loaf rule is already in the engine: the bakery, the cobblers, Bram Cress's cheese all lose a customer); someone has a legitimate reason to resent the thaw; (c) ch4/6: the village argues in the Pot about whether to stand with the farm or pay the Lord quietly. The warm resolution then *costs* somebody something.
  4. Let one child say the chalk rhyme **to her face** once (a mother hushes it). That is the 6-to-8 level of bite, and it makes the later "dropped" rhyme (E-6.L04) matter.
- **Who.** Author, in the dossier (§1, §3, §4 and the almanac §5 table). Ticket 05 should be told the ladder.

### F2. The Moonedge Tick: broken logic, a perverse incentive, and a bad word
- **Symptom.** The Tick shrinks "every time the farm pays" (E-3.L03; almanac: four marks, then three, two, one, a small tick), and "ends with Quill's numbers" (E-6.L04, triggered two steps after `c6_quill_answer`). But the shipped `c6_lord` raises the claim to **a half** and `c6_quill_answer` then **lowers it to a tenth**. By the slice's own rule a lower payment should make the Tick *grow back*. Worse: if each payment relieves the neighbours, then the ch4 **"first no"** (`c4_no`) hurts the village, and the ending "a tenth, as in the old days" is the farm's win at the village's expense. That may be a drama worth having, but nothing in the slice or in ticket 07 resolves it; E-6.L04 simply assumes it ends. It also makes the Lord *taxing his own people for a family's loss*, which sits uneasily with "afraid, not wicked" (a Lord afraid of a Count is plausible; a Lord billing the baker for a lost farm is wicked).
- **Cause.** The author tried to use the Tick to give the engine's escalation (C10: 10/20/33/50 %) a face on the village side. The coupling to the engine's numbers is what breaks it.
- **Change.** **Decouple.** Keep the Tick as a *fixed grievance*: a chalk mark "for Moonedge" on loaves, sacks and crossings since the fire, unchanged by what the farm pays, ended by one beat in chapter 6 when the Lord's accounts change (ticket 07 decides how; Quill's true numbers are the obvious cause). Drop "shrinks as the farm pays" (E-3.L03 becomes Pieter sneaking a smaller mark onto the widows' sacks: Tally already does this in people.md; keep that). Give the rule in one child sentence: "Since the fire, every loaf in Hazelwood gets a chalk mark for the farm that burnt. Nobody likes it." **Rename it** ("the Moonedge Mark" or "the Moonedge chalk"): *tick* is an insect to a six-year-old listener ("a Moonedge bug?") and a different word in the German text. And do not let it become a second guilt-lever on the spine in ch2 (E-2.L02 "she wants to repair it") without a cost she can feel.
- **Who.** Author (dossier §2.2, §4 E-2.L02/E-3.L03/E-6.L04, atlas §6, foodways §1, gossip §6); ticket 07 confirms the end; manager decides the rule (it is a 💡 touching the C5 refinement).

### F3. The bend is pre-protected, and the ending is over-forgiven
- **Symptom.** Four layers already hold the girl before the Fair: the village half-knows (V6: Otto, Jan, Hilde, Maud, Agnes), the Lightning Ballad is a **kindness-lie built on purpose by three women**, Longnight has a ready-made institution for telling and receiving (Small Ones), and E-6.L06 confesses the lie right after the bend. Then six warm resolution beats follow the bend (E-6.L04, L06, L07, L08, L09, L10). The shipped `c6_crisis` says the whole square "goes quiet" and `c6_bend` says "the shouting that nobody believed"; the premise promises the player "should feel told something nobody else knows"; C7 has the cousin keep "the lights" because nobody believed Fenna. In the slice nearly the whole village already acted on the secret. The bend stops costing the spine anything (character.md §3: "the bend must cost the spine something visible"; diagnose: "did you restore when you should not have?").
- **Cause.** Each of the four additions is individually kind; together they remove the risk the whole book is built to run.
- **Change.** (1) Keep the Cup and both rehearsals (E-2.L05 avoidance, E-4.L09 "My feet hurt"). They are cheap and the second one is brave in a small way. (2) **Shrink V6 to Otto and Maud**, the author's own offered fallback (gossip §4), and make the Ballad's mercy **Maud's alone and half-deliberate**, not a three-woman conspiracy; cut Agnes's "nine years of silence". (3) After the bend keep only: E-6.L06 (short, Maud, one line), E-6.L10 (the family answers "Here!", which is also Jan's moment), and E-6.L04 only if F2 is accepted. Move E-6.L07 (Pieter's lantern), E-6.L08 (soup on the steps) and E-6.L09 (the bucket reversed) to free play or barks, or to *before* the bend where they warm the run-up. (4) Make sure E-6.L05 (the Ballad) comes **before** the bend (it does by trigger; keep it so), and add one cheap plant for the ballad earlier (children skipping "the little one ran out", ch2 or ch3), otherwise the Ballad appears out of nowhere in ch6.
- **Who.** Author; ticket 04 owns V6 and must confirm.

### F4. The cast is too big and too alike for a child
- **Symptom.** Over 100 named people and creatures for a story told in 2-to-4-sentence boxes with no speaker names and no walking visitors (node-map §3). The seeds themselves introduce about 45 distinct names; chapters 2 and 3 each add 9 to 10 new people. Real collisions: see section 5 (cast triage) and section 6.
- **Cause.** The ticket asked for 50+; the author gave 100+ and attached an event or open thread to many.
- **Change.** Adopt the triage in section 5 as a rule: **at most 20 people may carry a box; about 30 may appear as a one-line mention; the rest stay in the bible and in trader data, never in text.** Rename the collisions, cut or merge the dead weight, and add a "new names per chapter" cap (three new named villagers per chapter beyond the favour-givers the game already forces on the player).
- **Who.** Author (people.md, dossier §4 names in seeds); the manager approves the 20.

(4) Re-run the charge audit on a step-ordered list with slice 01's and slice 03's seeds (08a/08b), not on this slice alone.

### F6. D11 leaks: years counted in the village's mouth
- **Symptom.** "Seven years" appears in the dossier one-pager, E-1.L03 (via foodways), Jan's off-screen (*"for seven years"*), the Cole seed ("watching the farm for seven years"), customs 6 and 9, foodways §1. "**Nine** Fairs", "**nine** years" appear in E-6.L01, E-6.L10, people A24, almanac 4.4 and customs 11. The row of buckets (six at the post, one more each summer, "seven years of buckets" in almanac 4.1) is a visible game-year counter: the player sees it grow through 9 to 12 game years. Charter §3 and D11: game time is never counted in player texts.
- **Cause.** Backstory numbers were treated as safe because they are "before the game". They are false by chapter 2 (the game has passed two years), and the buckets count the game.
- **Change.** Ban numerals of years in every village line: "since the fire", "every year", "as long as anyone remembers". Make the bucket row **uncounted** (a row of buckets, oldest rusty, newest bright; never "six"); the player may count but the text must not invite it. Also reconcile **7 vs 9**: the fire was seven years before the start, so "nine Fairs" and "nine years" are wrong even as backstory (and a Fair "called by full tables" is not yearly anyway).
- **Who.** Author (global search and replace in the slice; text-change notes for any shipped line).

### F7. Geography and time: small faults that a careful child (or a Godot developer) will meet
- **Weekly barge, eight-day river.** Landing Day is "the week's sixth day", every week. Weir is 3 days down and 5 days up (atlas §3, §4.5), so one barge cannot be at the Landing every seven days. The letter round trip "about three weeks" suggests the author half-knew. **Change:** Landing Day is fortnightly (every second sixth-day); `sq_long_voyage` stays the long run. Or make it four days up. Pick one; update the almanac, E-2.L01, E-4.L07, and the 3-versus-4-days rumour line (atlas §3 says 4 days to Weir for a rumour, but the barge takes 3).
- **The fire-night chain from the Hop.** The Hop is 12 minutes from the gate (about 900 m); a bucket chain needs a person for every metre, which is more people than the village has (about 140). The Hazel runs **through** the farm (digest 1.3) and the pond is "at the foot of the rise" (atlas §8). **Change:** the chain runs from the stream at the foot of the rise to the barn, a hundred paces, which 140 people can fill. The Hop stays where Otto saw the sooty child at dawn.
- **Where the stream starts.** The Hazel "starts to be a stream" at the pond (atlas 4.2) *and* at the Hazel Head, 12 minutes into the forest (§7). Pick: the Head is the thread in the forest; the pond is the farm's reservoir fed by it.
- **"The river" twice.** Moonedge's family called the stream "the river", which is why the marsh is the River Meadow; the Alder is "the river" to everyone else (atlas 4.1). One confusion is allowed; keep it for the book's pages, not for text a six-year-old hears. The author's own line is right: tell it once.
- **How a family goes to Weir by the ferry.** Otto's pole ferry crosses 4 minutes to the Far Bank; Weir is downstream. A night crossing to avoid the Weigh House's toll men is plausible and good, but it has to be said in one line (they crossed to avoid the Landing, then took the Far Bank road).
- **Mei walks the letter up the hill (E-2.L01) and the farmer shouts "Here!" at the Landing (E-4.L07).** Both are fine; give the second a reason Mei's hand delivery has stopped (the road is cobbled? the farmer asked to be called at the Landing like everyone else?). Otherwise ch4 reads as a step backward.
- **Sound.** The Quarry whistle and the nine rings are heard "from Moonedge" (almanac §2, B4, B11) at 30 to 50 minutes' distance. Plausible across a valley at night; make it the whistle and the bell *as faint*, never "the sound you hear".
- **Hazel overload** (see section 6).
- **Who.** Author (atlas, almanac, dossier); a three-line edit each.

### F8. Folk belief is light, but ritual density is not
- **Symptom.** Stated beliefs are fine (a saucer for the Neighbour, a word to the well, a ribbon on the oak, hum don't whistle). But the calendar has eight festivals plus Reckoning, a Reading of the Names, a bell code, a confession-and-cup ritual with a fixed formula, a memorial night with a bell, floating lights with wishes for the absent, and the authors' own phrase in customs 6, "a game and a prayer-that-isn't". A public confession answered by a shared cup is, structurally, a rite of absolution. That drifts toward a liturgical village, which the parent ruled out (no religion).
- **Cause.** Customs were generated to receive the farmer's secret; liturgy is what a village does when it has a rite for secrets.
- **Change.** Cap the village's *rites* at five that a child can hold and say: **the Open Bowl, the Cup, "Here!", Bucket Night, Hook outside**. Everything else is a thing people do for fun (an egg roll, a skipping rhyme, a pancake toss), with no formula. Delete the word "prayer" from the files; change "Small One" to a name that does not echo Mumyo's epithet (see F9) and make the telling optional ("nobody is made to"). Keep the Neighbour and the ribbon as they are.
- **Who.** Author.

### F9. The rest of the inventions, small repairs
- **Small Ones** is also near the frame's "little fellow with bells" and DECISIONS' "the little one with bells". A six-year-old listener may hear "tell a small one" as "a small child". Cheapest fix: "a true thing", as in "Say a true thing and pass the cup." Keep "small" as an echo of Berit's "Small is plenty" only if the author means it.
- **E-1.L05** leaves "the green patch wet" after the night bucket. The ch1 turn is *the bunny kept the patch alive*; a village bucket watering the patch competes with it. Make the wet patch explicit as "not the bucket's doing", or drop the wet patch and keep only the empty bucket.
- **Bucket overload.** The book already has the dented bucket (slice 01), the childhood bucket carried to the tortoise's dry pond (slice 03), the box's bucket, and now the village's buckets with tin leaves. The echo may be a gift, but a six-year-old can lose which bucket is which. Give the village buckets one differentiator the player can see (a tin hazel leaf) and say it each time.
- **Jan's "Here!" and Agnes's roll call at the Fair** (E-6.L10, "for nine Fairs she has called Moonedge to a silence") contradict each other: Jan answers at the Landing every week, so the Fair would not be silent if he is present. Merge: there is one roll call (Landing Day); at the Fair the family answer the *same* call Jan has answered alone, and Jan sits down.
- **The seller** is "Rue" in slice 01 and "Tansy Pockets" in 02; a Ninepin child is also "Rue" (people G). **Manager decides** (my pick: Rue is her given name in the hour, "Pockets" is what the village calls her; the child is renamed).
- **Maud's unopened drawer of Berit's letters** (people A2) contradicts slice 01 (Berit told Maud in autumn that she was sending Fenna back, E-1.11) and does not answer 01's X13 (how Maud met Berit after the family left). Resolve: Maud wrote, Berit answered once, Maud opened the last one.
- **Hall bell:** slice 01 asks for "a big slow bell at dusk" in autumn; this slice has one slow ring = the hour, nine slow = night. Pick: dusk gets a bark, night gets nine.
- **Over-ambition in the Lord's reach table** (atlas §6): oven-penny, stall-penny, water-toll, mill toll, forest edge, Far Bank licences and the Tick. That is a taxation system for a book about a child. Only the chalk mark and the share need to be in a text. The rest is bible.
- **Who.** Author, except the seller's name (manager) and V6's wording (04).

## 4. The lived-in test, the child test, and the charge audit

### 4.1 Lived-in
- **Can a reader walk around Hazelwood in their head? Yes.** The square, the Hall steps, the board, Deep, the Mother Hazel; Mill Lane to the mill, the smithy, the Washing Stones; Lantern Lane; Hazel Gate with Maud's cottage; the Watch Walk; the 91 steps to the Landing; the ferry stage; the Far Bank. Times add up (section 3, F7).
- **Does the village go on without the player? Yes**, better than most books manage: the open-threads table, the barks, the week, the weather words.
- **Stage-set areas.** The outer farms and the Far Bank (Highfold, Cressbrook, Three Oaks, Alderbank, Quarry Row, the Diggings, Linden Dale) are a list of names with a person each; they do not touch a single seed except Marta and Bram's cows. They are fine as bible; they must not get a line of text.
- **The player never walks the village** (node-map §3: no walking visitors, the village is a board and a market). So the geography serves authors, not players. Its value is that every voice sounds from a real place. The risk is a text that tries to *describe* places the child cannot see. Add a rule: **a child hears at most ten places named**: Moonedge, Hazelwood (the Square), the Hall, the mill, the Hop, the Landing, the Hurst, the forest edge (Ribbon Oak, Stone Line), Weir, the next valley.
- **Is it too much?** Yes in names (F4) and in rites (F8); no in places.

### 4.2 Child test
- **An eight-year-old would not hold:** the number of people; the Tick as arithmetic ("one fewer mark"); the Count's seat (Greywater) and the quarter-end logic (never meant for text, but it leaks through E-3.L06 and B9); the tolls table; "Reckoning". They would hold: the Open Bowl, the Cup, "Here!", the bucket row, the chalk rhyme, Pieter's lantern, Jan, Maud, Ella and the sails, Rosa's lost tune, the Empty Table.

## 8. Dials

- **Language (six-year-old).** The customs and sayings are short and say-back-able. Cut the stretch words from the village's mouth (*tick*, *Reckoning*, *Weighmaster*, *oven-penny*); rename as in F2 and F7.
- **Story (eight-year-old).** The pieces that carry real weight are few and good: the Cup, the Ballad's "Mostly", Jan's "Here!", the Empty Table. The village needs one thing at stake (F1, F5).
- **Folk belief.** Light and pagan: yes. Ritual density: no (F8). No gods, no priest: kept.
- **Warm, never cruel.** Kept throughout. The danger is the opposite: so warm that it is sugary. The caution at almanac §9 ("don't let the village be too jolly") is right and the seeds do not follow it.
- **Wants and bite.** Mostly scenery with warm: ~31 of 51 seeds put the want on a villager and the obstacle on the farmer's own spine, which is the right engine and the wrong quantity.
