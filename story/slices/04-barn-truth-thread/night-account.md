**What changed from revision 1:** the Lord's lantern order and the father's "yes" are **dropped** (DECISIONS: the lantern hung **outside, on its hook by the barn door**; the fire began inside, in the hay). *How the flame reached the hay stays open, in the farmer's own words*: the lantern was unhooked and carried in during the play, and she does not know whose hands (hers, or his lights). The village's half-knowledge is Otto and Maud only, and the Ballad is Maud's alone (slice 02, revision 2). Berit's refrain is "I was busy." (slice 03). No year is counted anywhere a player can read it.

The ✅ facts kept: the fire began in the hay barn while the girl played with Mumyo; the Lord took more each year and owes upward; the chain dried the land; the alpaca's alarm woke everyone and nobody was hurt; the family split; Fenna never sees Mumyo again.

## 2. The Lord's side 🔒 💡

There is no order about lanterns. The Lord is what he always was in this book: a frightened man who owes a Count and takes a bigger share each year. His share is why every pair of hands was at the far field hauling water that dusk, why the children were left alone, why there was no water in the well. His **Reeve** (Rudge) takes statements the day after the fire and writes a report that names a child. That is the only place the Lord's side touches the night. (The Reeve's name appears once, in the report, ch5.)

## 3. Seven nights, and the eighth 🔒 💡

The **alpaca** hummed at the barn at dusk on seven nights, facing the door where the lantern hung and the child went in. Berit moved her to the far paddock. She hummed there too, facing the barn. On the eighth night the note woke the farm (`c6_alpaca`). *The warning was about this barn at this hour; Berit moved the warning, not the child, not the lantern. "I was busy."* (03's alpaca page: "She hummed at the hay barn seven nights. I moved her to the far paddock. She hummed there too. I was busy.")

The **bunny** stood at the field edge and saw the lights in the barn gaps. A flame near dry hay makes her thump the *roll*. Nobody listened.

## 4. The night, step by step 🔒 💡

Late summer, the fourth dry month. This is also the day of **Bucket Night** (dusk of the day of the fire).

1. **Afternoon.** The last water goes up from the well at noon. The grown-ups take the cart and the yoke to the far field to carry water from the stream. Berit stays in the kitchen. **Liv** (12) is set to mind the two small ones in the loft room: *"and keep an eye on the quiet one."* (Fenna is the eldest of her own brother and sister, five and two.)
2. **Dusk.** As every dusk, the father lights the lantern and hangs it on the hook **outside** the barn door, and goes back to the field. Fenna (8) has done the last chore of the day: put the bucket away. It is **empty**; there is nothing to put in it. She takes it into the barn anyway. *A child with a bucket is never asked where she is going.* It is the first time she uses the bucket as a pass.
3. **In the barn.** Mumyo is in the rafters, where he has been most dusks that summer: hand-sized, white hair, a grey vest with bells, one of them cracked. She sets the bucket down. For the first time that day she carries nothing. He makes **lights**, small cold ones, hung like beads over the hay. She climbs the stack and leaps for them. She laughs, loud, for the first time in a long while. *This is the play.*
4. **From the loft window**, Liv sees the barn's gaps flicker, as on many evenings that summer. She thinks the lights are her mother.
5. **The lantern comes in.** She remembers it on the hook outside. She remembers it inside, swinging, over the hay. **She does not remember lifting it.** Three readings, none ruled out and none ever told: she took it down to see him properly; his lights lifted it (he moves things; it is his one prank that went wrong); both. *Mumyo knows. He never says.* (The telling keeps this open: "I don't know whose hands.")
6. **The hay takes it**, a hand-wide fire, a bed, a wall: "as if it had been waiting".
7. **She fights it.** She climbs down, takes the **bucket**, strikes at the flames. It is empty. She knows it is empty; she carried the last water herself. She strikes again.
8. **Mumyo tries.** He beats his wings at the flames; the draught makes them bigger.
9. **She turns on him.** *"Look what you did! Go away! I never want to see you again!"* (sketch). She runs for help. He takes her at her word.
10. **The door.** She runs out across the yard with the bucket in her hand, sooty, shouting "**the lights! the lights!**" (the word she has been living in all summer). **As she passes the doorpost the bucket's rim strikes the empty hook, and dents.** She drops it at the threshold and does not stop.
11. **The note.** At that moment the alpaca's long strange note. The household wakes (the cousin was already at the window). *Nobody is hurt because of her.*
12. **Liv** carries the two-year-old down the ladder and drags the five-year-old after her. She sees Fenna with Berit by the kitchen door and the barn door burst into light.
13. **Berit** holds Fenna, who says someone was in there. Berit says: *Hush.*
14. **The village.** Pieter, on the Wall, counts the glow as the farm's lantern a minute too long (05's to use or not). Ansel rings five fast, one slow. A bucket chain from the stream at the foot of the rise to the barn. Too late.
15. **Dawn.** Fenna sits by the Hop, dry-eyed, soot to the knees. **Otto** sees her from the ferry stage; **Maud** holds her and has heard her shout "the lights". Mumyo is back in the shell. The alpaca walks out of the yard with her head high.
16. **The bucket.** Berit finds it at the barn door in the ash, dented, sooty inside. She takes it and tells nobody. (Her box note says only "It was dented when I found it.")

## 5. The morning after, and the years 🔒 💡

- **The Reeve's questions.** Rudge comes with a book. The father says: *A lantern fell. The girl was in the barn.* Liv, asked by a large man with a book, says the true thing she can say: *Fenna ran out of the barn with the bucket.* The book: *the girl, eight, seen running from the barn with a pail; witness, a girl of the household, twelve.* She says nothing about the lights.
- **Fenna says "an imp"**, to Berit and her father, the next day. Her father says: **"Not a word. Not to anyone. They will say you were playing with fire."** He is afraid for her; the Reeve has a book. Berit believes her and says nothing either (a Keeper's granddaughter; she keeps "the old friends" to herself). The child hears *nobody believes me.* What she was told was *do not say it where it can be used.*
- **The village's kindness.** In the days after, sitting up with the broken family, **Maud** says "lightning" to the first person who asks: the kindest word, and a lightning fire cannot be charged to a family. Half-deliberate. The Players and the children grow it into the Ballad and a skipping rhyme. No plot, no committee.
- **The Lord** ends the lease (the farm cannot keep its barn, and will never pay again) and makes sure no one in his lands takes the family on.
- **The split.** In the first winter the family goes by Otto's ferry at night: father, mother, the two small ones and Liv to Weir; Berit takes Fenna to a hill cottage (reasons: `liv-family-berit.md` §3.1).
- **Afterwards.** Fenna says nothing. She keeps busy and carries a bucket. Liv puts a river stone on the gatepost on Bucket Night each year, before the village comes. (How often, how many, and who walks behind her are 05's and the art's to settle; **the game never counts them**.)
- **The funeral.** Berit dies; Fenna holds the horse; "a while". (`liv-family-berit.md` §3.2)

**The Ballad's trick:** the one thing the child shouted was "**the lights!**". Maud heard it; the kindest word she had was *lightning*. So the Ballad is the child's own word, reshaped; its child-hero "runs out shouting to wake the house", which is true. This is why the lie lasts and why **Liv hears it as theft**: it takes her lights and gives them to the weather. (E-6.B21, B22.)

The farmer thinks the Mark and the sideways looks are about her. They are about the Lord.
