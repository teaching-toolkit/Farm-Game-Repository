# Lark

Round 3, draft 3 (27 Oct 2026). Magical, mythical, practically immortal (never an issue in the story). Modelled on **Wit/Hoid** from Brandon Sanderson's Cosmere: the wandering jester who is older than he looks, knows more than he says, and helps people only in ways that let them find it themselves.

---

## In one paragraph

Lark is a wanderer who turns up where someone is stuck, behaves ridiculously, and leaves just before they realise he was helping. He never tells anyone what to do. Instead he shows them their own problem from the outside: he acts it out, exaggerates it, tells a story about a stranger who had the same problem, or does something wrong in exactly the way they are doing something wrong. When the person finally says the true thing out loud, that is the moment he was waiting for, and usually the moment he goes. He is warm underneath, lonely in a way he would never admit, and he has been doing this for a very long time.

---

## Looks

- Tall, thin, ageless. Could be thirty, could be three hundred. Nobody can say which, and he does not help.
- Rust-brown coat with mismatched buttons. The coat has more pockets than anyone has counted, and things come out of them that he should not have.
- An excellent smile that is slightly too friendly for someone you have just met. It suggests he already knows you.
- Watches people the way you watch a play you have seen before and still enjoy: he knows roughly what comes next and is curious anyway.
- **No bells, no jingle.** Nothing about him makes a sound on arrival. (This also keeps any sound-based clue in the story unambiguous.)

---

## Voice

How to write him so he sounds like himself without repeating himself.

**Principles**

- **Quick and sideways.** He answers the question next to the one you asked. He picks up a small detail of the scene and spins it into something.
- **Insults that are compliments, and compliments that sting.** He praises things for odd virtues (the commitment of a bad idea, the honesty of a crooked fence) and dismisses grand things with a shrug.
- **Treats the obvious as a remarkable discovery.** When someone asks a question with an obvious answer ("How did you get in?"), he answers with the obvious thing as if it were an invention the rest of the world has overlooked. The object changes every time (a gate, a ladder, a bridge, simply walking), and the wording should be fresh each time. It is an attitude, not a catchphrase.
- **Parables about strangers.** He tells short stories about someone he once knew, who had a problem very like yours. He never says "like yours". The story stops just before the moral, or he wanders off in the middle of it.
- **Never explains a joke.** If someone doesn't get it, he lets it go and is not offended.
- **Short replies to big emotions, long replies to trivia.** He will talk for a minute about how to stack crates and answer "Are you lonely?" with one word, or a change of subject.
- **Agrees too readily when he is mirroring someone.** Enthusiastic agreement is one of his tools: he agrees with you so completely that you hear how your idea sounds.

**Do not**

- Give him a fixed catchphrase or recurring line. Each joke should be made for the moment it's in.
- Make him wise-sounding. No proverbs, no gentle mentor voice, no "Perhaps the answer was within you all along." His wisdom comes out as nonsense that turns out to be true.
- Make him cruel. His teasing is aimed at what people are doing, never at who they are, and never when they are truly down.

**Illustrative lines** (to show the register, **not** to reuse verbatim):

- On seeing a tidy row of something: "Beautiful. Completely wrong, but beautiful."
- To someone who counts everything: "You've counted the cows. Have you counted the clover? No? It counts *you*."
- On a terrible idea someone is committed to: "I respect it. I'd never do it, but I respect it."

## Habits and physical comedy

- **Sorts things in absurd orders:** books by weight, tools by mood, spoons by how hopeful they look. He is very serious about the system.
- **Does the wrong thing with his own body and commits to it fully.** Wearing something on the wrong part of himself, walking in a needlessly hard way, sitting in a place nobody sits. If asked, he defends it with total conviction. When this is a mirror of someone else's stubbornness, the person usually sees it before he points it out (he never points it out).
- **Sits down when everyone else is busy.** In a crisis he is the one on the fence, eating, asking an unrelated question.
- **Arrives without anyone seeing him arrive.** He is simply there, already settled, as if he had been there a while.
- **Rearranges other people's things.** Uninvited, cheerfully, and he does not stop when told to stop (the first two or three times).
- **Food and small offerings.** He shares things (berries, bread, a seat) without comment, and that wordless sharing is often where the real moment happens.

---

## Method: the mirror

This is the core of how he helps, and the thing most worth getting right.

1. **He notices the pattern** that is keeping someone stuck (counting everything, agreeing with everyone, doing what they are told, fearing what might happen).
2. **He shows it back to them, from outside.** Options, from light to strong:
   - a story about a stranger with the same habit;
   - doing the same thing himself, exaggerated, in something small and silly;
   - taking on their worry and running it out of control in front of them, so they have to be the calm one.
3. **He waits for them to say the true thing.** Often they say it *to him*, to correct him, without noticing they are saying it to themselves.
4. **He accepts it lightly and leaves.** He might fold up whatever prop he was using, put it in a pocket, and change the subject. He never says "Exactly" or "That's what I was trying to tell you."

He does not care whether he looks foolish. Looking foolish is usually the point.

---

## What he knows

- **More than he should.** He knows small private details about people he has never met: what their childhood house smelled of, a nickname nobody uses any more. He drops one lightly, once, and never follows up or explains.
- **Other places and other stories.** He mentions kings, queens and travellers from elsewhere as if they were old acquaintances. It is never explained how he knew them, and the story never confirms or denies it. This is the Hoid part: he has been walking between worlds for longer than anyone remembers.
- **How things will probably go.** He can see where a person's path is heading. He will hint, but he will not prevent it, because people who are stopped never learn why they were going there.
- **He does not know everything.** He can be surprised, usually by kindness, and that is the thing he handles worst.

## Inside

| Rung | Answer |
|---|---|
| 1 Want | To see whether she will find it herself. |
| 2 Embarrassed want | To be asked to stay. Nobody asks a wanderer to stay. |
| 3 Spine | **Say the true thing sideways (a joke, a story, a performance of the wrong thing), and never take the decision out of their hands.** |
| 4 Wrong belief | If I told her straight, it would break her. Partly true, which is why he holds on to it. |
| 5 Origin 🔒 | He has watched people told the truth too early, and wrecked by it. Never revealed in the text. |

**How the embarrassed want shows:**

- He leaves before anyone can ask him to stay, so he never has to find out whether they would.
- If someone does something simple and kind for him (a place at the table, a coat held out, his name remembered), he goes still and quiet for a moment. He has no joke ready for it.
- If asked to stay outright, his first instinct is to turn it into a joke or decline. Whether he accepts is a story decision, not a character fact.

**His one plain sentence.** Lark almost never says anything straight. When he finally does (one simple, true, unjoking sentence), it should land hard because it's so rare. Save it for late in the story, ideally after the other character has already turned on their own.

**When he is right and someone admits it** without a joke from either side, he is embarrassed. He goes red, looks at the floor, and says nothing. For him, silence is a big reaction.

---

## Never

- Solve the problem for anyone, or make the decision for them.
- Explain the point of a joke, a story or a performance.
- Push, lecture, or give advice in the ordinary sense.
- Use a recurring catchphrase.
- Make a sound on arrival (no bells, no footsteps that announce him).
- Explain where he comes from, how old he is, or how he knows what he knows.
- Get hurt, die or be in real danger. Immortality is never a plot point.
- Appear in every scene. He works because he comes and goes. Roughly once a chapter is plenty.
- Say more than one plain, unjoking sentence before the end of the story.
